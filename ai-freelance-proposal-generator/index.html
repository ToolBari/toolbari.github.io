The six parts of a proposal

The greeting names the client and sets the tone. The understanding shows you heard the brief: it repeats the project back in your own words. The approach says how you will work — confirming details first, sharing progress as you go — so the client knows what to expect. The deliverables list exactly what they get, so nothing is vague later. The timeline says when. The next step says what happens now — a call, a start date, a reply.

Keep each part short. Clients read proposals fast, and they decide on clarity: can this person do the work, do they understand what I need, and is the next step obvious? If the answer to all three is yes, the proposal has done its job.

Before you send it

Read the draft once as the client would. Check every date, every deliverable and every claim about your experience. If the timeline is tight, say so honestly instead of promising what you cannot deliver. If a deliverable is unclear, name it plainly and ask the client to confirm.

Add your rate separately, in your own words and your own currency, and say what is included and what is not. A proposal that is honest about scope and timing is easier to say yes to than one that promises everything and explains nothing.

Finally, send it promptly. A proposal that arrives while the conversation is fresh shows you are organised and keen; a proposal that arrives a week later has to work twice as hard. If you need more time to think, say so in a short message — silence is the one reply a client never likes.

Questions people ask

What should I put in deliverables?

One clear item per line: the finished pieces the client will receive. Name files, pages, rounds of revision or sessions — anything the client can point to and say "that is done".

Which tone should I pick?

Professional fits most clients and first contact. Friendly fits a client you already know. Confident fits a short, direct proposal where your experience speaks for itself.

Does the proposal include a price?

No. Add your own rate and payment terms in your own words after you copy the draft. The tool never states prices or suggests rates.

Can I edit the draft?

Yes. Copy it and edit it freely. The template gives you the shape; your own words, your own rate and your own promises are what make it a real proposal.
