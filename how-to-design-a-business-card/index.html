How to Design a Business Card

A business card has one job: to help someone remember you and reach you later. A card that is clear, readable and honest about what you do will be kept, while a crowded or confusing card will be thrown away.

A good business card is not a work of art and it is not a brochure. It is a small, practical tool, and most people decide within a few seconds whether a card is worth keeping. The layout, the size of the text, the colours and the feel of the paper all send a message before a single line is read. This guide walks through each decision in turn: what to include, what to leave off, how to arrange the lines, how large the text should be, and what kind of paper to print on.

What to include on a business card

Start with the essentials. Every business card needs your name, the name of your business, and a short line that says what you do. That short line matters more than most people think. A name and a phone number mean very little a week later, when the person holding the card cannot remember where they met you. A line like "Tailor — shirts and suits made to order" turns a card into a memory.

Next, add one way to reach you that you actually check. For most small businesses that is one phone number and one website or social page. One of each is enough. Two phone numbers, three social pages and a website do not make you easier to reach. They make the card harder to read and leave the reader unsure which number to call.

Then stop. A card is small, and every line has to earn its place. Leave off a long list of services, a list of every product you sell, and any detail that belongs on your website instead. Leave off your home address unless customers visit you there, and leave off a job title that means nothing outside your own company. If a line does not help someone remember you or contact you, it does not belong on the card.

Put the important things first

Information hierarchy is a grand name for a simple idea: the most important thing on the card should be the easiest to see. When someone picks up your card, their eyes should land on your name first, then on what you do, and then on how to reach you. Everything else is background.

Make your name the largest text on the card. Your business name or your line about what you do comes next, slightly smaller. Your contact details come last and smallest, because nobody needs to read a phone number at a glance. They only need to find it when they are ready to call.

Group related lines together. Keep your name, your business name and your line about what you do in one cluster, and keep your phone number and website together in another. A gap between the two groups tells the reader, without a single word, that these are two different kinds of information. Empty space is not wasted space. It is what makes the words on the card easy to find.

Make the text easy to read

Text size is the most common mistake on business cards, and the easiest to fix. Make your name 12 to 14 point. Make your contact details 8 to 10 point. Keep nothing below 8 point. Text smaller than that is hard to read in good light and unreadable in poor light, and a card that cannot be read is a card that gets thrown away.

Before you print, test the card at arm's length. Hold a printed sample the way a stranger would hold it and try to read it without squinting. If you cannot read it easily, the text is too small or the contrast is too low. This simple test catches most problems before they reach the printer.

Contrast matters as much as size. Dark text on a light background is the easiest combination to read. Avoid pale text on a pale background, and avoid placing text on top of a busy photograph or pattern. If the background fights with the words, the words lose. Stick to one or two typefaces for the whole card, and avoid script or decorative typefaces for small text.

Adding a QR code

A QR code is a bridge between the card and your online presence. One scan can take someone to your website, your booking page or your contact page, and saves the reader from typing a long address. Only add a code if it leads somewhere useful: a code that opens an empty page is worse than no code at all.

Make the code at least 2 centimetres square, and leave clear space around it on all sides. A code crammed into a corner or printed over a pattern may not scan at all. Place it where it is easy to find, usually on the back of the card or in a clear corner of the front. Always test it before you print: scan it with your own phone and check that it opens the right page.

Print basics: paper and finish

Paper is the part of a business card people feel before they read. A thicker card feels solid and professional, while a thin card bends in a pocket and feels cheap. You do not need the thickest paper available, but you should avoid the thinnest. A card that survives a week in a wallet without creasing is doing its job.

A matte finish is easy to write on, which is useful when someone wants to jot down a note about where they met you. A glossy finish makes colours look brighter, but it can be hard to write on and can catch the light in a way that makes text harder to read. If you are unsure, matte is the safer choice.

Before you commit to a large print run, order a small batch first. Check the spelling, check the phone number, check the website address, and scan the QR code. Bright colours can look very different on paper than on a screen, so check a printed sample. A few minutes of checking saves a box of useless cards.

Mistakes that get cards thrown away

The most common reason a card is thrown away is simple: it is hard to read. Too much text, text that is too small, or text in a low-contrast colour all lead to the same place. If someone has to work to read your card, they will not bother. Keep it short, keep it large enough to read, and keep the contrast high.

The second reason is that the card says nothing memorable. A card with a name and a number but no hint of what the person does is forgettable within a day. Always include that short line about your work. Outdated details are just as bad: a card with an old phone number sends people to a dead end.

Finally, check your details every time you reprint, and throw away old cards rather than handing them out with corrections written on them. And keep the design honest: a card covered in clip art or a design copied from a different kind of business confuses people about what you do. Simple, clear and honest beats clever and confusing every time.

Questions people ask

What should a business card include?

A business card should include your name, your business name, a short line saying what you do, one phone number, and one website or page where people can find you. Leave off anything that does not help someone remember you or contact you.

What size should the text be?

Make your name 12 to 14 point and your contact details 8 to 10 point, and keep nothing below 8 point. Test the card by holding it at arm's length: if you cannot read it easily, the text is too small.

Should I put a QR code on my card?

Yes, if it leads somewhere useful, such as your website, your booking page, or your contact page. Make the code at least 2 centimetres square, leave clear space around it, and scan it yourself before you print.

What makes people keep a business card?

People keep cards they can read at a glance, that clearly say who you are and what you do, and that feel solid in the hand. A QR code or a useful note on the back gives someone one more reason to hold on to it.
