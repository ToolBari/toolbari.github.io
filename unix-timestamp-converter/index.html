What a Unix timestamp is

A Unix timestamp is a count of seconds since 1 January 1970 at midnight in UTC, a moment called the epoch. The number 0 means that exact moment, 60 means one minute later, and 1700000000 means a date in late 2023. Because the count starts from one fixed moment, the same timestamp means the same instant everywhere on Earth.

Computers love timestamps because they are single numbers: easy to store, easy to compare, and free of time-zone confusion. Humans find them unreadable, which is why converters like this one exist.

Seconds or milliseconds?

Older systems count in seconds, which gives a 10-digit number for dates around now. Many newer systems count in milliseconds — thousandths of a second — which gives a 13-digit number. The two look alike at a glance, so check the length before converting.

If you paste a 13-digit number while the unit is set to seconds, the date will land thousands of years in the future. Switch the unit to milliseconds and the same number gives the right date.

Timestamps in everyday tools

You meet timestamps more often than you might think. File systems record when a file was created and changed, databases stamp each record with the moment it was written, and web services pass timestamps back and forth whenever they need to agree on a time.

When a tool shows you a bare number instead of a date, paste it here to see what moment it means — and check whether it is seconds or milliseconds before you trust the answer.

Questions people ask

What is the Unix epoch?

It is 1 January 1970 at midnight UTC — the starting point timestamps count from.

How do I know if my timestamp is seconds or milliseconds?

Count the digits: around 10 digits means seconds, around 13 means milliseconds.

Why do UTC and local time differ?

UTC is the same everywhere, while local time adds your time zone offset, so the same moment shows a different clock time.

Can I convert dates before 1970?

Yes. Dates before 1970 have negative timestamps, and this tool converts them the same way.
