How the conversion works


The converter draws your image onto a small grid, one cell per character, and measures the brightness of each cell. Dark cells become dense characters like @ and %, bright cells become light characters like . and a space, and the shades in between use the rest of the ramp. The result is plain text that looks like the picture when viewed from a distance.


Brightness is measured from the red, green and blue channels, with green counting most because human eyes are most sensitive to it. The height of the output is halved relative to the width, because text characters are roughly twice as tall as they are wide, and without that correction the art would look stretched.


Getting a good result


Simple, high-contrast images work best: a portrait against a plain background, a bold logo, or a clear silhouette. Busy photos with lots of small detail turn into noise, because each character can only show one average brightness. If the result looks muddy, try a larger width for more detail, or crop the image to the subject first.


The output is plain text, so you can paste it into a message, a document or a code comment. It keeps its shape best in a monospace font, where every character has the same width. In a proportional font the columns will not line up and the picture will fall apart.


Questions people ask


Which images work best as ASCII art?
Simple, high-contrast images with a clear subject. Portraits, logos and silhouettes convert well, while busy photos with fine detail usually look muddy.


What does the width setting do?
It sets how many characters wide the output is. A larger width keeps more detail but makes a longer text block, while a smaller width gives a compact result.


Can I use the ASCII art anywhere?
Yes, it is plain text. Paste it into a message, a document or a code comment. A monospace font keeps the columns lined up.


Why is the art shorter than the image?
Text characters are about twice as tall as they are wide, so the height is halved to keep the picture in proportion. Without that, the art would look stretched.
