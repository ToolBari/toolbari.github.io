What makes a hook work

A hook is the first line of a video or post, and its job is simple: make someone stop and keep reading or watching. Good hooks do one of three things: they ask a question the reader wants answered, they promise a clear benefit, or they say something surprising enough to pause on. The best hooks are short, specific, and honest about what follows. A hook that promises more than the post delivers will cost you trust, so keep the line true to the content behind it.

The five hook styles

Question hooks invite the reader in by asking something they have wondered about. Bold claim hooks state a strong opinion that makes people want to see the proof. Story hooks open with a small moment from real life, which feels personal and easy to follow. Surprise hooks share an unexpected fact or a common mistake. List hooks promise a clear, countable payoff, such as five ways or three mistakes. Try each style on the same topic and see which one sounds most like you.

Using hooks in videos and posts

For a video, say the hook in the first few seconds, before any introduction. For a written post, put the hook on the first line by itself so it stands out. One hook per post is enough: pick the strongest line and build the rest of the post around it. If the hook asks a question, answer it in the post. If it makes a claim, back it up. Regenerate to get five fresh lines whenever the first set does not fit the tone you want.

Keeping hooks honest

A hook should earn attention, not trick people into it. Avoid lines that promise a secret, a shortcut, or a result you cannot deliver. If a template line mentions a result, such as doubled progress or a fixed problem, only use it when it is true for you. Edit every hook before publishing: swap in your own words, check the facts, and drop any line that overstates what follows. Honest hooks build an audience that stays, and readers come back when the first line keeps its promise.

Questions people ask

What is a hook?

A hook is the first line of a video or post. Its job is to make someone stop and keep reading or watching, usually by asking a question, promising a benefit, or saying something surprising.

Which style should I pick?

Try each style on the same topic. Questions invite readers in, bold claims challenge them, stories feel personal, surprise lines pause the scroll, and lists promise a clear payoff.

Can I use a hook as a title?

Yes. A short hook often works well as a title or a caption, especially list and question hooks. Trim it to fit and keep it honest about the content.

How do I make a hook my own?

Edit the line in your own words, add a detail only you know, and check that any claim in it is true. A hook that sounds like you will always beat a copied line.
