What this tracker does and who it is for

Building materials do not sit still. Cement, steel, brick and timber prices move with supply, demand, transport costs and the season, and the difference between buying this month and next can be real money on a big job. This tracker keeps a simple record: you log the price you see, with the date and the shop, and the tracker shows the history for each material and how much it has moved since your first entry.

It is built for anyone buying materials for a build — a homeowner doing a renovation, a small contractor pricing jobs, or a site manager keeping an eye on costs. You do not need market knowledge to use it. You only need the prices you already see when you shop, and the habit of writing them down.

What it will not do is tell you the market price. This page has no live prices, no market feed and no connection to any shop. Every number in it is a number you typed. That is a deliberate limit: a page that showed invented prices would be worse than useless. Use this tracker to see your own prices move, and check the shop for today's price before you spend.

How to use the tracker on your phone or computer

Log one price at a time. Pick the material, type the price in your own currency, pick the date, and add the shop name if you want to remember where you saw it. Tap Log this price and the entry joins the history for that material. The bars show each price against the highest price for that material, so a rise or a fall is visible at a glance.

Once a material has two or more prices, the tracker shows the change since your first entry — the amount and the percentage. One price on its own is just a number; two prices make a trend. The more prices you log for the same material, the clearer the trend becomes.

Your prices are saved only in this browser, so they are still here when you come back. Nothing is uploaded or shared. The copy button copies a plain-text summary of all your materials, which you can paste into a message or keep with your budget. Each entry has a delete button, and the clear button removes everything.

Which materials to track

Track the materials that cost the most on your job. For most builds that means cement, steel, brick and timber — the four the tracker lists first — because they are bought in bulk and their prices move. A small change in the price of cement, multiplied by a hundred bags, is real money.

Add other materials only if they matter to your job. If you are fitting a kitchen, track the worktop material. If you are roofing, track the tiles. The Other option covers anything else, but a tracker full of materials you buy once is clutter — track the few that repeat.

Track each material separately, even if you buy them together. Cement and steel move for different reasons, and a single average across materials hides what is really happening. The tracker keeps a separate history for each material precisely so you can see which one is rising and which one is steady.

Reading the price moves

A rise of a few percent over a few months is normal for most materials. Prices drift upward with transport and energy costs, and a small steady rise is not a reason to panic-buy. What deserves attention is a sharp move — a jump of ten percent or more in a few weeks — because that usually means a shortage, a tax change or a supply problem.

A fall is worth reading carefully too. A price can fall because a shop is clearing stock, because a new supplier has arrived, or because the grade has changed. Before celebrating a cheaper price, check that the grade, the unit and the delivery terms are the same as before. A lower price for a lower grade is not a bargain.

Compare prices from the same shop over time before comparing across shops. Two shops can charge different prices for the same material on the same day, and that gap tells you about the shops, not about the market. The tracker's shop field exists so you can see which shop you logged each price from.

Seasonal patterns are worth watching too. Some materials are cheaper when demand is low — timber in winter, for example, when fewer people are building — and dearer in the busy season. A year of logged prices shows the pattern for your area, and the pattern tells you when to buy. But a pattern is not a promise: one bad winter or one supply shock can break it, so treat the seasonal habit as a guide, not a rule.

Buying before a rise — and when not to

If a material you need is rising steadily and you have somewhere dry to store it, buying early can save money. Cement keeps if it stays dry, timber keeps if it stays covered, and bricks keep almost anywhere. Buying a month early at today's price beats buying next month at a higher one — as long as you were going to buy it anyway.

Do not buy early just because a price moved. A rise that has already happened is not a promise of another rise, and materials bought too early can be damaged, stolen or simply in the way. Buy early when the trend is clear, the storage is safe and the job is certain — not on a guess.

Never buy a material you do not yet need in the hope of selling it later. That is trading, not building, and this tracker is not a trading tool. It shows you your own prices over time so you can time your own purchases — nothing more.

Storage is part of the decision. Materials bought early need somewhere dry and safe, and materials left in the rain or in the way cost more than they save. Before buying early, check that you have the space, that the material keeps well, and that nobody will move it twice. A bag of cement ruined by damp is not a bargain at any price.

A worked example: cement over three months

Imagine you log cement at 450 on 1 March from City Builders. On 1 April the same shop quotes 470, and on 1 May it quotes 495. The tracker shows a change of 45 since the first entry — a rise of 10 percent over two months. For a job needing a hundred bags, that is the difference between 45,000 and 49,500.

The same three months might show steel flat: 820, 815, 825 — a move of less than one percent. The two materials tell different stories, and the tracker keeps them apart so you can act on the one that matters. In this example, buying the cement early would have saved money; buying the steel early would not have.

Notice what the example does not claim: it does not say cement will keep rising, and it does not say 495 is the price today. Prices move both ways, and the only price that matters when you buy is the one the shop quotes that day. The tracker shows your own history; the shop shows today's price.

Common mistakes that make price tracking useless

The first mistake is comparing different grades as if they were the same. Cement comes in grades, timber comes in grades, steel comes in grades, and a cheaper price for a lower grade is not a saving. Always log the grade in the shop field or in your own notes, and compare like with like.

The second mistake is one price from one shop. A single price tells you what one shop charged on one day. It tells you nothing about the market and nothing about the trend. Log the same material more than once, and from more than one shop when you can, before drawing any conclusion.

The third mistake is mixing up the unit. Cement by the bag and cement by the tonne are different numbers, and timber by the length and timber by the cubic metre are different numbers too. Always log the price in the same unit for the same material, and say which unit you used — otherwise the history compares things that cannot be compared.

Questions people ask

Does this show live market prices?

No. This page shows only the prices you enter yourself, with the dates you logged them. It does not fetch live market prices or connect to any shop. Always check the current price with the shop before you buy.

Which materials should I track?

Track the materials that cost the most on your job — usually cement, steel, brick and timber, because they are bought in bulk and their prices move. Add other materials only if they matter to your job.

How often should I log a price?

Whenever you see a new price for a material you track — when you visit a shop, get a quote, or are about to buy. Two prices make a trend; the more you log for the same material, the clearer the picture.

Why do material prices change?

Supply and demand, transport and energy costs, the season, and shortages all move prices. A sharp rise usually means a shortage or a supply problem; a small steady rise is normal for most materials.

Can I compare two shops?

Yes. Log the shop name with each price, and the tracker shows which shop each price came from. Compare the same material, grade and unit across shops to see which shop is genuinely cheaper.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your prices are saved only in this browser using local storage. Nothing is uploaded, synced or shared, and clearing your browser data will remove them — so copy the summary if you want a lasting record.
