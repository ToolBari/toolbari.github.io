Your tap water comes with a story written in numbers — pH, hardness, chlorine, lead, nitrates and TDS — and this guide translates every one of them into plain words. You will learn how to read a water-quality report line by line, what to test for and when, and what each result means for drinking, cooking and your appliances.

How to use this guide

This guide is a translator. You bring a water-quality report, a lab result, or a home test kit reading, and the guide explains what each number means in plain words. You do not need a science background. Each measure gets a short, honest explanation: what it is, where it comes from, what counts as high or low, and what you can do about it.

Start with the measures explained below, so the words on your report stop looking like code. Then read the section on reading your report line by line, which walks through a typical report in the order you will meet it. If you do not have a report yet, the section on the three testing methods tells you how to get one, and what each method can and cannot tell you.

Everything on this page is general information. Water rules, limits and testing services differ from country to country, so where a number or a limit matters, check the official guidance for your own area. This page will not invent a limit for your country, and it will not pretend that one number fits every home.

The measures explained

pH tells you how acidic or alkaline your water is, on a scale from 0 to 14, where 7 is neutral. Most tap water sits a little above or below 7. Very low pH water is acidic and can slowly corrode metal pipes and fittings, which is how metals like lead and copper can end up in the water. Very high pH water can taste bitter or soapy. pH on its own does not say whether water is safe to drink — it is one clue among several, and it matters most for what it does to your pipes.

Hardness measures the calcium and magnesium dissolved in your water, usually reported in milligrams per litre. Hard water is not a health danger on its own — in fact the minerals in it are the same ones found in food — but it leaves white scale on kettles, taps and shower heads, makes soap harder to rinse, and shortens the life of water heaters. Soft water has little of these minerals, so it lathers easily and leaves no scale. Hardness is a household problem, not a safety verdict.

Chlorine is added by water suppliers to kill bacteria, and it is the reason tap water sometimes smells faintly of a swimming pool. A small amount left in the water at your tap is normal and is there on purpose — it keeps the water safe on its way through the pipes. If the smell bothers you, letting a jug of water stand uncovered for a while usually softens it. Chlorine is not a sign that something is wrong; it is a sign that the water was treated.

Lead is the measure to take most seriously, because it comes from old pipes, solder and fittings inside buildings rather than from the water source itself. That means your supplier can deliver clean water and your tap can still show lead, especially in older homes and in water that has sat in the pipes overnight. Health authorities set very low limits for lead, and the safest advice is to keep it as low as possible — run the tap for a short while before drinking if the water has been standing, and get a lab test if your home has old plumbing.

Nitrates come mainly from fertiliser, animal waste and sewage, and they matter most for babies, because high nitrate levels can affect how a baby's blood carries oxygen. Adults are far less sensitive to nitrates, but a high result still means something is reaching your water that should not be there, so it is worth finding the source. Nitrates are one of the main reasons private wells are tested regularly.

TDS stands for total dissolved solids — everything dissolved in the water, from minerals like calcium to salts, added up into one number, usually in milligrams per litre. A high TDS number does not by itself mean the water is dangerous: a glass of mineral water has a high TDS because of the minerals in it. TDS is a summary, not a verdict. It is useful for comparing one sample with another, and for spotting a sudden change, but it cannot tell you which substances are in the water.

  • pH — how acidic or alkaline the water is; 7 is neutral
  • Hardness — calcium and magnesium; causes scale, not illness
  • Chlorine — disinfectant added by the supplier; a faint smell is normal
  • Lead — comes from old pipes and fittings; keep it as low as possible
  • Nitrates — from fertiliser and waste; a concern mainly for babies
  • TDS — total dissolved solids; a summary number, not a safety verdict

Reading your water report line by line

A water-quality report looks dense, but every line follows the same pattern: the name of the measure, the result, the unit, and the limit or guideline it is judged against. Read it one line at a time, and never skip the unit — a result of 50 means nothing until you know whether it is milligrams or micrograms per litre.

The most common units are mg/L (milligrams per litre) and µg/L (micrograms per litre). One milligram is a thousand micrograms, so 0.01 mg/L and 10 µg/L are the same number written two ways. You will also see ppm (parts per million), which for water is near enough the same as mg/L. When a report says "not detected", it means the substance was below what the test can measure — it does not prove the water contains none of it at all, only that the amount is too small for that test to see.

Work through the report with a pen. For each line, ask three questions: what is this, is the result above or below the limit, and does it affect health or only taste and household use? Most reports separate health measures from taste and appearance measures, and that split is the most useful thing on the page. A result above a health limit deserves action; a result above a taste guideline deserves a decision, not a panic.

If your report comes from your water supplier, remember it describes the water leaving their plant or at a sampling point in the network — not necessarily the water at your kitchen tap. Your own pipes, your storage tank if you have one, and the time water spends standing in them can all change the result. That is why a supplier report and a home test can disagree, and both can be right.

When to worry and when not to

Worry — and act — when a health measure is above its limit: lead, bacteria, or nitrates above the guideline are all reasons to stop drinking the water until you know more, and to get a second, certified test. A sudden change is also a reason to act: water that turns brown, smells strongly of chemicals, or tastes sharply different from one day to the next should be checked before you drink it.

Do not panic about the measures that are mostly about taste and household life. Hard water will not make anyone ill. A high TDS number on its own is not a danger sign. A faint chlorine smell is normal. Water that looks milky for a few seconds after running the tap is usually full of tiny air bubbles, which clear from the bottom up — that is air, not dirt, and it is common after pipe work in the street.

Some people should be more careful than others. Babies are more sensitive to nitrates, and people with weak immune systems should be more careful about bacteria in water. If anyone in your home is in one of those groups, say so when you talk to your water supplier or your doctor, and follow their advice rather than a general guide like this one. This page is information, not medical advice.

The 3 ways to get your water tested

A certified lab gives the most thorough answer. You take a sample in the bottle the lab provides, following their instructions, and they test it for the measures you choose. It takes days rather than minutes, and it costs more than a home kit, but the result is a real measurement you can act on. To find a lab, search for an accredited water testing laboratory in your country, and check what accreditation means there — an accredited lab follows a standard method and has its work checked.

A home test kit — strips you dip in the water, or drops you add to a sample — is quick and cheap, and it is good for a first look. It can tell you roughly whether your water is hard, whether chlorine is present, and whether the pH is high or low. What it cannot do is give you a precise number you can trust for a health decision. Treat a home kit as a screening tool: useful for deciding whether a lab test is worth paying for, not a replacement for one.

Your water supplier's report is the third route, and it is free. Most suppliers publish their test results, either once a year or more often, and many will send you a copy if you ask. The report covers the water they supply, which is the right starting point for most questions — but it cannot tell you what your own pipes add. If your home is old, or you use a private well, the supplier report is not enough on its own.

  • Certified lab — the most thorough answer; costs more and takes days
  • Home test kit — quick and cheap; a rough first look, not proof
  • Supplier report — free and official; covers the supply, not your pipes

What each result means for drinking, cooking and appliances

For drinking, the measures that matter most are the health ones: bacteria, lead and nitrates. If those are within their limits, the water is fine to drink even if it is hard, even if the TDS number looks high, and even if you can smell a little chlorine. Taste is a separate question from safety, and many people who dislike their tap water are reacting to taste, not to danger.

For cooking, hardness is the measure you will notice most. Hard water can make tea taste flat and leave a film on the surface, and it makes rice and beans take longer to soften. Chlorine can affect the taste of food cooked in tap water, though most of it escapes as steam. None of this makes the food unsafe — it is a matter of flavour, and a simple jug filter is often enough for people who notice it.

For appliances, hardness is again the main story. Scale builds up in kettles, water heaters, washing machines and dishwashers, and over time it makes them use more energy and wear out sooner. If your water is very hard, descaling kettles and heaters regularly is worth the effort, and a softener is a real option — but buy one because of scale, not because hard water is dangerous. Very acidic water can also shorten the life of metal pipes and fittings, which is another reason to know your pH.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is trusting a single strip test. A strip dipped once in a glass of water is a rough hint, affected by temperature, timing and how you read the colour. It is fine for a first look, but it is not a lab result, and it should never be the reason you buy an expensive treatment system or stop drinking your tap water.

The second is confusing hardness with safety. Hard water leaves scale and spots on glasses, so it feels like bad water — but the minerals that make it hard are not a health problem. People spend a lot of money softening water that was perfectly safe to drink, while ignoring the measures that actually matter, like lead from old pipes.

The third is testing once and never again. Water changes with the seasons, with rainfall, and with work on the pipes in your street. A single test is a snapshot, not a permanent verdict. If you use a private well, test at least once a year, and test again after heavy rain or flooding. If you are on a public supply, read the supplier report when it comes out.

The fourth is buying a filter before knowing what is in the water. Different filters remove different things: a carbon filter improves taste and removes chlorine, but it does not remove lead or nitrates; a reverse-osmosis unit removes far more, but it wastes water and removes useful minerals too. Find out what you want to remove first, then buy the filter rated for that job — and change its cartridge on schedule, because a spent filter can make water worse, not better.

The fifth is forgetting the pipes. A perfect supplier report does not guarantee perfect water at your tap if your home has old lead pipes or a tank that is never cleaned. If your water tastes odd or tests badly while your neighbours' water is fine, the problem is probably inside your own home, and that is where the fix belongs.

This page cannot test your water. A static web page has no way to measure what is in your tap, and any page that claims to is not telling the truth. A single strip test is a rough hint, not a lab result — if a result worries you, get a certified lab test before you spend money on treatment or change what your family drinks.

Questions people ask

Is my tap water safe to drink?

In most places with a public supply, tap water is tested regularly and is safe to drink, but the honest answer depends on your supply and your own pipes. Read your supplier's report, and if your home is old or you use a private well, get a certified lab test before you assume anything.

What does TDS mean?

TDS stands for total dissolved solids — everything dissolved in the water, from minerals to salts, added up into one number, usually in milligrams per litre. A high TDS number does not by itself mean the water is dangerous; it is a summary, not a safety verdict, and it is most useful for comparing samples.

How do I test my water at home?

A home test kit with strips or drops gives a quick, rough reading for things like hardness, chlorine and pH. For a real answer — especially for lead, nitrates or bacteria — take a sample to a certified lab and follow their sampling instructions exactly.

What is hard water?

Hard water contains a lot of calcium and magnesium, which leave white scale on kettles and taps and make soap harder to rinse. It is not a health danger on its own; it is a household nuisance, and a softener or regular descaling handles it.

Should I buy a filter?

Only after you know what you want to remove. Different filters remove different things, so find out what is in your water first, then buy the filter rated for that job, and change its cartridge on schedule. A filter bought blind can be a waste of money.

Where do I get my water tested?

A certified lab gives the most thorough result — search for an accredited water testing laboratory in your country. Your water supplier's published report is free and covers the supply, and a home kit is a cheap first look, not proof.
