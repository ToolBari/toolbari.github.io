What this tool does and who it is for

A home insurance inventory organizer is a room-by-room list of what you own, with an approximate value for each item. You add items one at a time, picking the room, naming the item, and entering its approximate value. The page adds up the totals per room and for the whole home, so you can see what your belongings are worth at a glance.

This page is made for homeowners and renters who want to be ready for a claim before one happens. It is also useful for anyone buying home insurance for the first time and unsure how much cover to ask for, because the total of the inventory is a starting point for that conversation with an insurer.

The organizer covers the rooms a home usually has: living room, kitchen, bedroom, bathroom, study or office, garage or shed, and a general room for everything else. Each item gets a room, a name and an approximate value, and the page does the adding up. There are no prices built in and no values suggested: the numbers are yours.

Everything stays in your browser. There is no account, no server, and no one else who can see your inventory. The copy button turns the list into plain text you can paste into a document, save in a note, or print and keep somewhere safe. The list is there when you come back, because it is saved on your device.

How to use it, step by step

Start with one room. Pick the room, type the item name, such as Sofa or Television, and enter its approximate value as a plain number. Press Add item, and the entry appears in your list under that room, with the room total updated. Work through the room, then move to the next one.

List the valuable items first in each room, then the rest. A television, a laptop, a washing machine and a sofa matter more than a kettle or a lamp, because they are worth more and are the items a claim turns on. The small items can follow, but the valuable ones should be in the list even if you run out of time.

Check the totals as you go. The page shows a total per room and a grand total for the whole home. The grand total is the number to compare with your insurance cover: if the total is far above your cover, you may be under-insured, and if it is far below, you may be paying for cover you do not need.

  • Take photos of each room and of valuable items, and keep them with the printed list
  • Update the list when you buy or sell something valuable, not once a year

What the output means

The room total is the sum of the approximate values in that room. It tells you which rooms hold the most value, which is useful both for insurance and for your own sense of what matters. A living room full of electronics will usually outvalue a bathroom, and the room totals show that plainly.

The grand total is the sum of all room totals: the approximate value of everything you have listed. It is the number to carry into a conversation with an insurer, because it answers the question of how much cover you need. It is an estimate built from your own numbers, not a valuation, and an insurer will apply its own rules when it pays a claim.

The grand total also tells you when to review your cover. If the total has grown since you last checked, because of new purchases or rising prices, your cover may no longer match what you own. A yearly comparison of the total against the cover takes a minute and can save a shortfall at claim time.

The item list itself is the most useful output. After a loss, the hardest part of a claim is remembering what you owned. A list made in calm times, with names and values, is worth far more than memory under stress. The copied list is that record, ready to print and keep somewhere safe.

Nothing in the output is a promise of what an insurer will pay. Policies value belongings in different ways: some pay replacement cost, some pay current value, and some cap what they pay per item or per room. The inventory gives you the facts; the policy decides the payout.

A worked example

Take a small home with three rooms listed. In the living room: a television valued at 800, a sofa at 600, and a laptop at 900. In the kitchen: a fridge at 700, a washing machine at 500, and a cooker at 450. In the bedroom: a bed at 400 and a wardrobe at 350. Each item goes into the organizer, one at a time.

The room totals are simple to check by hand. The living room is 800 plus 600 plus 900, which is 2300. The kitchen is 700 plus 500 plus 450, which is 1650. The bedroom is 400 plus 350, which is 750. The grand total is 2300 plus 1650 plus 750, which is 4700.

The owner compares that total with the home insurance cover, which is 5000 for contents. The inventory total sits inside the cover, with room to spare, so the cover looks about right for the items listed. If the owner later buys a new television worth 1200, the total moves to 5900, above the cover, and the cover needs a review.

The owner also photographs each room and the valuable items, and keeps the photos with the printed inventory in a folder away from the home. If the home is ever damaged, the list and the photos together make the claim far easier than memory alone.

Tips for a good result

Start with the most valuable room, not the easiest one. The room with the electronics, the jewellery, or the expensive furniture is the room that matters most to a claim. Listing it first means the most important values are recorded even if you stop halfway.

Use replacement values, and be honest. The value to enter is roughly what it would cost to replace the item with a similar one, not what you paid years ago and not what you wish it were worth. Honest numbers make an honest total, and an honest total makes a useful conversation with an insurer.

Photograph everything you list. A photo of a room shows what was in it in a way a list cannot, and a photo of a serial number or a receipt proves ownership. Keep the photos somewhere safe, away from the home if you can, because photos kept in the home are lost in the same event as the items.

Keep the inventory where a claim can reach it. A list saved only on a computer in the home is lost in the same fire or flood as the items it describes. Print the list, keep a copy away from the home, and keep the photos in a separate place too. An inventory that survives the event it was made for is the whole point of making one.

  • Update the list when you buy, sell or throw away something valuable
  • Keep receipts for big purchases with the inventory, or photograph them
  • Note the make and model of electronics, because it helps a claim and a replacement
  • Review the whole list once a year, and check the total against your cover

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This organizer cannot value your belongings. The numbers you enter are your own estimates, and an insurer will apply its own rules when it pays a claim. A value you enter is not a promise of what you will receive, and the page never claims it is.

It cannot store photos or receipts. It holds a list of names and values, nothing more. Your photos and receipts stay wherever you keep them, and the page only helps you track the list. Keep the photos and the printed list together, somewhere safe.

It cannot keep your inventory safe for you. The list lives in this browser only, which means it is lost if the browser data is cleared or you switch devices. Use the copy button regularly and keep the printed list somewhere safe, ideally away from the home it describes.

Questions people ask

Why do insurers want a home inventory?

An insurer needs to know what you owned to settle a claim, and memory alone is unreliable after a loss. An inventory made in calm times lists each item and its approximate value, so the claim starts from facts instead of guesswork. It also helps you choose the right amount of cover before anything happens.

What should I list first in each room?

List the valuable items first: electronics, appliances, furniture, and anything worth a lot. These are the items a claim turns on. Smaller items can follow, but the valuable ones should be in the list even if you run out of time. Work room by room, and finish one room before starting the next.

Should I list the value or the replacement cost?

List roughly what it would cost to replace the item with a similar one, and note in your own records that the figure is a replacement estimate. Policies value belongings in different ways, so check your own policy for how it pays. The number you enter is your estimate, not a valuation.

Do I need photos of my belongings?

Yes, photographs help a great deal. A photo of a room shows what was in it, and a photo of a serial number or a receipt proves ownership. Keep the photos with the printed inventory, somewhere safe, and ideally away from the home, so they survive the same event as the items.

Is my inventory uploaded anywhere?

No. Your inventory is saved in your own browser, on your own device, and nothing is uploaded anywhere. There is no account and no server. Use the copy button to move the list somewhere safe, because the browser copy is lost if the browser data is cleared.

How often should I update the list?

Update it whenever you buy, sell or throw away something valuable, rather than waiting for a yearly review. A new television, a sold sofa, or a replaced appliance all change the total. Review the whole list once a year as well, and check the grand total against your insurance cover.
