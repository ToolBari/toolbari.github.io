How the cost is worked out

The cost of a 3D print has two parts: the material and the energy. The material cost is the weight of filament the print uses, multiplied by the price of that filament per kilogram. The energy cost is the electricity the printer uses during the print, multiplied by your electricity rate. Add the two together and you have the cost of one print.

The material part is usually the larger of the two. A print that uses 120 grams of filament at a price of 25 per kilogram costs 3.00 in material. The energy part is usually smaller: a six-hour print on a 150-watt printer uses 0.9 kilowatt-hours, which at a rate of 0.30 per kilowatt-hour costs 0.27. The total is 3.27.

The calculator shows the working step by step, so you can check every number. If a result looks wrong, the working shows which input caused it. All money figures are plain numbers in your own currency — the calculator never assumes a currency, and it never states a price for filament or electricity. Those numbers come from you.

What goes into the material cost

The material cost depends on two numbers: how much filament the print uses, and what the filament costs per kilogram. The weight comes from your slicer software, which estimates it before you print. That estimate is usually close, but it can be off by a few grams, so the material cost is an estimate too.

The price per kilogram is what you paid for the spool, divided by its weight. A one-kilogram spool bought for 25 costs 25 per kilogram. A spool sold by weight in grams needs converting first: divide the price by the grams and multiply by 1,000. Keep the receipt or note the price when you buy, so you are not guessing later.

Supports and brims use filament too, and the slicer weight usually includes them. If your slicer reports the model weight and the support weight separately, add them together before entering the total. Waste from failed prints and from purging filament between colours is not included — the calculator prices one successful print.

What goes into the energy cost

The energy cost depends on three numbers: how long the print takes, how much power the printer draws, and what you pay for electricity. Energy is measured in kilowatt-hours: a printer drawing 150 watts for six hours uses 900 watt-hours, which is 0.9 kilowatt-hours. Multiply by your rate per kilowatt-hour to get the energy cost.

Printer power varies. The figure on the power supply is the maximum the printer can draw, not what it draws all the time. A heated bed and a hot nozzle draw the most power while heating up, then less while holding temperature. Using the power-supply figure gives a cautious estimate — the real energy cost is usually a little lower.

If you do not know your printer's power, check the label on the power supply or the manual. If you cannot find it, the calculator still works with a rough figure, but the energy part will be rough too. Your electricity rate is on your bill, usually shown per kilowatt-hour.

Who should use this calculator

This calculator is for anyone who prints at home or in a small workshop and wants to know what a print actually costs. Hobby printers use it to check whether a big print is worth the filament. People who sell prints use it to set prices that cover their costs. Teachers and makerspace organisers use it to work out what to charge members.

It is also useful before buying a printer. If you know roughly how much you will print each month, you can estimate the running cost and compare it with the price of the machine. A cheap printer that prints slowly can cost more to run than a dearer, faster one — the calculator makes that comparison concrete.

The calculator does not include everything. It leaves out failed prints, wear on the nozzle and the bed, and your own time. For pricing work you sell, those extras matter, and you should add a margin for them. For understanding the direct cost of a print, the two parts here are the ones that count.

How to use it step by step

First, slice your model and note the filament weight and the print time from your slicer. Both numbers are shown before you print. Enter the weight in grams and the time in hours — if the slicer shows 5 hours 30 minutes, enter 5.5.

Next, enter the filament price per kilogram and your electricity rate per kilowatt-hour, both as plain numbers in your own currency. Then enter the printer power in watts from the power supply label or the manual.

Press the button to calculate. The result shows the total cost, with the material and energy parts listed separately and the working shown. Copy the result if you want to keep it with the project. Nothing is stored or sent anywhere — the calculator works entirely in your browser.

Worked example

A phone stand uses 120 grams of filament and takes 6 hours to print. The filament costs 25 per kilogram. The electricity rate is 0.30 per kilowatt-hour, and the printer draws 150 watts.

Material: 120 grams is 0.12 kilograms. Multiply by 25 per kilogram to get 3.00. Energy: 6 hours times 150 watts is 900 watt-hours, which is 0.9 kilowatt-hours. Multiply by 0.30 per kilowatt-hour to get 0.27. Total: 3.00 plus 0.27 is 3.27.

Now suppose the same stand is printed with thicker walls and uses 200 grams instead, taking 9 hours. Material becomes 0.2 times 25, which is 5.00. Energy becomes 9 times 150, which is 1,350 watt-hours or 1.35 kilowatt-hours, times 0.30, which is 0.41. The total is 5.41. The heavier print costs about two-thirds more, mostly from the extra filament.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is entering the filament weight in the wrong unit. Slicers show grams; the price is per kilogram. Entering 120 as kilograms instead of grams would make the material cost a thousand times too large. Check the unit before pressing the button.

Another mistake is using the print time in minutes. The calculator expects hours, so 360 minutes must be entered as 6. Entering 360 as hours would make the energy cost sixty times too large. Convert minutes to hours by dividing by 60.

A third mistake is forgetting that the slicer weight is an estimate. The real weight can differ by a few percent, especially on prints with a lot of retraction or support. For pricing, weigh a finished print on kitchen scales once and compare it with the slicer figure — that tells you how far out your slicer runs.

Finally, do not use the maximum power figure and then complain the energy cost looks high. The power-supply rating is a ceiling. If your prints mostly run at steady temperature, the real draw is lower, and the real energy cost is lower too. The calculator gives a cautious estimate on purpose.

Ways to bring the cost down

The simplest saving is less filament. Reducing infill from 20 percent to 10 percent can cut the weight of a solid-looking part by a third, with little loss of strength for decorative objects. Hollowing a model or printing with fewer walls has the same effect. Check the slicer weight before and after each change to see the saving.

Shorter prints use less energy. A larger nozzle prints faster because it lays down more plastic per pass, and a coarser layer height finishes sooner. Both trade a little surface quality for time, which is a fair trade for parts that do not need a fine finish.

Buying filament in larger spools or in multi-packs usually lowers the price per kilogram, but only if you use it before it absorbs moisture. Damp filament prints badly, so store open spools in a dry box. A failed print wastes more money than any saving on filament price.

Printing several small parts in one job also helps: the printer heats up once instead of several times, and the total time is usually less than printing them one by one. The calculator prices one print at a time, so run it for the whole batch to see the saving.

Questions people ask

What does it cost to 3D print one object?

It depends on the filament weight, the filament price, the print time and your electricity rate. This calculator works it out from those five numbers. A small object might cost under 1, while a large, heavy print can cost several times that.

Does the calculator include electricity?

Yes. It multiplies the print time by the printer power to get the energy used, then by your electricity rate. The energy cost is shown separately from the material cost, so you can see which part is bigger.

Why does filament price matter so much?

Because material is usually the larger part of the cost. A print that uses 200 grams of filament costs twice as much in material as one that uses 100 grams. The price per kilogram is the number to watch when comparing spools.

What if I do not know my printer's power?

Check the label on the power supply or the manual — the figure is printed in watts. If you cannot find it, use a rough figure and treat the energy part as an estimate. The material part will still be accurate.

Does it count failed prints?

No. The calculator prices one successful print. Failed prints waste filament and time, so they raise the real cost of a project. For pricing work you sell, add a margin for failures and for wear on the printer.

How accurate is the result?

The result is as accurate as the numbers you enter. The slicer weight and the power figure are estimates, so the total is an estimate too — usually within a few percent for the material part. Weighing a finished print is the best way to check.
