How the calculator works

Enter how many photos, videos, apps and documents you have, plus the average size of one item in each group, and the calculator adds up the total in megabytes and gigabytes. It also suggests a plan size. The suggestion is not the exact total: it adds 20 percent headroom first, then rounds up to the next common plan size, so the plan you pick has room to breathe.

The headroom matters because storage is never as tidy as the arithmetic. Apps grow with updates, photo libraries grow every week, and a phone or computer that is completely full runs slowly and cannot install updates. A little spare room is not waste — it is what keeps the device working well.

One convention to know: this page counts 1 GB as 1024 MB, which is how phones, tablets and computers report storage. Storage sellers sometimes count 1 GB as 1000 MB instead, which is one reason a drive labelled with one number seems to hold slightly less once it is plugged in. The calculator is consistent throughout, so the total and the recommended plan always use the same measure.

Why storage fills up faster than you expect

Most people underestimate their storage by a wide margin, and the reason is almost always video. A single minute of video can take as much space as hundreds of photos, so a modest collection of family clips can outweigh a whole photo library. People count their photos carefully and then forget the videos entirely.

Apps are the second surprise. The size shown in an app store is only the starting size. An app that begins at 100 MB can grow to several times that as it stores downloaded content, saved messages and cached files. Messaging apps are the worst offenders: every photo and video someone sends you is stored on your device unless you clear it.

Documents are the smallest group for most people, but they still add up when they include presentations, spreadsheets with images and scanned paperwork. And behind all of it, the operating system and its updates take a fixed share that you cannot use at all. That is why a device with a nominal capacity always offers less usable space than the number on the box.

How big is a photo, a video, an app?

There is no single right answer, because sizes depend on your device and your settings. As a rough guide, a photo from a modern phone is often between 2 and 6 MB, a minute of ordinary video is often between 100 and 200 MB, and an app is often between 50 and 500 MB once it has been used for a while. Treat these as starting points, not facts about your device.

The reliable way to get your own numbers is to check a few real files. On most phones, opening a photo and looking at its details shows its size. In a file manager, sorting by size shows the biggest files first, which quickly tells you whether video or photos dominate. In settings, the storage screen breaks the total down by category, which is the fastest reality check of all.

If you are planning for a family, remember that children and teenagers often have far more video than adults, and that anyone who records in a high-quality mode will have much larger files. When in doubt, enter the larger average you measured rather than the smaller one — it is better for the calculator to overestimate slightly than to leave you short.

Worked example: a family phone

Take a phone with 2000 photos at an average of 4 MB each, 100 videos at 300 MB each, 50 apps at 150 MB each and 500 documents at 2 MB each. The calculator works through each group in turn. Photos come to 2000 times 4, which is 8000 MB. Videos come to 100 times 300, which is 30000 MB. Apps come to 50 times 150, which is 7500 MB. Documents come to 500 times 2, which is 1000 MB.

Adding the four groups gives 46500 MB in total. Dividing by 1024 turns that into 45.4 GB. That is the space the files themselves need, before any headroom. Notice how the videos alone, at 30000 MB, are more than three times the photos, even though there are only 100 of them. That is the pattern on most phones.

With 20 percent headroom, 45.4 GB becomes 54.5 GB. The smallest common plan size at or above that figure is a 100 GB plan, so that is what the calculator recommends. A 50 GB plan would hold the files today but leave no room for next month, and a phone with no free space is a phone that starts refusing updates.

Choosing a plan size without overpaying

The cheapest plan that fits today is not always the cheapest plan over a year. If you pick a size with no headroom, you will be back choosing again within months, and the time spent moving files around is worth more than the small saving. The 20 percent rule in the calculator is a balance: enough room for a few months of growth, without jumping to a size you will never fill.

Think about how fast your collection grows. If you add roughly the same number of photos every month, work out the monthly increase and multiply by twelve — that is next year's growth, and it belongs in the plan you buy now. If your collection is mostly static, the calculator's recommendation is already generous.

Sometimes the right answer is not a bigger plan at all. Moving older photos and videos to a computer or an external drive frees the phone without any monthly cost, and it is often the better choice for files you rarely open. The calculator helps you see the size of the job before you decide which way to solve it.

Another question is whether the plan covers one device or several. A family plan shared across phones, tablets and computers fills faster than a plan for one phone, because every device adds its own photos and backups. Run the calculator once per device and add the totals together before you compare plan sizes, or you will buy a plan that fits one phone and starves the rest. The same applies to a small business: count every device that will share the plan, not just your own.

Freeing space before you buy more

Before paying for more storage, it is worth an hour of tidying, because most devices carry a surprising amount of dead weight. Start with duplicates: burst photos, accidental screenshots and the same video saved twice. Then look at downloads — installers and attachments you opened once and never needed again.

  • Back up photos and videos to a computer, then remove the copies you no longer need on the phone.
  • Clear the cache of messaging and social apps, which can hold gigabytes of media you have already seen.
  • Remove apps you have not opened in months; you can install them again later if you miss them.
  • Delete downloaded films, music and maps from trips that are over.
  • Empty the deleted-items folder, because files there still take up space until it is emptied.

Work through the list and then run the calculator again with the smaller numbers. Many people find the tidy-up alone drops them a whole plan size, which is a saving every month from then on. Even when it does not, you now know exactly what you are paying to keep.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is counting photos and forgetting video, which is like budgeting for groceries and forgetting the rent. Video dominates on most devices, so if you enter one careful number, make it the video number. The second mistake is entering the size of a new app rather than the size it has grown to after months of use.

Another common error is mixing up the two ways of counting gigabytes. If you divide by 1000 in your head and the phone divides by 1024, your estimate will look slightly better than reality. The calculator uses 1024 throughout, so its total matches what your device reports.

Finally, do not treat the total as a one-off. Storage is a moving number: photos arrive weekly, apps update monthly, and a single holiday can add more than a whole ordinary month. Recheck the numbers every few months, and treat the recommended plan as a size that should still fit in six months, not just today.

One more mistake is forgetting that storage needs change when you change device. A new phone with a better camera takes larger photos and videos than the old one, so the same number of files suddenly needs more space. A new laptop with a bigger screen invites larger downloads. Whenever you upgrade a device, run the calculator again with the new file sizes rather than assuming the old plan still fits.

Questions people ask

How much storage do I need for my photos?

Multiply the number of photos by the average size of one photo. As a rough guide, 2000 photos at 4 MB each need about 8 GB. The calculator does this sum for photos, videos, apps and documents together, so you get one total instead of four separate guesses.

What is the difference between MB and GB?

A gigabyte is much larger than a megabyte: this page counts 1 GB as 1024 MB, the way phones and computers report storage. A single photo is usually a few megabytes, while a film or a large app is measured in gigabytes.

Why does my phone say storage is full when my files are small?

The operating system, its updates and your apps all take space before your files are counted, and apps grow as they store downloads and cached media. The calculator counts only the files you enter, so the total it gives is the space your files need on top of what the system already uses.

Does this calculator look at my files?

No. It only works with the numbers you type in, and nothing is uploaded or read from your device. That is why the results are only as good as the sizes you enter, so check a few real files first if you are unsure.

How much headroom should I leave?

About 20 percent is a sensible rule, which is what the calculator adds before it suggests a plan size. A device with almost no free space runs slowly, cannot install updates and may refuse to save new photos.

Should I count videos separately from photos?

Yes, always. Videos are usually far bigger per file than photos, so a small number of videos can outweigh a large photo library. Counting them together with one average size hides the group that matters most.
