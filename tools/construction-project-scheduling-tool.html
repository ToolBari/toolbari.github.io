How to use the scheduler

Add your jobs one at a time: a name, a start date, and a length in days. The timeline draws itself as you go, with each job as a bar placed by its start date and sized by its length. The summary at the top tells you the project's first and last dates, the total length in days, and — most usefully — which jobs overlap.

An overlap is not automatically a problem. Two jobs can share a site when they work in different areas or use different crews. But an overlap you did not plan is a clash waiting to happen: two crews needing the same space, the same access, or the same equipment on the same day. This tool flags every overlap so you can decide, in advance, whether it is fine or a problem.

Delete a job with its delete button, and the timeline redraws. Copy the schedule to share it as plain text — with dates, lengths and overlaps included — or clear everything and start over. Everything stays in this browser only, on this device, and your schedule is saved automatically as you add jobs.

The tool plans dates; it does not know your site. A schedule that looks perfect on screen can still fail on the ground, and the rest of this page is about the judgement that turns a list of dates into a plan that survives contact with reality.

Breaking a build into jobs

A build is not one job — it is dozens of jobs, and a schedule is only as good as the list it is built from. Start by writing down every job the build needs: groundworks, foundations, walls, roof, windows, plaster, paint, and everything in between. If a job is not on the list, it is not in the schedule, and a job that is not in the schedule is a surprise waiting to happen.

Keep the job list honest. A job called "Everything else" is not a job, it is a confession that the plan is incomplete. Every job on the site should appear as its own entry, with its own dates, so that nothing is forgotten and nothing is assumed. If you cannot name a job, you have not planned it.

Make jobs small enough to schedule. A job that takes three months is not a job, it is a phase — break it into the steps it actually contains, each with its own start and length. Small jobs are easier to estimate, easier to track, and easier to move when something slips. A schedule of small jobs is a schedule you can actually follow.

Give every job an honest length. A job that took eight days last time will take eight days this time, and writing five because the deadline is tight does not make it five — it makes the schedule a fiction, and fictions are discovered on site, at the worst moment. Ask the people who will do the work how long it takes, and believe them.

Do not forget the invisible jobs. Drying time, curing time, waiting for an inspection, waiting for a delivery — these are not jobs anyone does, but they take days all the same, and a schedule that leaves them out is a schedule that is wrong from the start. Put them in as jobs of their own, with honest lengths, so the timeline tells the truth.

The order jobs must go in

Some jobs cannot start until others finish. You cannot plaster a wall that does not exist, you cannot fit a kitchen in a room with no floor, and you cannot put a roof on walls that are not built. These dependencies are the skeleton of your schedule, and getting them right matters more than getting any single date right — because a dependency missed is a crew standing idle, being paid to wait.

This tool does not link jobs together automatically — it shows you the dates, and the order is yours to set. When you add a job, ask: what must be finished before this can start? Then set its start date after that job's end date, and check the timeline to make sure the bars do not tell a different story.

Watch for the chain. A project has a longest chain of dependent jobs — foundations, walls, roof, plaster, paint — and that chain sets the earliest possible finish date, whatever you do to the other jobs. Shortening a job that is not on the chain changes nothing; shortening one that is changes everything. The timeline makes the chain visible: look for the bars that run end to end with no gaps.

Where two jobs genuinely can run together, let them — but only if they use different crews and different parts of the site. Two jobs sharing one crew is not two jobs running together; it is one crew split in half, and both jobs will take longer than planned.

Overlaps are also where safety problems hide. Heavy work above and finishing work below, or noisy work beside work that needs concentration, are combinations that cause accidents and mistakes. A schedule that keeps dangerous combinations apart is not just efficient — it is part of looking after the people on site.

What to do when a job slips

A job will slip. Materials arrive late, weather turns, a hidden problem appears when a wall comes down — and the question is never whether a slip will happen, but what you do when it does. The first rule is simple: update the schedule the same day. A schedule that is a week out of date is worse than no schedule, because people follow it and are surprised.

When a job slips, move what depends on it. A job that starts after the slipped one may need to move too, and the chain may shift. The timeline redraws with every change, so check it after each move — one fix that creates two new clashes is not a fix. Read the summary after every change, and resolve every overlap you did not intend.

Do not try to catch up by squeezing the next job. Cutting drying time to make up a lost week is how plaster fails and paint peels — the days you save are borrowed from the quality of the finished work, and the debt comes due later, when fixing it costs far more than the week you saved. Some times cannot be shortened, and drying time is one of them.

Use the slack you built in. A schedule with a few spare days between jobs absorbs a small slip without moving anything — that is what the slack is for. If you built no slack, the slip passes down the chain to every job after it, and a one-week delay becomes a one-month delay. Slack is not wasted time; it is what keeps one delay from becoming ten.

Tell everyone what changed. A schedule is only useful if the people doing the work know it. When a date moves, tell the crew, the suppliers and anyone else affected — the same day, in plain words. A surprise on site costs far more than a phone call.

Weather and materials deserve their own honesty. Outdoor work in the rainy season will lose days — not might lose days, will lose them — and a schedule that pretends otherwise will be rewritten in the rain. If a supplier has ever been a week late, plan as if they will be a week late again, and do not schedule the job that needs the material for the day it is supposed to arrive.

What this tool cannot do

This tool cannot know your site. It does not know your ground conditions, your access, your crew's skill, or your local weather — and it cannot tell you how long a job will really take. The lengths you enter are your judgement, and the timeline is only as good as the numbers you put into it.

It cannot manage dependencies for you. It shows dates and flags overlaps, but it does not know that plastering must wait for the walls, or that the roof must be on before the plaster starts. That knowledge is yours, and the schedule is where you write it down — not where you discover it.

It cannot track progress, costs or materials. There is no budget, no list of deliveries, and no record of what is actually done versus what is planned. For tracking progress against the plan, you need a different tool — this one plans the dates, and the dates are its whole job.

It cannot share your schedule with anyone. Your jobs stay in this browser only, on this device. Use the copy button to share the schedule as plain text, and remember that a copied schedule is a snapshot — if the plan changes, the copy is out of date.

Common mistakes

The most famous mistake is scheduling plastering before the roof. It sounds absurd, and it happens — because the schedule was built from a list, not from the order the work must go in. Always check the order: what must be finished before this job can start? If the answer is a job that starts later, the schedule is wrong.

The second is forgetting drying time. Plaster dries, concrete cures, paint needs its coats to dry between them — and none of that happens faster because the deadline is tight. Drying time is a job with a length, and it belongs in the schedule like any other. Leave it out, and the schedule is a fiction from the first week.

The third is hopeful durations. A job that takes eight days does not take five because the deadline is tight — it takes eight days, and the schedule should say eight. Hopeful numbers make a schedule that looks good on paper and fails on site, and the failure is discovered at the worst possible moment.

The fourth is no slack anywhere. A schedule packed tight, with every job starting the day the last one ends, has no room for anything to go wrong — and on a building site, something always goes wrong. Leave gaps. The gaps are not wasted time; they are what keep one delay from becoming ten.

The fifth is ignoring overlaps. The tool flags them for a reason: an overlap you did not plan is a clash, and a clash discovered on site costs far more than a clash fixed on screen. Read the summary after every change, and resolve every overlap you did not intend.

The sixth is a schedule that never gets updated. A plan made in week one and never touched again is a historical document, not a schedule. Update it when things change — the same day, while the change is fresh — and tell everyone what moved.

The seventh is planning in isolation. A schedule built by one person, without talking to the people who will do the work, is a schedule built on guesses about how long things take and in what order they can run. Talk to the crew. They know how long the work takes, and they will follow a schedule they helped build far more willingly than one handed down to them.

This tool plans dates — it cannot know your site, your crew, or your weather. Every date it shows is a plan, not a promise: check it against reality, allow for delays, and never treat a schedule as a substitute for talking to the people doing the work.

Questions people ask

How do I plan a construction schedule?

List every job the build needs, set a start date and an honest length in days for each one, and check the order — what must finish before each job can start. The timeline shows the whole project at a glance, and the summary flags any jobs that overlap.

What comes first in a build?

Groundworks and foundations come first, then the structure — walls and roof — before the inside work begins. The roof must be on before plastering starts, and plaster must be dry before painting. The order is the skeleton of your schedule: get it right first, then set the dates.

How long does a small build take?

There is no single answer — it depends on the jobs involved, the crew, the weather and the site. Anyone who gives you one number without seeing the job list is guessing. List the jobs, estimate each one honestly with the people who will do the work, and this tool adds up the total.

What if a job runs late?

Update the schedule the same day: move the job, move anything that depends on it, and check the timeline for new overlaps. Do not squeeze the next job to catch up — drying time and curing time cannot be shortened without damaging the work. Tell the crew and suppliers what changed.

Can I print my schedule?

Yes. The copy button copies your schedule as plain text — dates, lengths and overlaps included — which you can paste into a document and print from there. You can also print this page directly from your browser.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your jobs stay in this browser only, on your own device, and this page does not connect to any server. If you clear your browser data, your schedule will be removed, so use the copy button to keep a backup.
