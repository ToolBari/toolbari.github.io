Morning Adhkar — Start the Day with Remembrance

Morning adhkar is the remembrance said after Fajr until sunrise: the morning dua, "Asbahna wa asbahal-mulku lillah..." (Sunan Abi Dawud 5068), and SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi one hundred times (Sahih Muslim 2692). It takes about ten minutes, and it sets the whole day right — the first words of the day belong to Allah, and the sins of the day start being wiped away before the day has really begun.

What morning adhkar means

Adhkar is the plural of dhikr, and it means the remembrances — the set phrases the Prophet (peace be upon him) said at fixed times of the day. Morning adhkar is the set said after Fajr prayer until the sun rises. It is not one phrase but a small routine: the morning dua that declares the kingdom belongs to Allah, and then one hundred SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi. Together they take about ten minutes, and they are the first work of the day.

The morning matters because the day is decided in the morning. A Muslim wakes up, prays Fajr, and then has a choice: go straight into the noise of the day, or sit for a few minutes and remember Allah first. The morning adhkar is that sitting. The phone can wait. The news can wait. The first words after prayer belong to Allah, and the rest of the day follows the direction those words set. A day that starts with "the whole kingdom belongs to Allah" is a day that is hard to spend chasing the world as if it belonged to you.

The morning dua itself is a declaration of ownership. "We have entered the morning and the whole kingdom belongs to Allah." Everything you will touch today — your money, your work, your family, your health — belongs to Allah, and the day begins by saying so. That is why the dua pairs the kingdom with praise and with tawhid in a single breath: the kingdom is His, the praise is His, and there is no god but Him, alone, without partner. A Muslim who says that in the morning has already put the whole day in its right place before doing anything else.

Morning adhkar is also protection. The day ahead is full of things you cannot control: a bad message, a lost deal, an accident, a harsh word. The morning remembrance does not remove those things, but it changes how you meet them. A heart that has spent ten minutes remembering Allah in the morning does not panic the way an empty heart panics. The protection is not a wall around the day; it is a heart that knows who owns the day.

Arabic, transliteration and meaning

أَصْبَحْنَا وَأَصْبَحَ الْمُلْكُ لِلَّهِ، وَالْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ، لَا إِلَهَ إِلَّا اللَّهُ وَحْدَهُ لَا شَرِيكَ لَهُ، لَهُ الْمُلْكُ وَلَهُ الْحَمْدُ وَهُوَ عَلَى كُلِّ شَيْءٍ قَدِيرٌ

Transliteration: Asbahna wa asbahal-mulku lillah, wal-hamdu lillah, la ilaha illallahu wahdahu la sharika lah, lahul-mulku wa lahul-hamdu wa huwa 'ala kulli shay'in qadir. Meaning: "We have entered the morning and the whole kingdom belongs to Allah. All praise is for Allah. There is no god but Allah, alone, without partner. To Him belongs the kingdom and to Him belongs all praise, and He is able to do all things."

Say the morning dua once, slowly, after Fajr. The first half is about the morning itself: we have entered it, and the kingdom belongs to Allah. The second half is the words of tawhid — the same phrase that the Tawhid 100× guide teaches as a hundred-a-day dhikr. In the morning dua it is said once, as the crown of the sentence. Learn the dua in two halves: first "Asbahna wa asbahal-mulku lillah, wal-hamdu lillah", then the tawhid phrase. Two halves, one sentence, and the whole day's direction in it.

سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ وَبِحَمْدِهِ

Transliteration: SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi. Meaning: "Glory and praise be to Allah" — Allah is perfect and free of every imperfection, and all praise belongs to Him. Say it one hundred times in the morning. The phrase is short — two words — and it is the same dhikr taught in the SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi guide, where the full reward is explained. In the morning it is said a hundred times, steadily, one after another, until the count is complete.

If you are learning the Arabic, take a week to get the morning dua right before you worry about speed. Read it from the page above, listen to a trusted reciter, and say it aloud after Fajr every day. The harakat — the small vowel marks above and below the letters — are shown here so the pronunciation is clear. A correct dua said slowly is better than a fast dua said wrong, and after a week of daily practice the words will come without looking.

How many times, and when

The window for morning adhkar is after Fajr until sunrise. That is the time the Prophet (peace be upon him) taught for it, and it is the time the reward belongs to. Sit after Fajr prayer, say the morning dua once, then say SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi one hundred times. The whole routine takes about ten minutes at a calm pace — the dua takes under a minute, and the hundred takes eight or nine.

The order is simple and worth keeping: the morning dua first, then the hundred. The dua opens the morning — it names the day and hands it to Allah — and the hundred follows as the work of the morning. If you reverse the order, nothing is lost, but the opening matters: start with the declaration, then do the counting. A routine with a fixed order is a routine that survives tired mornings, because you do not have to decide what to do next.

If you wake up after sunrise, the morning window has passed, but the dhikr is not wasted. Say the morning dua and the hundred anyway, in the morning, as soon as you can. The reward tied to the exact time belongs to the exact time, but the words themselves are still beloved, and the hundred SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi still carries its reward whenever it is said (Sahih Muslim 2692). Do not let a missed window become a missed day: a late morning adhkar is far better than no morning adhkar.

If ten minutes feels long at first, split the hundred. Say the morning dua and thirty-three SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi after Fajr, then finish the remaining sixty-seven on the walk to work or while waiting for the bus. The hundred does not have to be said in one sitting — the count is what matters, not the sitting. But keep the morning dua tied to Fajr: it is the opening of the day, and openings belong at the start.

The reward

The reward for one hundred SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi in the morning is stated plainly: whoever says it one hundred times in a day has his sins wiped away, even if they are like the foam of the sea (Sahih Muslim 2692). The foam of the sea is an image of countless small things — wave after wave, too many to count. That is what a day's small sins look like: not one great sin, but many small ones, piling up unnoticed. One hundred short phrases in the morning wipes them away.

The morning dua carries its own reward in the words themselves. It is a declaration of tawhid — the words that the whole faith stands on — said at the start of the day. A Muslim who begins the day by saying there is no god but Allah, alone, without partner, and that the kingdom and the praise belong to Him, has begun the day with the best words there are. The reward for tawhid is not counted in numbers the way the hundred is; it is the reward of starting the day as a believer starts it, with Allah first.

There is a third reward, quieter than the first two, and it is the one people notice first in their own lives: the day goes differently. A morning spent in remembrance makes the rest of the day calmer. The traffic, the difficult colleague, the bad news — they still come, but they land on a heart that has already spent ten minutes with Allah, and that heart does not shake the way an empty heart shakes. That is the rest the Quran promises: "Verily, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Quran 13:28). The counted reward is for the Hereafter; the rest is for today.

How to count

Count on the fingers of your right hand (Abu Dawud 1502). The simplest method is to press each fingertip in turn with your thumb: three joints on each of four fingers gives twelve, and three rounds gives thirty-six — so thirty-three is just under three full rounds. For a hundred, count three rounds of thirty-three and add one more, or count twelve per hand-round and keep track of the rounds on the other hand. With a little practice the hands learn the count and the tongue is free to think about the words.

Prayer beads are allowed, and many people find them helpful for a long count like a hundred. But the fingers are better: they are always with you, they cost nothing, and they keep the counting quiet and private. The Tasbih Counter tool is a third option for people who like a visible target on a screen. Whichever you use, the rule is the same — count to stay present, not to race. A slow hundred said with the heart is worth more than a hurried hundred said on autopilot.

Two practical tips help the morning count stay honest. First, say the full phrase every time — SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi, not a shortened mumble. The tongue speeds up on its own after a few repetitions, and the phrase shrinks without you noticing. Second, keep the same counting method every day. A method that changes daily is a method that gets abandoned; a method that stays the same becomes a habit, and a habit is what carries you through the mornings when you do not feel like it.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is rushing. The tongue learns the phrase quickly, and after a few days it can run through a hundred in five minutes — but speed is not the goal. If you finish and cannot remember saying it, you went too fast. Slow down until each repetition is a real phrase, said on purpose. Ten minutes at a calm pace is the right speed; there is no prize for finishing in five.

The second mistake is leaving the morning adhkar until later in the day. The window is after Fajr until sunrise, and the habit belongs to that window. If you tell yourself you will do it at noon, you usually will not do it at all — the day fills up, and the dhikr is the first thing dropped. Tie it to Fajr: prayer ends, the dua is said, the hundred is counted, and only then does the day begin. A habit tied to prayer survives; a habit tied to "later" does not.

The third mistake is saying the words without knowing what they mean. The morning dua is a declaration about who owns the day, and it only changes the day if you know what you are saying. Learn the meaning — the kingdom belongs to Allah, the praise belongs to Allah, there is no god but Him — and think about it while you say it. A hundred phrases said with understanding is worth more than a hundred said as sounds.

The fourth mistake is stopping after a few days because the habit feels small. A small habit is the point. Ten minutes every morning, kept for a year, is more than sixty hours of remembrance — and it took ten minutes a day. The people who keep dhikr for life are not the people who started with the biggest numbers; they are the people who started with a small routine and never stopped.

Frequently asked questions

What is morning adhkar?

Morning adhkar is the remembrance said after Fajr until sunrise: the morning dua "Asbahna wa asbahal-mulku lillah..." (Sunan Abi Dawud 5068) and SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi one hundred times (Sahih Muslim 2692).

When is the time for morning adhkar?

After Fajr prayer until sunrise. Sit after Fajr, say the morning dua once, then count one hundred SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi. The whole routine takes about ten minutes.

How many times do I say SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi in the morning?

One hundred times. Whoever says it one hundred times in a day has his sins wiped away, even if they are like the foam of the sea (Sahih Muslim 2692).

What is the reward for the morning adhkar?

One hundred SubhanAllahi wa bihamdihi wipes sins away even if like the foam of the sea (Sahih Muslim 2692), and the morning dua begins the day with tawhid — the best words there are.

Can I say morning adhkar after sunrise?

Yes. The window is after Fajr until sunrise, but if you miss it, say the dua and the hundred anyway in the morning. A late morning adhkar is far better than no morning adhkar.

Do I need wudu for morning adhkar?

No. Dhikr can be said in any state, with or without wudu, sitting, standing or walking. The morning routine is usually done while still seated after Fajr prayer.
