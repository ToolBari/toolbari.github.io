How to use the builder

The builder at the top of this page makes a real SRT subtitle file from lines you type. Each subtitle line has three parts: a start time, an end time, and the words that appear on screen. Add a line for every sentence or phrase in your video, set the times to match when the words are spoken, and the preview below the builder shows the SRT file as you work. When you are happy, download the file and add it to your video. The file is made entirely in your browser, and nothing is uploaded anywhere.

Times can be typed in several ways. Seconds on their own work, so 5 means five seconds into the video. Minutes and seconds work too, so 1:30 means one minute and thirty seconds. For exact timing, add milliseconds after a dot or a comma, such as 1:30.500. The builder checks your lines as you type: it warns you when two lines overlap, when a line stays on screen for too long or too short a time, or when a line has more than 42 characters and is hard to read at a glance.

Timing rules that make subtitles easy to read

A subtitle should appear just as the words are spoken and disappear just after they finish. Most lines stay on screen for one to six seconds. Shorter than one second, and the reader has no time to read the words. Longer than six or seven seconds, and the line sits on screen after the speech has moved on, which feels wrong and pulls the eye away from the video. If a sentence takes longer than seven seconds to say, split it into two lines at a natural pause, such as a comma or a breath.

Leave a small gap between lines when the speaker pauses. A gap of a tenth of a second or two is enough to let the reader notice that one line has ended and another has begun. If two lines touch exactly, with one ending at the same moment the next begins, the subtitle can flicker or feel rushed. The builder warns you about overlapping lines, but a tiny gap between lines is a matter of taste, and a short pause usually reads better than no pause at all.

Timing matters more than perfect wording. Viewers forgive a subtitle that shortens a sentence, because they are watching the video as well as reading. They do not forgive a subtitle that appears three seconds late, because the words and the picture no longer match. When you are unsure, time the line to the speech first and tidy the wording afterwards. Play the video with the subtitles on, watch the first minute, and fix the lines that feel early or late before you move on.

How many words fit on one line

A subtitle line should hold no more than about 42 characters, including spaces, which is roughly six to eight words. Two lines at a time is the most a viewer can read comfortably, so a subtitle holds at most two lines of 42 characters each. Short lines are easier to read than long ones, because the eye scans them in one movement and returns to the video. If a sentence is longer than two short lines, shorten it: cut filler words, drop repeated phrases, and keep the meaning.

Breaking a line in the right place matters as much as its length. Break after a natural pause, such as a comma, the word and, or the end of a phrase, and keep words that belong together on the same line. A line that ends halfway through a phrase forces the reader to hold the first half in mind while reading the second half, which is tiring over a whole video. Read each subtitle aloud as you write it: if you run out of breath, the line is too long, and if the break feels odd, move it to the next pause.

Numbers, names and unusual words need extra care. Write numbers as digits when they are short, such as 5 or 2027, and spell them out when they are long, because a long string of digits is hard to read quickly. Check the spelling of every name, because a wrong name in a subtitle is the mistake viewers notice first. If a speaker uses a word your audience may not know, keep the word and let the video explain it, rather than replacing it with a plainer word that changes the meaning.

The auto-subtitle methods compared

This builder makes subtitles from lines you type, which is the most accurate way to subtitle a short video. For a long video, typing every line takes a long time, and that is when automatic subtitles help. Automatic subtitle tools listen to the video and write the words for you. They are fast, and for clear speech in a quiet recording they are surprisingly good. They are never perfect, and the guide below explains their mistakes and how to fix them.

The best-known free automatic method is the subtitle tool inside YouTube Studio. Upload your video, open the subtitles section, and YouTube writes automatic captions for it. You can then edit the captions line by line, fix the mistakes, and download the result as an SRT file. The automatic captions are a starting point, not a finished product, and a careful edit takes about a quarter of the time that typing from scratch would take.

Free video editing apps on phones and computers also offer automatic subtitles now, and they work in a similar way: the app listens to the audio track, writes the words, and lets you edit them before you export the video. The quality depends on the recording. Clear speech, one speaker at a time, and a quiet background give the best results. Music, background noise, several people talking at once, and strong accents all make the automatic text worse, and a noisy recording can come back with more mistakes than correct words.

Fixing the usual auto-subtitle mistakes

Automatic subtitles make the same kinds of mistakes everywhere, and knowing them makes the fix quick. Names are the first casualty: a person name, a place name, or a brand name often comes back as a similar-sounding ordinary word. Read through the automatic text once with the video playing, and fix every name first, because names are the words viewers notice. Numbers are the second casualty, especially dates and prices, so check every number against what the speaker actually said.

Timing mistakes are the next problem. Automatic tools sometimes split a sentence in the wrong place, so that one line holds the end of one sentence and the start of the next. Merge the broken lines, then split them again at a natural pause. They also sometimes miss a short word at the start of a sentence, such as a name or a yes, so listen to the first word of each line and add the missing word. Finally, automatic tools rarely add punctuation well, so read the whole file once for commas and full stops, which make the subtitles far easier to follow.

A practical workflow is to let the automatic tool do the first pass, then fix the result in this builder. Download the automatic SRT file, open it, and copy the lines into the builder, or edit the file directly and check the timing rules in the guide above. Fix the names, then the numbers, then the timing, then the punctuation, in that order, and play the video once with the finished subtitles before you publish it. The result is far better than either typing from scratch or trusting the automatic text as it is.

Adding your SRT file to a video

An SRT file is a separate file that travels beside the video. Most video players and platforms let you add it, and the steps are similar everywhere: find the subtitles or captions section, choose the SRT file, and the player shows the subtitles while the video plays. Keep the SRT file name close to the video file name, because some players find the subtitles automatically when the two names match.

On YouTube, open YouTube Studio, choose the video, open the Subtitles section, and upload the SRT file. YouTube shows the subtitles to viewers who switch captions on, and you can edit them inside YouTube Studio afterwards if you spot a mistake. On a computer, players such as VLC let you add a subtitle file from the Subtitle menu while the video is playing, and they also pick up an SRT file automatically when it sits in the same folder as the video and shares its name.

For social media, the steps depend on the app. Some apps accept an SRT file when you upload the video, and others expect the subtitles to be burned into the video itself, which means the words become part of the picture. This builder makes a plain SRT file, which is the right format for YouTube, players, and any platform that accepts subtitle files. If your platform needs burned-in subtitles, a free video editor can take the SRT file and bake the words into the video, and the timing you set here carries over exactly.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is writing lines that are too long. A line of sixty characters forces the viewer to read quickly and miss the video, and two long lines at once are worse. Keep each line under 42 characters, keep each subtitle to two lines at most, and shorten the wording rather than squeezing more words in. The builder warns you when a line is too long, so watch for the warning as you type.

The second mistake is overlapping times, where one line starts before the previous line has ended. Players handle overlaps badly: some show both lines at once, and some skip one of them. The builder warns you about overlaps, and the fix is simple: make the first line end a fraction of a second before the next one starts. The third mistake is forgetting to save the SRT file. The builder keeps your lines in the page only while the page is open, so download the file when you finish, and keep a copy with your video files.

Questions people ask

What is an SRT file?

An SRT file is a plain text file that holds subtitles. Each entry has a number, a start and end time, and one or two lines of text. Video players and platforms read the file and show the words on screen at the right moment. You can open an SRT file in any text editor to see how it works.

Most lines stay on screen for one to six seconds. Less than one second is too short to read, and more than seven seconds feels wrong because the speech has moved on. If a sentence takes longer than seven seconds, split it into two lines at a natural pause.

Why do auto-subtitles get words wrong?
