What is a SERP?

SERP is short for search engine results page. It is the page of results a search engine shows after someone types a search. When you search for something on Google, the list of blue links you see is the SERP. Every result on that list has the same three parts: a title in blue, a page address in green or grey, and a short description in plain text. Those three lines are the whole of your page as far as the searcher is concerned, until they decide to click.

People choose which result to open in a few seconds, and they choose by reading the title first, then the description. They do not open every result to compare them. They scan down the list, and they click the result that promises the answer they want. That means your title and description are not decoration. They are the advertisement for your page, and they are the only advertisement most visitors will ever see.

The SERP is also where small mistakes become visible. A title that is too long gets cut in the middle of a word. A description that is the same on every page tells the searcher nothing about this page in particular. A page with no description at all gets a random piece of text pulled from the page, which is often the worst possible sentence to show. All of these problems are easy to fix, but only if you see them before you publish. That is the whole job of this tool.

Writing a title people click

A good title makes one promise, uses one keyword, and stays under 60 characters. One promise means the title says what the visitor will get, in plain words: a guide, a calculator, a list, an answer. One keyword means the main word the searcher typed appears in the title, near the start if you can manage it, so the searcher sees at once that the page matches the search. Under 60 characters means the whole title shows in the results, with nothing important cut off at the end.

Put the most important words first. Searchers read from the left, and they decide quickly. A title that starts with the topic and ends with the site name works far better than a title that starts with the site name and buries the topic at the end, because the end is the part that gets cut. If your title must be long, make sure the first 60 characters still make sense on their own, so that even a cut title still promises something clear.

Numbers and plain words help. A title that says how many items, how many steps, or how long something takes gives the searcher a reason to click, because it tells them what to expect. Words like free, simple, step by step and complete work when they are true, and they hurt you when they are not, because a disappointed visitor leaves at once and never comes back. Write the title you would click yourself, then check it in the preview.

  • One promise: say what the page gives the visitor, in plain words.
  • One keyword: use the main search word, near the start of the title.
  • Under 60 characters: keep the whole title visible in the results.
  • Most important words first: the end of a long title may be cut off.
  • Honest words only: never promise what the page does not deliver.

Writing a description that gets the click

The meta description is the short paragraph under the title in the search results. It does not decide where your page ranks, but it decides whether anyone clicks on it once they see it. A good description answers the searcher in one breath: what the page covers, who it is for, and what the visitor will be able to do after reading it. That is a lot to fit into 160 characters, so every word has to earn its place.

Start the description with the answer or the benefit, not with your site name or a long introduction. The first words are the ones the searcher reads, and if they are dull, the rest never gets read. Use plain words and short sentences. If the page is a tool, say what the tool does and that it is free to use. If the page is a guide, say what the guide covers and how many steps it has. If the page answers a question, give the short answer in the description and let the page give the full one.

Every page needs its own description. When every page on a site shares the same description, the searcher cannot tell the pages apart, and the search engine may stop showing your description at all and pick its own text from the page instead. Writing a fresh description for each page takes a few minutes, and it is one of the cheapest improvements you can make to a site. This tool makes it easy: type the description, watch the count, and trim until it fits.

Why titles get cut by width, not only by length

Here is the part most guides leave out: Google cuts titles by width, not only by character count. The title gets about 600 pixels of room on a desktop screen, and each letter takes up a different amount of that room. Wide letters like W and M and capital letters take much more room than narrow letters like i, l and t. That means two titles with the same number of characters can behave very differently. A title full of wide capitals can get cut at 50 characters, while a title of narrow lower-case letters might still fit at 62.

That is why the 60 character guide is a guide, not a guarantee. It works well for ordinary titles written in normal sentence case, because ordinary text averages out. It fails when a title is written in ALL CAPITALS, when it is full of wide words, or when it uses a lot of punctuation. The safe habit is to keep titles short, to write them in normal sentence case, and to check them in a preview like this one before you publish. If the preview shows the cut, shorten the title, even if the count says it should fit.

Mobile screens make this worse, because the same title has less room across a phone. A title that fits on a desktop can still get cut on a phone, and the phone preview in this tool shows you exactly that. When you are choosing between two titles, pick the shorter one. A short title that shows in full always beats a long title that ends in three dots, because the dots hide the very words that would have made the searcher click.

Worked example: before and after

Imagine a page that teaches people how to grow tomatoes at home. The first version of the title reads: The Complete Beginner Guide to Growing Tomatoes at Home in Pots and Garden Beds. That title is 84 characters long. In the results, it gets cut to something like: The Complete Beginner Guide to Growing Tomatoes at Home in... The searcher never sees the words about pots and garden beds, which were the most useful part of the title, and the page looks unfinished.

The fixed title reads: How to Grow Tomatoes at Home — A Simple Guide. That is 45 characters. It fits in full on desktop and on mobile, it starts with the words the searcher typed, and it promises a simple guide. Nothing important is hidden. The title lost some words, but it lost only words the searcher did not need, and the page still covers pots and garden beds inside.

The description had the same problem. The first version read: Welcome to our website. Here you will find everything you need to know about growing tomatoes at home, including how to pick seeds, prepare soil, water your plants, feed them, deal with pests and harvest your tomatoes when they are ripe. That is far over 160 characters, and it wastes the first words on a welcome nobody needs. The fixed version reads: Learn how to grow tomatoes at home step by step, from picking the right seeds to watering, feeding and harvesting your first ripe tomatoes. It fits, it starts with the promise, and it says exactly what the page covers.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a clickbait title that disappoints. A title that promises a secret, a miracle or a result the page cannot deliver will get clicks for a day and lose readers for good. Visitors who feel tricked leave at once, and they remember the site name for the wrong reason. A plain honest title that promises what the page really gives will always do better over time than a clever title that overpromises.

The second mistake is using the same description on every page. It usually happens because a site template fills in one default description for all pages, and nobody notices. The fix is simple but slow: go through the pages one by one and write a fresh description for each, starting with the pages that get the most visits. The preview tool makes the checking fast, but the writing still has to be done by hand.

The third mistake is stuffing the title or description with keywords until it reads like a list of search terms instead of a sentence. Search engines stopped rewarding that years ago, and readers never liked it. One keyword, used naturally, is enough. A title that reads like a sentence written for a person will always beat a title that reads like a list written for a machine.

The fourth mistake is leaving the description empty and letting the search engine pick its own text from the page. The picked text is often a menu item, a cookie notice, or the first sentence of the page, which is rarely the best sentence to show. Writing your own description takes five minutes and gives you control over the only words most searchers will ever read about your page.

What this tool cannot do

This tool previews your result; it does not publish it. After you copy your title and description, you still have to paste them into your own page, in the title tag and the meta description tag. If your site is built with a page builder or a content system, those fields are usually in the page settings or the SEO section of the editor.

The preview is also a close guide, not a perfect copy of Google. Google changes its layout from time to time, and it sometimes rewrites titles and descriptions when it thinks its own version fits the search better. That happens most often when a title is too long, when a description is missing, or when the search matches a different part of the page. A short, honest title and a fresh description for each page are the best way to keep Google showing your words instead of its own.

Finally, this tool cannot tell you how your page will rank. Ranking depends on the page itself, on how well it answers the search, and on many other things this page cannot measure. What the tool can do is make sure that when your page does appear in the results, it looks complete, honest and worth clicking. That is a small job, but it is the job that turns a ranking into a visitor.

Questions people ask

What is a SERP?

A SERP is the page of results a search engine shows after a search. Each result has a title, a page address and a short description, and people pick which page to open by reading those three lines.

How long should my page title be?

Keep your title under 60 characters. Longer titles get cut in the results, and the cut often hides the most important words at the end.

How long should my meta description be?

Keep your meta description under 160 characters. Longer descriptions get cut in the results, so put the main promise and the main benefit in the first 160 characters.

Why does Google cut my title even when it is under 60 characters?

Google cuts titles by width, not only by character count. Wide letters and capital letters take more room, so a short title full of wide capitals can still get cut. Keep titles short and avoid long words in capitals.

Does this preview look exactly like Google?

It is a close preview, not a perfect copy. Google changes its layout from time to time and may rewrite your title or description, but the preview shows the cut points and the shape of the result well enough to fix problems before you publish.
