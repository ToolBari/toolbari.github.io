Tasbih Fatimah — The Bedtime Dhikr

Tasbih Fatimah is the dhikr for bedtime: SubhanAllah thirty-three times, Alhamdulillah thirty-three times, and Allahu Akbar thirty-four times before sleep. The Prophet (peace be upon him) taught it to his daughter Fatimah (may Allah be pleased with her) when she asked for a servant, and told her it is better than a servant (Sahih al-Bukhari 3705, Sahih Muslim 2727).

What Tasbih Fatimah means

Fatimah (may Allah be pleased with her) was the daughter of the Prophet (peace be upon him), and her hands were worn from grinding grain and drawing water. She asked for a servant to help with the work of the house. The Prophet (peace be upon him) did not give her a servant. Instead, he gave her something better: a short dhikr to say every night before sleep. SubhanAllah thirty-three times, Alhamdulillah thirty-three times, Allahu Akbar thirty-four times. That is Tasbih Fatimah, and it has been said by Muslims at bedtime ever since.

The words "better than a servant" are worth stopping on. A servant would have eased the work of Fatimah's hands — the grinding, the carrying, the cooking. The tasbih does something a servant cannot do: it fills the night with the remembrance of Allah, it earns reward while the body rests, and it gives a strength that does not come from another person. A servant helps for a day; a habit of dhikr helps for a lifetime, and it keeps helping after death, when no servant can help at all. The Prophet (peace be upon him) was not refusing his daughter's need. He was answering it with something greater than what she asked for.

That is the first lesson of Tasbih Fatimah: when you ask Allah for help with a burden, the answer may not be a lighter load. It may be a stronger heart. The tasbih does not grind the grain or draw the water, but it changes the person who grinds and draws. A tongue that ends the day with the praise of Allah wakes up with a heart that is closer to Him, and a day that starts with a heart close to Allah is a lighter day, whatever work it holds.

The three phrases are the same three said after every prayer — SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah and Allahu Akbar — but here they are counted for the night. SubhanAllah declares Allah perfect and free of every flaw. Alhamdulillah praises Him for everything He has given. Allahu Akbar declares Him greater than everything, including the worries of the day that follow a person into bed. Said together at the end of the day, the three phrases are a closing of accounts: the day ends with Allah declared perfect, praised and magnified, and the night begins under His protection.

Arabic, transliteration and meaning

سُبْحَانَ اللَّهِ

Transliteration: SubhanAllah — 33 times. Meaning: "Glory be to Allah" — Allah is perfect and free of every imperfection.

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ

Transliteration: Alhamdulillah — 33 times. Meaning: "All praise is for Allah" — all praise belongs to Him alone.

اللَّهُ أَكْبَرُ

Transliteration: Allahu Akbar — 34 times. Meaning: "Allah is the Greatest" — greater than everything, including every worry of the day.

Say the three phrases in order: SubhanAllah thirty-three times, then Alhamdulillah thirty-three times, then Allahu Akbar thirty-four times. The total is one hundred, and the extra Allahu Akbar is what makes the hundred complete — thirty-three plus thirty-three plus thirty-four. Keep the order the same every night, so the habit runs by itself once the body has learned it. If you are learning the Arabic, read each phrase from the page above and listen to a trusted reciter saying it; the harakat — the small vowel marks above and below the letters — are shown so the pronunciation is clear.

Take a night or two to get the counts right. The number that trips people up is the last one: thirty-four Allahu Akbar, not thirty-three. Count the first two phrases on the fingers, then count the last phrase the same way and add one more. Once the hands know the pattern, the tongue is free to think about the words, and that is the point — the numbers exist so the tongue has something to aim at while the heart learns to be present.

How many times, and when

Tasbih Fatimah is said once a day, every night, before sleep. Thirty-three SubhanAllah, thirty-three Alhamdulillah, thirty-four Allahu Akbar — one hundred phrases in total, taking about two minutes at a calm pace. Say it in bed, after the night adhkar, as the last dhikr of the day. It is the last thing the tongue says before sleep, so the night starts with the remembrance of Allah, and the sleep that follows is a sleep that began with His praise.

The simplest way to keep it is to tie it to getting into bed. Pillow, blanket, tasbih — in that order. If you wait until you are already half asleep, the tongue will not finish, and the habit will die within a week. Sit up in bed, say the hundred phrases, then lie down to sleep. Two minutes sitting up is the whole price of the habit, and it is a small price for a practice the Prophet (peace be upon him) called better than a servant (Sahih al-Bukhari 3705, Sahih Muslim 2727).

If you fall asleep in the middle of the count, nothing is lost. You have said what you said, and the intention was there. The next night, sit up a little earlier, or say it before you get into bed at all — after the night prayer, or while the bedroom is still lit. The habit matters more than the hour: what counts is that it happens every night, not that it happens at a perfect minute.

There is no second sitting for Tasbih Fatimah. It is not repeated in the morning, and it is not said after prayer — the after-salah routine is a different count, with its own guide on this hub. Tasbih Fatimah belongs to the night. One sitting, one hundred phrases, every night. That single-mindedness is part of its strength: a practice tied to one time of day is a practice that is easy to remember, and a practice that is easy to remember is a practice that survives.

The reward

The reward is stated in the words of the Prophet (peace be upon him) himself: this tasbih is better than a servant (Sahih al-Bukhari 3705, Sahih Muslim 2727). Think about what that means for a household. A servant was, in Fatimah's time, the most practical help a person could ask for — someone to take the heavy work off your hands every single day. The Prophet (peace be upon him) said these hundred phrases are better than that. Not equal to it, not almost as good — better. The reward of the dhikr outweighs the help of a servant, because the dhikr helps in both worlds, and a servant can only help in one.

There is a second reward, quieter than the first, and it is the one people notice in their own lives: the night changes. A person who ends the day with SubhanAllah, Alhamdulillah and Allahu Akbar does not carry the day's anger and worry into sleep unchanged. The phrases interrupt the replay of the day — the argument, the bill, the deadline — and they put something better in its place. That is the rest the Quran promises: "Verily, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Quran 13:28). The counted reward is for the Hereafter; the rest is for tonight.

There is a third reward, and it is the most practical of all: strength for the next day. Fatimah (may Allah be pleased with her) asked for help with work, and the Prophet (peace be upon him) gave her a practice that strengthens the one who does the work. The scholars have noted this for centuries — the dhikr of the night gives energy for the labour of the day. A body that sleeps after the praise of Allah wakes with a lighter heart, and a lighter heart carries the same load more easily. That is what "better than a servant" means in daily life: not that the work disappears, but that the worker is strengthened to do it.

None of this makes Tasbih Fatimah a magic formula. The reward belongs to the remembering, and the remembering is meant to be done with the heart present — knowing who Allah is, and meaning the words. A hundred phrases said while thinking about the words is worth more than a hundred said while thinking about tomorrow's chores. The numbers are a target, not a trick: they exist so the tongue has something to aim at while the heart learns to be present, night after night.

How to count

Count on the fingers of your right hand (Abu Dawud 1502). The simplest method is to press each fingertip in turn with your thumb: three joints on each of four fingers gives twelve per round, and three rounds gives thirty-six — so thirty-three is just under three full rounds, and thirty-four is one more than thirty-three. Count SubhanAllah to thirty-three, then start again for Alhamdulillah, then again for Allahu Akbar, adding one extra at the end. With a little practice the hands learn the pattern and the tongue is free to think about the words.

Prayer beads are allowed, and many people find them helpful for a count like this, especially in the dark. But the fingers are better: they are always with you, they cost nothing, and they keep the counting quiet and private. The Tasbih Counter tool is a third option for people who like a visible target on a screen — though at bedtime, the fingers are usually the simplest choice of all, since the phone can stay on the table and the light can stay off.

Two practical tips help the count stay honest. First, say the full phrase every time — SubhanAllah, not a shortened mumble. The tongue speeds up on its own after a few repetitions, and the phrase shrinks without you noticing, especially when you are tired. Second, keep the same counting method every night. A method that changes nightly is a method that gets abandoned; a method that stays the same becomes a habit, and a habit is what carries you through the nights when you do not feel like it.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is saying it only when you are already falling asleep. The tongue starts the count, the eyes close, and the phrases dissolve into sleep before the first thirty-three are done. That is not a failure — the intention was there — but it is not a habit either. Sit up in bed, say the hundred phrases, then lie down. The two minutes of sitting up are what turn a good intention into a kept practice.

The second mistake is the count itself: thirty-three, thirty-three, thirty-three. The last phrase is thirty-four, not thirty-three, and the extra Allahu Akbar is easy to forget once the hands are used to the rhythm of three equal rounds. If you catch yourself finishing at thirty-three, add one more Allahu Akbar and carry on. After a week of counting carefully, the hands will remember the extra one on their own.

The third mistake is rushing. The tongue learns the phrases quickly, and after a few nights it can run through a hundred in under a minute — but speed is not the goal, and at bedtime the temptation to rush is strongest, because sleep is waiting. If you finish and cannot remember saying the phrases, you went too fast. Slow down until each repetition is a real word, said on purpose. Two minutes at a calm pace is the whole cost; there is no prize for finishing in one.

The fourth mistake is treating Tasbih Fatimah as a sleeping aid rather than as dhikr. It is true that the phrases calm the heart and help a person sleep — that is one of their blessings. But the point of the practice is the remembrance of Allah, not the sleep that follows it. A person who says it only to fall asleep has put the blessing before the worship. Say it because the Prophet (peace be upon him) taught it and because it is better than a servant (Sahih al-Bukhari 3705, Sahih Muslim 2727), and let the good sleep be a gift that comes with it, not the reason for it.

Frequently asked questions

What is Tasbih Fatimah?

Tasbih Fatimah is the bedtime dhikr the Prophet (peace be upon him) taught his daughter Fatimah (may Allah be pleased with her): SubhanAllah 33 times, Alhamdulillah 33 times, and Allahu Akbar 34 times before sleep (Sahih al-Bukhari 3705).

Why is it 34 Allahu Akbar and not 33?

Because the total is one hundred: 33 + 33 + 34. The extra Allahu Akbar completes the hundred phrases before sleep.

Why did the Prophet (peace be upon him) say it is better than a servant?

Fatimah (may Allah be pleased with her) asked for a servant to help with the housework, and the Prophet (peace be upon him) gave her this tasbih instead, saying it is better than a servant (Sahih al-Bukhari 3705, Sahih Muslim 2727). The dhikr gives strength and reward that a servant cannot give.

When should I say Tasbih Fatimah?

Every night before sleep, in bed, after the night adhkar. It takes about two minutes. Sit up in bed to say it, then lie down to sleep.

How do I count 33, 33 and 34?

Count on the fingers of your right hand (Abu Dawud 1502). Three joints on each of four fingers gives twelve per round; three rounds is thirty-six, so thirty-three is just under three rounds, and thirty-four is one more.

What if I fall asleep before I finish?

Then you have still said what you said, and the intention counts. Try to say it sitting up in bed first, so you finish before you lie down to sleep.
