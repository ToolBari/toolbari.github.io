Work out your shipping cost

These are estimates from typical courier pricing. Real prices vary by courier, by route and by season, so always check the exact price with your courier before you quote a buyer. The section below shows you how to check with two couriers in a few minutes.

How to use the calculator

Start with the weight of the packed box, not just the product. A kitchen scale works well for small parcels, and a bathroom scale works for bigger ones if you weigh yourself holding the box and then subtract your own weight. Then measure the box: length, width and height in centimetres, at the widest points. Pick your distance zone and your delivery speed, and the calculator shows an estimated cost range straight away.

The last three fields are optional, and they turn a shipping estimate into a selling decision. Packaging cost is what the box, tape and filling cost you. Product cost is what the items inside cost you to make or buy. Quantity is how many items are in the order. With those filled in, the calculator shows the cost per item and the total landed cost, which is the full cost of getting the order to the buyer, so you can set a price that still leaves you a profit.

What makes shipping expensive: weight or size

Couriers charge for whichever is bigger: the actual weight of your parcel or its volumetric weight. Volumetric weight is the weight a parcel would have if it were packed solid, and it is how couriers charge for the space a box takes up in a van or a plane. A box of pillows is light but takes up a lot of room, so the courier charges for the room. A box of books is heavy but small, so the courier charges for the weight. The calculator works out both numbers and charges on the bigger one, exactly as a courier does.

The formula for volumetric weight is simple: multiply length by width by height in centimetres, then divide by 5000. A box measuring 40 by 30 by 25 centimetres gives 40 times 30 times 25, which is 30000, divided by 5000, which is 6 kilograms. If that box actually weighs 4 kilograms, the courier charges for 6. If it weighs 9 kilograms, the courier charges for 9. The number 5000 is the divisor most couriers use, and this calculator uses it too, so the estimate matches how real quotes are worked out.

Distance and speed are the other two things that move the price. A parcel travelling across the country passes through more sorting depots and covers more miles than one going across town, and international parcels cross borders, which adds handling and paperwork. Express delivery costs more because the courier moves the parcel ahead of the standard queue, often on a faster vehicle or an earlier flight. Choosing standard speed for orders that are not urgent is the single easiest way to cut a shipping bill.

Local, national and international shipping

Local delivery covers parcels that stay within one city or area. It is usually the cheapest option, because the parcel may never leave the local depot, and many couriers offer same-day or next-day local delivery at standard prices. If you sell mostly to people near you, local delivery is where your shipping cost stays low, and it is worth offering as a clear option at checkout.

National delivery covers parcels travelling within your own country but outside your local area. The parcel goes through a regional sorting depot and travels by road or by domestic flight, which takes longer and costs more than local delivery. Delivery times are usually counted in days rather than hours, and the price depends on how far the parcel travels and how heavy it is.

International delivery is a different job altogether. The parcel crosses a border, which means a customs form describing what is inside and what it is worth, and the buyer in the destination country may have to pay import duty or tax before the parcel is handed over. Delivery takes longer, tracking can go quiet while the parcel is between countries, and the price is the highest of the three zones. If you sell internationally, say clearly in your listing who pays any import charges, so the buyer is not surprised at the door.

Standard or express: which to choose

Standard delivery is the right choice for most orders. It costs less, and for items the buyer does not need tomorrow, the extra day or two makes no difference to their happiness. Many shops offer standard delivery as the default and let the buyer pay extra for express if they want it. That keeps your prices low for most buyers while still serving the ones in a hurry.

Express delivery earns its price in three situations: the buyer asked for it and is paying for it, the item is urgent such as a gift for a date that is close, or the item is perishable and cannot sit in a depot for days. For everything else, express is money spent for speed nobody needed. When you are unsure, quote standard and mention express as an option with its price, and let the buyer decide.

A worked example: sending 20 handmade candles

Mina makes candles at home and sells them online. A buyer orders 20 candles, and Mina needs to know what shipping will cost before she quotes a price. Each candle weighs 0.3 kilograms, so 20 candles weigh 6 kilograms. She packs them in a box measuring 40 by 30 by 25 centimetres, and the packed box weighs 6 kilograms on her kitchen scale.

First, the volumetric weight: 40 times 30 times 25 is 30000, divided by 5000, which is 6 kilograms. The actual weight is also 6 kilograms, so the chargeable weight is 6 kilograms either way. The buyer is in the same country, and Mina chooses standard delivery. The calculator gives an estimated shipping cost of 24 to 38, with a middle estimate of 30. Spread across 20 candles, that is 1.5 per candle.

Mina paid 4 for the box and the filling, and the candles cost her 100 to make. The total landed cost is 100 for the candles plus 30 for shipping plus 4 for packaging, which is 134 for the whole order. Now she knows the lowest price she can charge without losing money, and she can set her selling price with a clear margin above 134. Packing to save money

The cheapest way to cut shipping costs is to make the box smaller. Couriers charge for size as well as weight, so a box that is twice as big as it needs to be can double the volumetric weight and push the price up. Choose a box that fits the items with just enough room for padding, and cut a large box down if you have to. Flat items can often go in a padded envelope instead of a box, which is lighter and cheaper.

Packing material matters too. Heavy filling adds real weight to the parcel, and the courier charges for it. Light filling such as air pillows or crumpled paper protects just as well for most items and weighs almost nothing. Wrap fragile items individually so they do not knock against each other, fill the gaps so nothing moves, and tape the box firmly so it does not burst open in transit.

Run through this packing checklist before you seal any box:

  • Weigh the packed box, not just the product, and write the weight on your order note.
  • Measure the box at its widest points, including any bulge.
  • Use the smallest box that fits, with light filling in the gaps.
  • Wrap fragile items on their own so they cannot knock together.
  • Tape every seam, and put a copy of the delivery address inside the box as well as outside.
  • Take a photo of the packed parcel before you hand it over, in case of a claim later.

How to check the exact price before you quote a buyer

This calculator gives you an honest estimate, but only a courier can give you an exact price. Before you quote a buyer, check with two couriers. Start with the packed box on the scale and a tape measure in your hand, and write down the weight and the three measurements. Then get a quote from the first courier: most couriers have a price checker on their website or an office where you can ask.

Then get a quote from a second courier for the same parcel, the same destination and the same speed. The two prices are often different, sometimes by a lot, because couriers price routes differently. Take the lower quote if the delivery time suits you, and keep the higher one in mind as your fallback. Add a small buffer to the price you quote the buyer, enough to cover a small weighing difference or a price change, and write the quote down with the date so you can check it again later.

Checking twice takes a few minutes and saves you from the most common selling mistake: quoting a price from memory and finding out at the counter that the real price is higher. If you ship the same product often, keep a note of the two quotes for your usual box and your usual destinations, and update it every few months, because courier prices change over time.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is weighing the product and forgetting the box. The box, the tape and the filling all add weight, and the courier weighs the parcel as you hand it over, not the item on its own. Always weigh the finished, sealed parcel. The second mistake is quoting one courier's price as if it were the only price. Couriers differ, and the first quote you get is not always the best one.

The third mistake is ignoring size. A big light box can cost more to send than a small heavy one, because the courier charges for the space. Measure every box, and never guess. The fourth mistake is forgetting the packaging cost when working out whether an order makes money. A box and filling that cost 4 on every order add up fast across a month of sales. The fifth mistake is promising express delivery at a standard price, which leaves you paying the difference out of your own pocket.

Questions people ask

Is shipping charged by weight or size?

Both. The courier works out the actual weight of your parcel and its volumetric weight, which is the space it takes up, and charges for whichever is bigger. A big light box can cost more to send than a small heavy one, so always measure the box as well as weighing it.

How can I make shipping cheaper?

Use the smallest box that fits, pack with light filling, and choose standard delivery instead of express when the order is not urgent. Compare two couriers before you commit, and send several items in one parcel instead of several small parcels when you can.

Should the buyer pay shipping?

That is your choice, and both ways work. Many shops ask the buyer to pay the delivery cost on top of the price, while others build it into the price and offer free delivery. Whichever you choose, work out the real shipping cost first so the delivery does not eat your profit.

What is volumetric weight?

Volumetric weight is the weight a parcel would have if it were packed solid, worked out from its size: length times width times height in centimetres, divided by 5000. Couriers use it to charge for the space a box takes up, which is why a large light box can cost more than a small heavy one.

How do I ship internationally?

Pack the item well, fill in the customs form that describes what is inside and what it is worth, and check that the item is allowed into the destination country. Allow extra days for delivery, and tell the buyer clearly whether they will have to pay any import duty or tax when the parcel arrives.

What if the package is lost?

Contact the courier with your tracking number and ask them to trace the parcel, and keep your proof of posting and your receipt. Most couriers have a lost-parcel process and a waiting period before they treat a parcel as lost, so start the claim as soon as the delivery date has passed.
