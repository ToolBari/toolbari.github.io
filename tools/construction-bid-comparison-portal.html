What this comparator does and who it is for

A builder's quote is rarely a single number. It is a bundle of materials, labour and extras, and two quotes with the same total can cover very different amounts of work. This comparator takes up to three quotes, breaks each one into its line items, and puts them side by side so you can see where the money goes — and, just as importantly, what is missing.

It is built for anyone hiring a builder for a home job: a new room, a roof repair, a kitchen refit, a small extension. You do not need to know anything about construction to use it. You only need the quotes themselves, and the patience to type each line item into the right box. The comparator does the arithmetic and flags the gaps.

What it will not do is find builders for you or judge their quality. A cheap quote from a builder who never finishes is the most expensive quote of all, and no tool can tell you that from numbers alone. Use this page to compare the money honestly, then check the builder separately — references, past work, and a written contract.

How to use the comparator on your phone or computer

Start with the first quote. Type the builder's name or a label such as Builder A, then enter the materials, labour and extras figures exactly as they appear on the quote. Enter amounts in your own currency — the page never assumes one. If a quote does not list a line item at all, leave that box blank rather than guessing a number.

Repeat for the second and third quotes. The third bid is optional: if you only have two quotes, leave the third block empty and it will be ignored. When everything is entered, tap Compare the bids. The table shows each line item across the three bids, the total of the items entered for each bid, and a clear flag wherever a bid is missing an item.

The copy button copies the whole comparison as plain text, so you can paste it into a message or keep it with your other papers. Nothing you enter leaves your browser — there is no account, no upload and no record kept anywhere else. If you close the page, the numbers are gone, so copy the comparison before you leave.

Reading a builder's quote line by line

A good quote reads like a list, not a mystery. Each line should say what is being supplied or done, how much of it there is, and what it costs. Materials lines usually name the material and the quantity. Labour lines usually give the number of days or hours and the rate. Extras cover everything else: permits, waste removal, scaffolding, delivery charges, and the builder's own margin if it is shown separately.

Read each line twice: once for the price, and once for what is actually included. Two quotes can both say new roof and mean different things — one includes new battens and flashing, the other does not. The words matter as much as the numbers, and this is where the cheapest quote often starts to look less cheap. If a line is vague, ask the builder to spell it out before you compare.

Watch for lines that bundle several things into one figure, such as materials and labour combined. A bundled line cannot be compared with an itemised one, because you cannot see where the money goes. Ask for the bundle to be split. A builder who will not itemise is not necessarily dishonest, but you cannot compare their quote fairly with one that is itemised.

The line items every quote should have

Materials should list the main things being bought for your job: timber, cement, bricks, tiles, pipes, wiring, paint — whatever the job needs. A quote that says materials with one figure and no breakdown is hard to judge, because you cannot tell whether the builder plans to use good materials or the cheapest available. The breakdown is the point.

Labour should show how the work is charged: by the day, by the hour, or as a fixed price for the job. Fixed-price labour protects you if the job runs long; day-rate labour protects the builder. Neither is wrong, but you should know which one you are agreeing to, and the quote should say how many days the builder expects the job to take.

Extras are the lines people forget to compare. Permits, waste removal, scaffolding hire, delivery charges, and cleaning up afterwards all cost money, and a quote that leaves them out will look cheaper than one that includes them. The comparator has an extras box for each bid precisely because these costs decide many comparisons.

Why the cheapest bid is often not the cheapest

The most common reason is a missing item. A bid that forgets the scaffolding, the waste removal or the finishing work will beat a complete bid on price and then cost more once the missing work is added. That is why this comparator flags missing line items instead of treating a blank as zero — a blank is a question, not a saving.

The second reason is cheaper materials. Two quotes for the same job can use very different grades of timber, tile or pipe, and the difference shows up years later in repairs. If one bid is much lower than the others, ask what materials it assumes. A builder who names the grade and the supplier is easier to trust than one who just says materials.

The third reason is the builder's own situation. A builder who is quiet may price low to win work, and that can be a genuine bargain. But a builder who prices below cost to win the job may cut corners, disappear halfway, or ask for more money later. A low price is a signal to check harder, not a reason to stop checking.

A worked example with three bids

Imagine three quotes for a small bathroom refit. Bid A lists materials 12,000, labour 8,000 and extras 1,500 — a total of 21,500. Bid B lists materials 11,000, labour 9,000 and extras 0 — a total of 20,000. Bid C lists materials 10,500 and labour 7,500, with extras left blank — a total of 18,000 for the items entered, with extras flagged as missing.

Bid C looks cheapest at first glance, but the flag changes the picture. If the extras for Bid C turn out to be 2,500 — permits, waste removal and delivery — the real total is 20,500, which is more than Bid B. And Bid B's extras of 0 deserves a question too: does it really include waste removal and permits for nothing, or has the builder simply left them out of the quote?

The worked example shows the habit that matters: compare totals only after the missing items are known. The comparator does the arithmetic, but the questions are yours to ask. A ten-minute call to each builder asking what is included will usually settle which bid is genuinely cheapest.

Common mistakes when comparing bids

The first mistake is comparing totals without comparing what is included. Two totals mean nothing unless the same work sits behind them. Always check that each bid covers the same rooms, the same materials and the same finishing work before you compare the numbers.

The second mistake is ignoring the extras. Permits, waste removal, scaffolding and delivery are real costs, and a quote that omits them is not cheaper — it is incomplete. Add the missing costs in your head, or better, ask the builder to quote them, before you decide.

The third mistake is deciding on price alone. A builder's reliability, past work and willingness to put things in writing matter at least as much as the total. Get references, look at a finished job if you can, and never pay the full amount upfront. The comparator tells you which bid costs less; only your own checking tells you which builder to trust.

Finally, do not rush the decision. A builder who pressures you to sign today is a builder to avoid, and a quote is not a contract until you agree to it. Take the comparison home, sleep on it, and check the references before you say yes — the cheapest bid will still be there tomorrow, and so will the questions you forgot to ask.

Questions people ask

How many bids should I compare?

Three is a good number for most jobs: one bid tells you nothing, two can leave you guessing, and three usually shows you the range. If the three are close together, the price is probably fair; if one is far below the others, ask what it leaves out.

What should a builder's quote include?

Materials with a breakdown, labour with the charging method and expected days, and extras such as permits, waste removal, scaffolding and delivery. It should also say when the work starts, how long it takes, and how payment is staged.

Why is one bid much cheaper?

Usually because something is missing: an unlisted line item, cheaper materials, or less work covered. Sometimes a builder is genuinely quiet and prices low to win work. Ask what is included before assuming the cheapest bid is the best deal.

Should I always pick the cheapest?

No. The cheapest bid can hide missing work, cheap materials or a builder who will not finish. Compare what is included, check references, and look at past work before deciding. Price is one factor, not the whole decision.

What are hidden extras?

Costs that are real but easy to miss: permits, waste removal, scaffolding hire, delivery charges and cleaning up. A quote that leaves them out looks cheaper than one that includes them, so always ask whether they are covered.

How do I check a builder's quality?

Ask for references from recent jobs and speak to those customers, look at a finished job if you can, and check that the builder puts the quote and the scope of work in writing. A builder who avoids written agreements is a warning sign.
