Planning a short e-book: the five-part shape

A short e-book does not need a complicated plan. The five-part shape works for almost any topic: a promise, three middle parts, and an ending. The promise is your first chapter, and it tells the reader what the book will do for them and who it is for. The three middle parts each cover one idea, one step or one story. The ending gathers the threads, tells the reader what to do next, and closes the book properly. Five chapters of this shape make a satisfying short book, and you can grow it by splitting a middle part into two.

Write your chapter titles before you write any chapter text. Titles force you to decide what each chapter is about, and a list of titles is a map of the whole book. If two titles say nearly the same thing, merge the chapters. If a title is vague, such as Things to Know, sharpen it until it promises something specific. A reader who sees your chapter list should be able to guess the journey of the book, from the promise at the start to the ending at the close.

Do not let the title block you. A working title is enough to begin with: something plain that says what the book is about, such as A Short Guide to Growing Herbs. You can change it at the end, when you finally know what the book became. Good titles are specific and honest: they promise one clear thing and the book delivers it. Avoid titles that promise everything, because a reader who feels promised everything expects everything and forgives nothing.

The shape bends to fit your topic. A how-to guide might run: promise, getting ready, the main steps, fixing problems, and what to do next. A collection of stories might run: why these stories, then one story per chapter, then a closing reflection. A family recipe book might run: how we cook, then one chapter per occasion, then a final chapter of kitchen wisdom. The shape is a tool, not a rule: use it until your book tells you what it wants to be.

Writing one chapter at a time

One chapter at a time is the whole secret of finishing a book. A chapter is small enough to write in a single sitting, and each finished chapter is proof that the book is real. Pick the chapter you feel most ready to write, even if it is not the first one: you can reorder the list later, and a book begun in the middle is still a book begun. Write the first draft quickly and badly if you must; you can fix ugly sentences, but you cannot fix a blank page.

Give each writing session a small target, such as one chapter or three hundred words, and stop when you reach it. Short regular sessions beat rare long ones, because a book written a little at a time stays fresh in your mind. If you get stuck in the middle of a chapter, write a single sentence that says what the next paragraph should do, then move on. You can come back to the hard part tomorrow with a clearer head, and the sentence you left behind will show you where to begin.

What makes a chapter work

A good chapter does one job. It opens with a reason to keep reading, which can be a question, a story or a plain statement of what the chapter will deliver. It then delivers that one thing, with examples the reader can picture, and it ends by handing the reader to the next chapter. Chapters that try to do three jobs at once leave the reader unsure what they have just read. If you cannot say what a chapter does in one sentence, split it or cut it.

Examples carry more weight than advice. Instead of telling the reader to be careful, show them a moment when carelessness cost someone something. Instead of listing rules, tell the story of one person who followed them. Your readers will forget your advice and remember your examples, so spend your best sentences on the moments that show rather than tell. Short paragraphs help too: a wall of unbroken text tires the eye, while short paragraphs pull the reader forward.

Exporting your book and turning it into an EPUB

When your chapters are in order, press the download button to save your book as a Markdown file. Markdown is plain text with simple marks for structure: a hash sign marks a heading, blank lines separate paragraphs, and nothing else is needed. Any text editor can open it, and it will still be readable in twenty years, which is more than can be said for most file formats. The copy button gives you the same text to paste anywhere, such as into a document or a notes app.

To turn the file into an EPUB you can read on a phone or an e-reader, use the free Calibre app. Install Calibre, add your Markdown file to its library, then use the convert feature to make an EPUB. Calibre lets you add a cover image, set the author name and tidy the chapter headings before you convert. The whole job takes about five minutes once you have done it once. Your book then opens in any reading app, with proper chapters the reader can jump between.

Proofreading before you share it

Read your book aloud before you share it, even if only to an empty room. Your ears catch clumsy sentences, repeated words and missing words that your eyes skip over, because reading aloud forces you to meet every word in order. Rest a day between writing and proofreading if you can: fresh eyes see what tired eyes miss. Check that chapter titles match what the chapters actually say, that names and dates stay the same throughout, and that the ending still fits the promise you made in chapter one.

Ask one trusted reader to read the draft if you can find one, and ask them two questions: where did you get bored, and where did you get confused. Those two answers tell you almost everything you need to fix. Do not ask whether they liked it; liking is kind but useless. A reader who marks the boring and confusing parts has given you a map for your final revision, and one round of honest fixes is worth ten rounds of polishing sentences that were already fine.

Common mistakes

Every first-time book maker runs into the same traps. They are easy to avoid once you have seen them.

  • Starting with no outline, then stalling at chapter three with nowhere to go.
  • Planning forty chapters and finishing none of them. Five finished chapters are a book; forty empty titles are a list.
  • Writing one giant chapter that tries to say everything. Split it, and your readers will thank you.
  • Skipping the proofread and sharing a draft full of small errors that distract from good ideas.
  • Never exporting a copy, then losing the draft when the browser data is cleared.

Plan the shape, write one chapter at a time, export often, and read the book aloud before you share it. A short book finished and shared is worth far more than a long book that never leaves the draft stage.

Questions people ask

Is my book saved anywhere online?

No. Your draft is saved in your own browser on this device, using local storage. Nothing is sent to a server. That also means the draft lives only in this browser, so export a copy with the download button if you want a backup.

What is a Markdown file?

Markdown is plain text with simple marks for structure: a hash sign makes a heading, blank lines separate paragraphs. Any text editor can open it, and free tools like Calibre can turn it into an EPUB. It keeps your book readable anywhere.

How do I turn my export into an EPUB?

Download the Markdown file, then open it in the free Calibre app and use its convert feature to make an EPUB. The guide above walks through the steps. Calibre also lets you add a cover and author details.

How many chapters can I add?

There is no fixed limit in the planner. Short e-books usually work best with five to twelve chapters, one idea per chapter. If your list grows past twenty, check whether some chapters belong together.

Can I use this on my phone?

Yes. The planner works in your phone browser with big tap-friendly buttons. You can write a chapter on your phone, then open the same browser later to carry on, as long as you use the same device and browser.

Does the planner add a cover or pictures?

No. The planner exports your words as clean Markdown text. It does not add a cover image or pictures. You can add a cover later in Calibre when you convert the file to EPUB.
