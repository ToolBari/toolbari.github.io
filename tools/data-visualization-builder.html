This builder draws simple charts on your own device — your data never leaves the browser and nothing is uploaded. It suits small sets of numbers, up to 20 rows; it is not built for big datasets, and a pie chart with more than 8 slices is hard to read, so the builder warns you when you try.

What this builder does

This builder turns a short list of numbers into a chart you can look at, download and share. Paste your data as rows — one label and one number per line, separated by a comma — pick a chart type, and the chart is drawn on a canvas right here in your browser. You can choose a bar chart, a line chart or a pie chart, give the chart a title, and pick a colour scheme. The download button saves the chart as a picture, and the copy button copies your data back as clean rows.

Everything happens on your device. Your numbers are never sent to a server, never uploaded anywhere, and never stored. If you close the page, your data is gone, so download the chart or copy the data if you want to keep it. That is a deliberate design choice: a chart tool does not need your data, and a tool that does not collect data cannot leak it.

The builder is made for simple charts: a handful of rows, one number per label, one clear message. It is the right tool for monthly spending, weekly temperatures, a small survey, or a household budget. It is not the right tool for thousands of rows, for several numbers per label, or for statistical analysis — for that you need a spreadsheet or a proper data tool, and the guide below says where this builder stops.

How to use it on your phone

Type or paste your data into the big box at the top. Each line is one row: a label, a comma, and a number. For example, "January, 120" is one row with the label January and the value 120. You can also paste rows from a spreadsheet — copy two columns and paste them in, and the builder reads the tab-separated values as rows. Blank lines are ignored, and a line that has no number is reported as an error with its line number, so you can fix it.

Then pick a chart type. Bar charts compare amounts across categories. Line charts show change over time. Pie charts show parts of a whole. Give the chart a title — a good title says what the chart shows, not just what it is about — and pick a colour scheme. Tap Draw chart, and the chart appears below the buttons. On a phone the chart fills the width of the screen and is easy to read without zooming.

The download button saves the chart as a PNG picture, which you can send in a message, put in a document, or print. The copy button copies your data rows, which is useful if you want to paste them into a spreadsheet later. The Load example button fills the box with sample data so you can try each chart type before entering your own numbers.

Which chart for which data

Choose the chart by the question you want answered. If the question is "which is biggest?" — which month sold most, which product costs most — use a bar chart. Bars are easy to compare because the eye judges lengths well, and a bar chart stays readable with up to about 20 bars. If the question is "how did it change over time?" — temperature over a week, sales over a year — use a line chart, because the line shows the direction and the shape of the change at a glance.

If the question is "what share is each part?" — where does the household budget go, how did the votes split — use a pie chart, but only when there are few parts. A pie with three to six slices is clear; a pie with twelve slices is a puzzle, because small slices become slivers that nobody can compare. For many parts, a bar chart sorted from largest to smallest is almost always better than a pie.

One more rule: a chart should have one message. If you find yourself explaining what the chart means for a minute before anyone understands it, the chart is doing too much. Split it into two charts, or remove the rows that do not matter. The best chart is the one a stranger understands in five seconds.

Worked example 1: monthly spending as a bar chart

Sara wants to see her spending over five months. Her data is: January 120, February 180, March 150, April 210, May 190. She pastes those five rows, picks a bar chart, titles it "Monthly spending", and draws it. The bars make the pattern obvious: spending rose from January to April, with a small dip in March, and April was the highest month. The numbers are printed on top of each bar, so the exact values are visible without guessing from the axis.

The bar chart starts at zero, which matters: a bar chart that starts at 100 would make April look three times January, which is not true. This builder always starts bar charts at zero, because a bar's length is supposed to match its value, and cutting the bottom off a bar lies about the size. If your values are all close together and you want to show small differences, a line chart is a better choice than a truncated bar chart.

Worked example 2: temperature over a week as a line chart

Tom records the afternoon temperature for a week: Monday 21, Tuesday 23, Wednesday 22, Thursday 26, Friday 28, Saturday 27, Sunday 24. As a line chart, the shape is clear at once: a warm-up through the week, a peak on Friday, and a cooler Sunday. The line connects the points in order, which is what makes it good for time: the eye follows the line and reads the trend without thinking.

A line chart only makes sense when the order of the rows matters. Days of the week, months of the year, years in a row — all of these have a natural order, and the line shows how the value moves through that order. Categories without an order, such as product names or cities, should not be joined by a line, because the line would suggest a connection between neighbours that does not exist. For unordered categories, use bars.

Worked example 3: a household budget as a pie chart

Priya splits her monthly budget into five parts: rent 45, food 25, transport 12, bills 10, other 8. As a pie chart, the shares are visible in one glance: rent is nearly half, food is a quarter, and the rest are small slices. Each slice is labelled with its name and its percentage, so the chart works even in black and white or for someone who cannot tell the colours apart.

The pie works here because there are only five parts and they add up to a whole: every part of the budget is included, and the percentages add to 100. A pie chart of only some of the parts, or of parts that overlap, misleads the reader, because the whole pie implies completeness. If your parts do not add to a whole, do not use a pie — use bars, which make no such promise.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a pie chart with too many slices. Twelve slices means twelve slivers, several too small to label, and a legend nobody reads. Keep pies to eight slices at most, and combine small parts into one "other" slice. The second mistake is leaving labels off: a chart without a title, without numbers on the bars, and without axis labels forces the reader to guess, and different readers guess differently. Label everything.

The third mistake is a misleading scale. A bar chart that does not start at zero exaggerates differences. A line chart with a squashed vertical axis hides real change, and one with a stretched axis turns a small wobble into a drama. The fourth mistake is using a chart for the wrong question: a pie chart for change over time, or a line chart for unordered categories. The fifth is decoration that gets in the way — 3D effects, shadows and patterns that make the chart harder to read while adding nothing. This builder draws flat, plain charts on purpose.

How to make the chart honest

An honest chart shows the data as it is, with nothing hidden and nothing exaggerated. Start bar charts at zero. Show the numbers on the chart, not only in a table somewhere else. Use a title that says what the chart shows, including the unit and the time period: "Monthly spending in 2026" is honest, "Spending" is vague. If a value is an estimate, say so in the title or in a note, and do not present rounded numbers as if they were exact.

Also show the whole picture. A chart of the five best months hides the seven worse ones; a chart of one product hides the rest of the range. If you leave rows out, say which ones and why. And remember that a chart is a claim: anyone who sees it will believe the shape before they read the numbers, so make sure the shape is true. A chart that needs a long explanation to stop misleading people is a chart that should be redrawn.

What this builder cannot do

This builder draws simple charts from up to 20 rows of one number per label. It cannot handle big datasets, several numbers per label, or charts that need two axes. It cannot clean messy data for you: if a row has no number, or a number with a letter in it, the builder reports the error and you fix the row. It cannot choose the right chart for you, though the guide above helps, and it cannot check whether your numbers are true — it draws what you give it, so the honesty of the chart starts with the honesty of the data.

It also cannot save your work online or share a link to your chart. Charts are downloaded as pictures and data is copied as text, and that is the whole sharing story. For a chart that updates itself from live data, or a dashboard with many charts, you need a spreadsheet or a dedicated data tool. This builder is for the quick, simple chart: paste, draw, download, done.

Questions people ask

Which chart should I use?

Use a bar chart to compare amounts across categories, a line chart to show change over time, and a pie chart to show parts of a whole with few parts. If you are unsure, a bar chart is the safest choice: it is easy to read, it works for most comparisons, and it stays honest when it starts at zero.

How many rows can I paste?

Up to 20 rows. Bar and line charts stay readable up to 20 rows, but pie charts get hard to read above 8 slices, so the builder warns you when a pie has more than 8. For bigger datasets, use a spreadsheet, which is built for large tables of numbers.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. The chart is drawn on your own device, in your browser, and your data never leaves it. Nothing is sent to a server, nothing is stored, and closing the page deletes your data. Download the chart or copy the data if you want to keep it.

Can I download the chart?

Yes. The Download button saves the chart as a PNG picture, which you can send in a message, put in a document, or print. The picture includes the title and the labels, so it makes sense on its own without the page around it.

Why does my pie chart look wrong?

The usual reasons are too many slices, negative values, or parts that do not add up to a whole. A pie needs positive numbers and few parts: combine small parts into one slice called Other, and check that every part of the whole is included. If the parts do not form a whole, use a bar chart instead.

How do I make the chart honest?

Start bar charts at zero, show the numbers on the chart, and give it a title that says what it shows, including the unit and the time period. Do not leave out rows that change the story, and do not present estimates as exact figures. A chart that needs a long explanation to stop misleading people should be redrawn.
