How name meanings work

Almost every name started as an ordinary word. Some names meant a thing — Rose, Stone, River. Some meant a quality — Grace, Felix (lucky), Ethan (strong). Some were job titles, place names, or the names of saints and heroes. Over hundreds of years, the word became a name, and the original meaning faded into the background. Looking up a name's meaning is a way of hearing what the word said before it became a name.

Meanings are not always tidy. A name can have two meanings in two languages, or a meaning that scholars argue about. Some names have a clear meaning in one language and a different, unrelated meaning in another. When that happens, the finder shows the best-known meaning — the one most dictionaries agree on — and the section below explains what to do when a name has more than one origin.

It helps to know the difference between a meaning and a story. A meaning is what the name's words actually say: Olivia comes from the Latin word for olive tree. A story is what people say about the name afterwards: that Olivias are creative, or that a name brings good luck. This finder gives you the meaning. The stories are up to you.

Meanings also change over time, because languages change. A name that meant one thing a thousand years ago can mean something slightly different today, and the meaning a dictionary gives is usually the oldest one it can trace with confidence. That is why two dictionaries sometimes give two different meanings for the same name: they are tracing different branches of the same word. When that happens, neither dictionary is wrong — the name simply has more than one history. The finder picks the meaning most dictionaries agree on, which is the safest answer to give a child who asks what their name means.

How to use the finder

Type a name into the box and tap Find meaning. The finder shows the name, its meaning, its origin, and whether it is a boy name or a girl name. Underneath, it shows up to five similar names — names from the same origin and gender, or, if there are not five of those, names starting with the same letter. Every name has a Copy button and a Save button, so you can keep the ones you like.

Spelling matters. The finder matches the name exactly, so Liam and Liem are different searches. If you type a name that is not in the database, the finder says so honestly and shows the closest matches — names that start with the same two letters. That usually catches a spelling slip, and it sometimes turns up a name you like better than the one you typed.

The finder holds 372 names, covering many origins: Hebrew, Greek, Latin, Arabic, Sanskrit, Irish, French, Spanish, Italian, Welsh, Norse, Persian, Scottish, English and more. It is not every name in the world — no list is — but it covers the names most parents in English-speaking countries are choosing, and it grows out of standard name dictionaries rather than guesswork.

Use the finder as a last check before you decide. When your shortlist is down to two or three names, look each one up and read the meaning out loud. Sometimes the meaning tips the balance: a name that means light or peace can feel like the right choice for a child born in a hard year, and a name whose meaning you dislike is easy to drop at this stage. The meaning will not decide the name for you, but it is one more piece of information, and it costs nothing to check.

Worked example: Liam

Type Liam and the finder shows: Liam — resolute protector (Irish), a boy name. Liam is a short form of William, and it has been the most popular boy name in the United States for several years running. It is short, strong, and easy to spell in any language that uses the Latin alphabet.

The similar names for Liam are all Irish boy names: Brian, Cian, Colin, Connor, Cormac. If you like the sound of Liam but want something less common, any of these five keeps the Irish feel with a different name. Cian and Cormac are the least common of the five, and both are easy to say once you have heard them once.

Worked example: Olivia

Type Olivia and the finder shows: Olivia — olive tree (Latin), a girl name. Olivia is the most popular girl name in the current trending lists for 2026, and it has held the top spot for several years. It comes from the Latin word for the olive tree, a symbol of peace in many cultures.

The similar names for Olivia are all Latin girl names: Aurelia, Aurora, Ava, Beatrice, Camellia. Aurora means dawn and is one of the fastest-rising names of 2026. Aurelia means golden and is far less common than Olivia, which makes it a good choice if you love the sound of Olivia but want a name your daughter is less likely to share with a classmate.

Type Mateo and the finder shows: Mateo — gift of God (Spanish), a boy name. Mateo is the Spanish form of Matthew, and it is one of the fastest-rising boy names of recent years. It works in both Spanish and English, which makes it a natural choice for families who speak both languages.

Names with more than one origin

Some names belong to more than one language, and their meaning changes with the language. A name like Amara means eternal in Igbo and everlasting in Sanskrit. A name like Maya has a different story in Hebrew, in Sanskrit, and in Greek. When a name has more than one origin, the finder shows the best-known one — the origin most dictionaries list first — and the meaning that goes with it.

If your family comes from a particular language, it is worth checking the name in that language too. A name that means one thing in English may mean something else in Arabic, Hindi, or Turkish, and a child who grows up hearing both will carry both meanings. A quick check in a dictionary for your family's language is the honest way to settle it.

A few names in the database are marked origin uncertain. That means the name's etymology is genuinely not settled — scholars disagree, or the name's history is lost. Persephone is one example: the name is ancient Greek, but what the Greek words meant is still debated. Where a meaning is uncertain, this finder says so plainly instead of inventing a meaning that sounds good.

If your family has an older relative who knows the language a name comes from, ask them. A grandmother who speaks the language will often know more about a name than any dictionary — not just what it means, but how it sounds, what it suggests, and whether it carries a memory. That kind of knowledge is not in any database, and it is worth more than a one-line gloss. The finder can tell you the dictionary meaning; your family can tell you what the name means to you.

When a name is not in the list

The database holds 372 names, which is a lot, but it is not every name. If you type a name and the finder does not have it, it says so plainly and shows the closest matches. Type Liem, for example — a common misspelling of Liam — and the finder shows the closest matches: Liam, Lila, Lily. Liam is the name you probably meant, and the other two are names that start with the same letters.

A name can be missing for three reasons. It may be a spelling the database does not carry — the database uses the most common spelling of each name. It may be a name from a language the database does not cover yet. Or it may be a genuinely new name, invented in the last few years, that has not reached the dictionaries yet. None of those makes the name a bad name. It only means the finder cannot tell you its meaning.

If the name you love is not in the list, the honest next step is a name dictionary or an etymology book for your family's language. Look for a source that gives the name's history, not just a one-line gloss. And be careful with websites that give a meaning for every name without ever saying they do not know — a source that never admits uncertainty is a source that invents answers.

New names deserve a word of their own. Parents invent names every year — by combining two names, by changing a spelling, or by turning a word into a name. A new name has no dictionary meaning yet, because no dictionary has caught up with it. That does not make it a bad name. It means the name's meaning is whatever you give it, and that is a different kind of gift: a name whose story starts with your child.
