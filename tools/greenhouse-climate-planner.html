What this tool does and who it is for

A greenhouse climate planner helps you decide what conditions to aim for inside your greenhouse, and what to do each day to hold them. You enter the size of your greenhouse, the crop you are growing, and the season. The page gives you general temperature and humidity targets for that crop, guidance on when to ventilate and when to shade, and a short daily checklist. It is a plan, not a sensor: it tells you what to aim for, and you check the real conditions yourself.

This page is made for home growers and small growers with one greenhouse, a polytunnel, or a cold frame that is big enough to walk into. If you have ever opened a greenhouse on a hot day to find wilted plants, or found mould on a damp morning, you already know why climate planning matters. The planner is for the grower who wants fewer surprises and healthier plants.

The planner covers six crop groups: tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, lettuce, herbs, and a general group for everything else. Each group has a temperature range and a humidity range, labelled as general reference values. The season changes the advice: summer is about cooling and shading, winter is about holding heat, and spring and autumn are about steady air flow and watching for damp.

Nothing here is connected to your greenhouse. The page cannot read a thermometer, cannot open a vent, and cannot switch a heater on. It gives you a plan to follow and a checklist to tick off, and the doing is yours. That is honest, and it is also enough: most greenhouse problems come from not having a plan, not from lacking equipment.

How to use it, step by step

Start by entering the size of your greenhouse: the length and the width. The page works out the floor area, which helps you judge how much air you are managing and how many plants the space can hold comfortably. Then pick your crop group and the season, and press Build my climate plan.

Read the plan from the top. First come the temperature targets: a day range and a night range for your crop. Then the humidity range. Then the ventilation and shading guidance for your season, and the daily checklist. Each item on the checklist has a tick box, so you can work through the day's jobs and see what is left.

Use the plan as a daily habit, not a one-time read. Check the temperature in the morning and again in the hottest part of the day. Open vents when the day range is reached, shade when the sun is strong, and close up before the night temperature falls. Tick the checklist as you go, and the page remembers your ticks.

  • Measure the greenhouse once and write the size down, so you enter the same numbers every time
  • Pick the crop group that matches most of what you grow, and read the general group for anything unusual
  • Rebuild the plan when the season changes, because summer advice and winter advice are opposites
  • Keep a cheap thermometer and humidity gauge in the greenhouse, and trust it over any plan

What the output means

The day temperature range is the range to aim for while the sun is up. Most warm-season crops grow best in the low to mid twenties during the day, and most cool-season crops prefer the high teens. If the temperature climbs above the range, the plants stop growing well and start losing water faster than their roots can replace it. That is when ventilation and shading earn their place.

The night temperature range is usually lower, and that is normal: most plants rest at night and do not need day heat. What matters at night is avoiding a sharp drop. A night that is too cold slows growth and can damage tender crops, while a night that stays warm and damp invites mould. The range gives you a floor to hold, not a target to chase.

The humidity range is about balance. Too dry, and plants lose water through their leaves faster than they can take it up. Too damp, and fungal diseases spread, especially where air does not move. The range in the plan is a middle ground for the crop group, and the checklist includes the small habits, like watering in the morning and spacing plants, that hold it there.

The ventilation and shading guidance tells you what to do when conditions move. Ventilation is your main tool for cooling and for drying the air. Shading cuts the heat coming in. Heating is the last resort, used only when the night temperature cannot be held any other way. The plan puts these in order so you reach for the cheap tools first.

A worked example

Take a home grower with a greenhouse 4 metres long and 3 metres wide, growing tomatoes in summer. The floor area is 4 times 3, which is 12 square metres. The grower picks tomatoes and summer, and presses Build my climate plan.

The plan gives a day temperature range of 20 to 26 degrees and a night range of 15 to 18 degrees, with humidity of 60 to 70 percent. Those are general reference ranges for tomatoes, labelled as such. On a hot summer day the greenhouse can pass 30 degrees by mid-morning, so the plan's ventilation advice is to open vents early, before the heat builds, and to shade the roof when the sun is strongest.

The daily checklist for this grower reads: check the temperature in the morning, open vents when the day range is reached, water in the morning rather than the evening, check for wilted leaves in the afternoon, shade the roof at midday if the sun is strong, and close vents before the night temperature falls. Six jobs, each small, each taking a minute or two.

In winter, the same greenhouse with the same crop gets a different plan. The targets stay similar, because tomatoes still want the same temperatures, but the advice changes: the problem is no longer too much heat, it is too little. The plan suggests closing vents earlier, using bubble wrap or fleece on cold nights, and watering sparingly, because damp cold air is the main risk in winter.

Tips for a good result

Ventilate early. The most common greenhouse mistake is waiting until the greenhouse is already hot before opening vents. By then the plants have already lost water they cannot get back. Open the vents when the temperature reaches the low end of the day range, not when it reaches the top.

Water in the morning. Watering in the evening leaves the air damp overnight, which is when most fungal problems start. Morning watering gives the leaves and the soil time to dry during the day, and the plants have water when they need it most, in the heat of the afternoon.

Give plants space. A crowded greenhouse traps damp air between the leaves, and damp still air is where disease starts. Fewer plants with room to breathe will usually outproduce a crowded house, and they are far easier to check, water and harvest.

  • Put the thermometer at plant height, not near the roof, because that is where the plants live
  • Open vents on both sides when you can, so air moves through instead of sitting still
  • Shade the roof, not the whole greenhouse, so light still reaches the plants
  • Check the temperature at the same times each day, so you learn how your greenhouse behaves

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This planner cannot measure anything. It has no sensor, no connection to your greenhouse, and no live readings. The targets it gives are general reference ranges for a crop group, and your variety, your soil and your local weather may want something a little different. Always check the real temperature and humidity with a gauge, and trust the gauge.

It cannot promise a result. A greenhouse held in the right ranges will usually grow healthy plants, but pests, diseases, poor soil and bad seed can still spoil a crop. Climate is one part of growing, and the planner covers only that part.

It cannot choose your equipment for you. Whether you need a fan, a heater, shading cloth or automatic vents depends on your greenhouse, your budget and your climate. The plan tells you what conditions to aim for; the choice of how to hold them is yours, and a local grower or garden centre can help with the specifics.

All temperature and humidity targets here are general reference ranges, not guarantees for a specific crop or climate. Every variety and every greenhouse behaves a little differently. Never trust a number on a screen over what your plants are telling you, and never rely on this page for live sensor readings, because it has none.

Questions people ask

What temperature should a greenhouse be in summer?

Most warm-season crops grow best with days in the low to mid twenties and nights in the mid teens, as general reference ranges. In summer the main job is keeping the greenhouse from going above that range: ventilate early, shade the roof when the sun is strong, and water in the morning. Check with a thermometer at plant height.

How do I control humidity in a small greenhouse?

Ventilate, water in the morning, and give plants space so air moves between them. Open vents let damp air out and drier air in. Watering in the evening leaves the air damp overnight, which invites mould. A small humidity gauge tells you where you stand, and the checklist on this page covers the daily habits.

When should I open vents and when should I shade?

Open vents when the temperature reaches the low end of the day range for your crop, not when it is already hot. Shade the roof when the sun is strong and ventilation alone cannot hold the temperature down. In the evening, close vents before the night temperature falls, so the greenhouse holds its warmth.

Can this planner work for any crop?

It covers six crop groups: tomatoes, peppers, cucumbers, lettuce, herbs, and a general group for everything else. The general group gives middle-of-the-road ranges that suit most common greenhouse crops. For an unusual crop, use the general group and check the plant's needs with a local grower or a gardening reference.

What is the cheapest way to heat a small greenhouse?

The cheapest heat is the heat you keep: close vents early, seal draughts, and cover plants with fleece or bubble wrap on cold nights. Water stored in containers inside the greenhouse soaks up heat by day and releases it at night. A heater is the last resort, used only when these free methods cannot hold the night temperature.

How often should I check greenhouse temperature?

At least twice a day: once in the morning, and once in the hottest part of the day. In summer, check again in the afternoon, because a greenhouse can overheat fast. In winter, check on the coldest mornings. Checking at the same times each day teaches you how your greenhouse behaves, which matters more than any single reading.
