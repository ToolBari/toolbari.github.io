What depth of field actually is

When you focus a lens, only one exact distance is truly sharp — everything nearer or farther is, strictly speaking, out of focus. But the eye cannot tell a tiny blur from a sharp point, so a band of distances around the focus point looks sharp in the finished photo. That band is the depth of field. It runs from the near limit to the far limit, and it is not centred on the subject: about a third of it lies in front of the focus point and about two thirds behind it, which is why focusing a little past the nearest thing you want sharp is often the safest choice.

Depth of field is what makes a portrait background melt into a soft blur while the eyes stay sharp, and it is what makes a landscape sharp from the foreground flowers to the far mountains. The photographer controls it with three things: the aperture, the focal length and the distance to the subject. A wide aperture (a small f-number such as f/1.8) gives a shallow depth of field. A long lens gives a shallower depth of field than a wide lens at the same distance. And the closer you stand to your subject, the shallower it gets — which is why macro photography, where the subject is centimetres away, has a depth of field measured in millimetres.

It is worth remembering that depth of field is about what looks sharp, not about what is sharp. The same photo viewed on a phone looks sharper than the same photo printed large on a wall, because the blur spots are smaller on the small screen. That is why the circle of confusion is part of the calculation: it is the agreed size of a blur spot that still counts as sharp, and it depends on the sensor because a smaller sensor's image is enlarged more to reach the same print size.

The maths behind the result

The calculator uses the standard optical formulas, with nothing hidden. First it works out the hyperfocal distance, H, from the focal length f, the f-number N and the circle of confusion c, all in millimetres: H equals f squared divided by (N times c), plus f. For a 50 mm lens at f/2.8 on a full frame sensor, that is 2500 divided by (2.8 times 0.03), plus 50 — about 29,812 millimetres, or about 29.8 metres. The hyperfocal distance is the focus distance at which everything from half that distance to infinity looks sharp, which is why landscape photographers love it.

Then the near limit and the far limit come from the hyperfocal distance and the subject distance s, both in millimetres. The near limit is H times s divided by (H plus (s minus f)). The far limit is H times s divided by (H minus (s minus f)). The total depth of field is simply the far limit minus the near limit. If the bottom of the far-limit sum goes to zero or below, the far limit is infinity — everything beyond the near limit looks sharp, and the calculator says so plainly instead of printing a meaningless number.

The circle of confusion values are the standard ones used across photography: 0.03 mm for full frame, 0.02 mm for APS-C and 0.015 mm for Micro Four Thirds. They come from the old rule that a blur spot is acceptably sharp if it is no bigger than about one fifteen-hundredth of the sensor diagonal, and they are the values every depth of field table and app uses. If you print very large or look very closely, you may want a stricter circle — but for ordinary viewing, these values match what photographers have used for decades.

Aperture, focal length and distance: what changes what

Aperture is the strongest lever you have while shooting. Opening up from f/8 to f/2.8 makes the depth of field roughly three times shallower, because the depth of field is roughly proportional to the f-number. That is why portrait photographers shoot wide open: f/1.8 or f/2.8 turns a busy background into a soft wash of colour. And it is why landscape photographers stop down to f/8 or f/11: the smaller opening deepens the sharp band until it covers the whole scene. Going further, to f/16 or f/22, deepens it a little more but starts to soften the whole image through diffraction, so most lenses are sharpest in the middle of their range.

Focal length works the other way: longer lenses give shallower depth of field at the same distance. An 85 mm portrait lens at f/2.8 and 3 metres gives a depth of field of about 20 centimetres, while a 24 mm wide lens at the same aperture and distance gives several metres. That is one reason wide lenses are forgiving to focus and long lenses are demanding: with a long lens, a small error in focus, or a subject who leans forward a few centimetres, is enough to throw the eyes out of focus.

Distance is the lever people forget. Halving the distance to your subject makes the depth of field roughly four times shallower. That is why a portrait at 3 metres is easy to keep sharp while a close-up at 0.4 metres is not: at macro distances the depth of field can be thinner than a coin, and photographers stack many shots taken at different focus distances to build one sharp image. If your depth of field is too thin, stepping back — or using a shorter lens — is often easier than stopping down.

Worked example

Take a full frame camera with a 50 mm lens at f/2.8, focused on a subject 3 metres away. The circle of confusion for full frame is 0.03 mm. The hyperfocal distance is 50 squared divided by (2.8 times 0.03), plus 50 — that is 2500 divided by 0.084, plus 50, which is 29,762 plus 50, or 29,812 millimetres: about 29.8 metres. The subject distance in millimetres is 3000.

The near limit is the hyperfocal distance times the subject distance, divided by the hyperfocal distance plus the subject distance minus the focal length: 29,812 times 3000, divided by (29,812 plus 3000 minus 50), which is 89,436,000 divided by 32,762 — about 2730 millimetres, or 2.73 metres. The far limit is the same top number divided by (29,812 minus 3000 plus 50), which is 89,436,000 divided by 26,862 — about 3330 millimetres, or 3.33 metres. The total depth of field is 3.33 minus 2.73, which is about 0.60 metres — sixty centimetres of sharpness, with the subject sitting a little past the middle of it.

Now change one thing: stop the lens down to f/8 and work it out again. The hyperfocal distance falls to about 10.5 metres, the near limit moves out to about 2.34 metres and the far limit moves out to about 4.18 metres, giving a total depth of field of about 1.84 metres — about three times as deep. That is the whole story of depth of field in one comparison: the same lens, the same subject, the same distance, and the aperture alone changes the sharp band from sixty centimetres to about 1.8 metres.

The three presets explained

The portrait preset — 85 mm, f/2.8, 3 metres — is the classic head-and-shoulders setup. The depth of field is about 20 centimetres, which is enough to keep both eyes and the tip of the nose sharp while the background dissolves. If you open up to f/1.8 the band thins to about 13 centimetres, and the far eye can go soft if the subject turns their head. If you stop down to f/5.6 it widens to about 40 centimetres, and the background starts to show recognisable shapes. The preset is a starting point: real portraits vary, and the calculator lets you test your own lens and distance before you shoot.

The landscape preset — 24 mm, f/11, 5 metres — is built for deep focus. The near limit sits at about 1.3 metres and the far limit is infinity, so everything from a little over a metre away to the horizon looks sharp. That is the hyperfocal idea in practice: focus at about 5 metres and the sharp band covers the scene. Stopping down further to f/16 would move the near limit a little closer, but diffraction would soften the fine detail, so f/8 to f/11 is where most landscape work lives.

The macro preset — 100 mm, f/8, 0.4 metres — shows the other extreme. The depth of field is only a few millimetres: at this distance, even f/8 cannot save you, and stopping down to f/16 only doubles a very small number. Macro photographers accept this and work with it — they pick the single most important plane, focus on it exactly, and sometimes take many shots at different focus distances and combine them. The preset shows why: the calculator's answer is a fraction of a centimetre, and no aperture makes it comfortable.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is forgetting that depth of field is measured around the focus point, not around the camera. The near and far limits are distances from the camera, and the subject sits inside the band, roughly a third of the way from the near limit. If you focus on the nearest thing you want sharp, you waste a third of the band in front of it; focusing a little beyond the nearest point uses the band better. The old rule — focus a third of the way into the scene — comes from exactly this uneven split.

The second mistake is trusting the numbers more than your eyes. The formulas assume a perfect lens and a standard circle of confusion, but real lenses are softer wide open, focus can drift, and a subject who moves a few centimetres can leave the band. Use the calculator to plan, then check the result on the back of the camera, zoomed in, before you move on. The numbers tell you what to expect; the screen tells you what you got.

The third mistake is chasing the deepest possible depth of field. Stopping down to f/22 does deepen the band, but diffraction — light bending around the small opening — softens the whole image, and a long exposure can add blur from camera shake or a moving subject. A sharp photo at f/8 beats a soft photo at f/22, even if the f/22 version has deeper numbers. Depth of field is a tool for choosing what is sharp, not a contest for the biggest number.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot measure your real depth of field — it computes it from the standard formulas, assuming a perfect lens, accurate focus and the standard circle of confusion for your sensor. It does not know your lens's true focal length (many zoom lenses are shorter than marked at close focus), it does not know how accurately you focused, and it does not know how large you will view the photo. A print on a wall is judged more strictly than a photo on a phone, and the same numbers can look sharp on one and soft on the other.

It also cannot tell you what depth of field to choose. A shallow band is right for a portrait and wrong for a landscape, and the choice is yours — the calculator only shows the consequence of each setting. Use it to plan a shot, to compare lenses before you buy, or to understand why a photo came out the way it did. The honest number is the starting point, and your eye is the finish line.

Questions people ask

What is depth of field?

Depth of field is the band of distances in a photo that look sharp, running from the near limit to the far limit around your focus point. Only one exact distance is truly sharp, but the eye cannot tell a tiny blur from a sharp point, so a whole band looks sharp. About a third of the band lies in front of the focus point and about two thirds behind it.

Why is my background blurry at f/1.8 but sharp at f/11?

A wide aperture, such as f/1.8, gives a shallow depth of field, so only a thin band around your subject looks sharp and the background melts into blur. A narrow aperture, such as f/11, deepens the band, so the background comes into focus too. Depth of field is roughly proportional to the f-number, so f/11 is roughly six times deeper than f/1.8.

What is the hyperfocal distance?

The hyperfocal distance is the focus distance that gives the greatest depth of field: focus there and everything from half that distance to infinity looks sharp. For a 50 mm lens at f/2.8 on a full frame camera it is about 29.8 metres. Landscape photographers use it to get a whole scene sharp, from the foreground to the horizon.

Does sensor size change depth of field?

Yes, through the circle of confusion. Smaller sensors use a smaller circle of confusion — 0.02 mm for APS-C and 0.015 mm for Micro Four Thirds, against 0.03 mm for full frame — so the same lens and aperture give a deeper depth of field on a smaller sensor. The calculator shows the circle it uses for each sensor size.

Why does the far limit say infinity?

When the far-limit calculation reaches zero or below, it means everything beyond the near limit looks sharp, all the way to the horizon. That happens with wide lenses, small apertures and distant subjects — the classic landscape case. The total depth of field is then open-ended, so the calculator reports the near limit and infinity instead of a made-up number.
