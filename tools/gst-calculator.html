How to use the GST calculator

The result shows the base amount, the GST amount, the split, and the total — and the Copy button copies the whole breakdown as plain text, ready to paste into an invoice, a message, or a notebook.

The rate list holds the current rates: 0 percent for Nil or exempt goods, 5 percent, 18 percent, and 40 percent for demerit and luxury goods. If you are working with an old bill from before the September 2025 reform, or with a special rate, pick Custom rate and type the rate yourself — 12 percent and 28 percent still work there, because old bills still exist even though the rates no longer apply to new sales.

The sale type matters because it decides how the tax is split. Within one state, the GST is divided equally between the central government and the state government — half CGST, half SGST. Between two states, the whole amount goes as IGST. The tax total is the same either way; only the split changes. If you are not sure which one applies, the rule is simple: if the buyer's state and the seller's state are the same, it is within-state; if they differ, it is between-states.

What GST is, in plain words

GST — Goods and Services Tax — is the single indirect tax India has used since 1 July 2017. Before GST, a product could carry several taxes at once: excise duty, VAT, service tax, octroi, and more, each collected by a different authority and each adding its own paperwork. GST replaced that pile with one tax, charged at each stage of a sale, with businesses able to claim back the GST they paid on their own purchases. The final customer pays the tax; the businesses in the middle pass it along.

That pass-it-along design is the reason GST has three names for what is really one tax. When buyer and seller are in the same state, the tax is split into CGST (central GST) and SGST (state GST), half each. When they are in different states, the whole tax is IGST (integrated GST), collected by the centre and shared with the destination state. A bill for 18 percent GST within a state therefore shows 9 percent CGST and 9 percent SGST; the same bill between states shows 18 percent IGST. The buyer pays the same total either way.

GST is a tax on value added, not on the full price at every step. A shop that buys goods for 1,000 plus 180 GST and sells them for 1,500 plus 270 GST does not hand over 270 to the government — it hands over the difference, 90, because it already paid 180 when it bought the goods. This is called input tax credit, and it is the reason businesses keep their purchase bills so carefully. For a household buyer, none of this matters directly: the price on the shelf already includes GST, and the bill shows the split.

The GST slabs after GST 2.0

On 3 September 2025, the GST Council approved the biggest simplification of GST since it began, and the new rates took effect on 22 September 2025. The reform is called GST 2.0, and its main change is simple: the old four-slab structure — 5, 12, 18 and 28 percent — became two main slabs, 5 and 18 percent, plus a special 40 percent rate for a short list of demerit and luxury goods. The 12 percent and 28 percent slabs were removed.

Most goods that were taxed at 12 percent moved down to 5 percent. Butter, ghee and cheese are everyday examples. Most goods that were taxed at 28 percent moved down to 18 percent — small cars, air conditioners and televisions among them. Some daily essentials moved further: soaps, toothpaste and hair oil went from 18 percent to 5 percent. Thirty-three life-saving medicines went to Nil, and individual health and life insurance premiums went to Nil too.

The 40 percent rate applies to a narrow list: tobacco and tobacco products, pan masala, aerated drinks, and certain luxury goods and large vehicles, along with online money gaming, betting and lotteries. The 40 percent rate on tobacco and pan masala products took effect on 1 February 2026, a little later than the rest of the reform. Gold jewellery and a few other items keep their own special rates, which is why the calculator offers a custom rate.

If you learned the GST slabs before September 2025, the honest thing to do is to forget the old list. A page or a video that still quotes 12 percent and 28 percent as current rates is out of date. This calculator uses the current structure — 0, 5, 18 and 40 percent — and the custom-rate box is there for the old rates only when you are checking an old bill.

Add GST vs remove GST

Adding GST is the easy direction. Take the base price, multiply it by the rate, and add the result. A base price of 1,000 at 18 percent gives 180 of GST and a total of 1,180. Shopkeepers and freelancers do this when they quote a price before tax and need the final bill amount.

Removing GST is the direction that trips people up, because the tempting shortcut is wrong. If a bill shows 1,180 including 18 percent GST, the GST is not 18 percent of 1,180 — that would be 212.40, which is too much. The 1,180 already contains the tax, so the base price is 1,180 divided by 1.18, which is 1,000, and the GST is the difference, 180. The formula is: base equals total divided by one plus the rate, and GST equals total minus base. The calculator does this for you, so you never need to remember which way round the division goes.

You need the remove direction whenever the price you have already includes GST — reading a shop bill, checking a freelancer's invoice, or working out how much of a quoted price is tax. You need the add direction whenever you are building a price from a base amount — quoting for a job, pricing a product, or preparing an invoice. Both directions use the same rate; only the arithmetic changes.

Worked example 1: a shop bill

A shop sells a mixer for 1,000 before tax, at 18 percent GST, to a customer in the same state. Enter 1,000, pick 18 percent, choose Add GST, and choose Within one state. The calculator gives this:

Base amount: ₹1,000.00
GST (18%): ₹180.00
  CGST (9%): ₹90.00
  SGST (9%): ₹90.00
Total: ₹1,180.00

The customer pays 1,180. On the bill, the 180 of GST appears as 90 CGST and 90 SGST, because the sale is within one state. If the same mixer were sold to a customer in another state, the total would still be 1,180, but the bill would show 180 as IGST instead of the two 90s.

Worked example 2: a freelancer invoice

A freelancer receives 11,800 from a client in another state, and the amount already includes 18 percent GST. To find the real earnings, enter 11,800, pick 18 percent, choose Remove GST, and choose Between states. The calculator gives this:

Base amount: ₹10,000.00
GST (18%): ₹1,800.00
  IGST (18%): ₹1,800.00
Total: ₹11,800.00

The freelancer earned 10,000 and collected 1,800 of GST, which goes to the government as IGST. Notice that 18 percent of 11,800 would have given 2,124 — the wrong answer, and 324 too much. Removing GST always means dividing by 1.18, never taking a percentage of the total.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is using the old slabs. If you still think in terms of 12 and 28 percent, you will overcharge some customers and undercharge others, and your returns will not match the current rates. Since 22 September 2025, the working rates are 5 and 18 percent, with 40 percent for the demerit list and Nil for exempt goods. Check any rate you are unsure about before you file — the slab for a product can change, and guessing is how errors start.

The second mistake is forgetting the split. Within a state, the GST must be shown as CGST plus SGST, half each; between states, as IGST. Showing the full 18 percent as CGST, or forgetting SGST entirely, makes the bill wrong even though the total is right. The calculator shows the split every time, so copy it onto the bill as shown.

The third mistake is the remove-GST shortcut described above: taking a percentage of the tax-inclusive total instead of dividing. It overstates the tax every time, and it understates the real price. If you ever find yourself working backwards from a total, divide by one plus the rate — or use the Remove option here and let the calculator do it.

The fourth mistake is rounding too early. If you round the GST on each line of a long invoice and then add the rounded figures, the total can drift by a rupee or two from the GST on the invoice total. Work with full figures and round once, at the end. This calculator rounds each result to the nearest paise, which is the right place to round for a single calculation.

Questions people ask

What are the current GST rates in India?

Since 22 September 2025, the main GST rates are 5 percent and 18 percent, with a special 40 percent rate for demerit and luxury goods and Nil for exempt goods. The 12 percent and 28 percent slabs were removed by the GST 2.0 reform.

What is the difference between CGST, SGST and IGST?

CGST and SGST apply when the buyer and seller are in the same state — the tax is split equally between the centre and the state. IGST applies when they are in different states, and the full tax is charged as IGST. The total tax is the same either way.

How do I remove GST from a price that already includes it?

Divide the total by one plus the rate. For 18 percent GST, divide by 1.18 — that gives the base price, and the GST is the total minus the base. Never take 18 percent of the total, because that overstates the tax.

Can I still calculate GST at 12 percent or 28 percent?

Yes, with the custom rate option — those rates still matter for old bills from before 22 September 2025. For new sales, use the current rates: 5, 18 or 40 percent, or Nil.

What falls under the 40 percent GST rate?

The 40 percent rate covers a short list of demerit and luxury goods and services, including tobacco and tobacco products, pan masala, aerated drinks, certain large vehicles and luxury goods, and online money gaming, betting and lotteries.

Is this calculator tax advice?

No. It calculates GST on the figures you enter, using the GST 2.0 rates in force from 22 September 2025. For filing, registration, or any question about which rate applies to your goods or services, check the official rate schedule or ask a tax professional.
