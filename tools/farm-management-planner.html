What this tool does and who it is for

A farm management planner is a simple record book that lives in your browser. It lets you list each field or plot, note what you planted there, record what it cost to grow, and write down what it yielded at harvest. On top of that, it holds a weekly task list, so the jobs that need doing each day are written down instead of carried in your head. Nothing here is fancy, and that is the point: a small farm does not need a dashboard full of charts, it needs a clear list it can trust.

This page is made for small farms, family farms, market gardens and anyone who grows on a few fields or plots and wants to know where the money and the time are going. If you have ever reached the end of a season unsure whether a crop made money or lost it, this planner is for you. It is also useful for a new grower who is planting a first season and wants to build good record habits from the start.

The planner works in three parts. First, the crop list: one entry per field or plot, with the crop name, the area, the expected cost and the expected yield. Second, the totals: the page adds up your area, your costs and your yields, and works out the cost per unit of yield for each crop, so you can compare crops fairly. Third, the weekly task list: a short list of jobs for the week ahead, with a tick box for each one, so nothing is forgotten.

Everything you enter stays in your own browser, on your own phone or computer. There is no account, no password, no server and no one else who can see your records. The copy button turns your plan into plain text you can paste into a message, save in a note, or print. That is the whole design: your records, in your hands, ready when you need them.

How to use it, step by step

Start with your fields. In the planner above, type a field name, such as North field or Plot behind the house, then the crop you planted there, the area, the expected cost and the expected yield. Use whatever units you normally use, but use the same units for every entry, so the totals make sense. Press Add crop, and the entry appears in your list with its own cost per unit. Repeat for each field or plot.

Next, build your weekly task list. Type a job, such as water the tomatoes or order seed for next month, pick the day it belongs to, and press Add task. The job appears in the list with a tick box. Tick a job off when it is done. The list keeps its ticks when you close the page and come back, because it is saved in your browser.

Use the copy button to take your plan with you. It writes out your crops, your totals and your tasks as plain text, ready to paste into a message or a note. If you want a paper copy, paste the text into any document and print it. The clear button removes everything you have saved, so only use it when you are ready to start a fresh season.

  • Enter one row per field or plot, not one row per crop type, so a crop grown in two places stays separate
  • Use the same area unit and the same money unit for every entry, so the totals are honest
  • Update the expected cost and yield when the real numbers come in, so the planner reflects the season as it really went
  • Add tasks at the start of the week and tick them off as you go, rather than trying to remember them all at once

What the output means

The totals row is the heart of the planner. Total area tells you how much land your plan covers. Total cost tells you what the season is expected to cost across all your entries. Total yield tells you what you expect to harvest. These three numbers together give you the shape of the season at a glance, and they are only as good as the numbers you entered, so keep them honest.

The cost per unit of yield is the most useful number for comparing crops. It is worked out by dividing the cost of one entry by the yield of that entry. A lower cost per unit means that crop produced each unit more cheaply. If one crop costs 0.33 per unit and another costs 0.39 per unit, the first one used your money more efficiently, even if the second one brought in a larger total harvest. This is the number to look at when you decide what to plant more of next season.

The task list shows how many jobs are done and how many are left. That small count matters more than it looks: a week where most jobs are ticked off is a week the farm is keeping up, and a week where jobs keep rolling over is a sign that the list is too long or that something is taking longer than planned. Use the count to keep the list realistic.

Remember that all of these numbers are your own records, not market data. The planner does not know what a crop sells for, and it does not know what a fair price is. The cost per unit tells you what growing cost you, not what you will earn. To work out profit, you still need the selling price from your market, which only you can add.

A worked example

Take a small farm with two fields. The North field is 2 units of area, planted with wheat. The grower expects it to cost 400 and yield 1200 units of wheat. The South field is 1.5 units of area, planted with potatoes, expected to cost 350 and yield 900 units of potatoes. Both entries go into the planner, one row each.

The totals are simple to check by hand. Total area is 2 plus 1.5, which is 3.5 units. Total cost is 400 plus 350, which is 750. Total yield is 1200 plus 900, which is 2100 units. If your own totals do not match what you expect, check that every entry uses the same units, because a mixed-up unit is the most common reason for a strange total.

Now the cost per unit. For the wheat, 400 divided by 1200 is 0.33 per unit. For the potatoes, 350 divided by 900 is 0.39 per unit. The wheat produced each unit more cheaply, even though the potatoes brought in a higher yield per unit of area. That does not mean wheat is the better crop: potatoes may sell for more per unit, and the planner cannot tell you that. It means the wheat cost less to grow per unit, which is one piece of the decision.

The weekly task list for this farm might hold five jobs: water the potatoes on Monday, check the wheat for weeds on Tuesday, mend the fence on Wednesday, order seed for next month on Thursday, and weigh and record the first potato harvest on Saturday. As each job is done, it gets ticked. By Saturday, the grower can see at a glance whether the week went to plan.

Tips for a good result

Keep the numbers small and honest. It is better to enter a rough cost you believe than a precise cost you invented. If you do not know the expected yield yet, enter your best guess and update it after harvest. A planner with honest guesses is useful; a planner with made-up precision is not.

Record costs as you spend them, not at the end of the season. A cost written down the day it happens is accurate. A cost remembered three months later is a guess, and guesses usually come out too low. If you buy seed, feed, fuel or tools, add the cost to the right entry on the same day.

Use the task list for the jobs you actually forget. Everyone remembers the big jobs. The task list earns its place with the small ones: the fence that needs mending, the phone call to the supplier, the form that needs sending. If a job is worth remembering, it is worth a line on the list.

  • Review the plan once a week, at the same time each week, so it becomes a habit
  • Compare cost per unit between crops, but never compare cost per unit between different units
  • Keep a paper copy of the plan somewhere safe, in case the browser data is cleared
  • Start next season by clearing the old plan and entering fresh numbers, rather than editing old ones

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This planner cannot tell you what to plant, what a crop will sell for, or what the weather will do. It holds no market prices, no yield forecasts and no weather data, and it never will, because it runs entirely in your browser and does not connect to any service. Any page that claims to know tomorrow's prices is guessing or selling something; this page does neither.

It also cannot keep your records safe for you. Your data lives in this browser only, which means it is lost if the browser data is cleared, if you switch to a different phone, or if the device is lost. Use the copy button regularly and keep the text somewhere safe. The planner is a notebook, not a vault.

Finally, it cannot replace local knowledge. What grows well, what sells, and what a fair price is all depend on your area, your soil and your market. Use this planner to keep your own records straight, and ask your local agriculture office or experienced neighbours for the advice that only local knowledge can give.

Questions people ask

What is a farm management planner?

A farm management planner is a record book for a farm: it lists fields and crops, records costs and yields, and holds a weekly task list. This page is a free planner that runs in your browser, with no account and no signup. It helps a small farm see where money and time are going, and plan the week ahead.

Do I need to sign up to use it?

No. There is no account, no password and no signup. You open the page and start entering your fields and tasks. Your data is saved in your own browser, so it is there when you come back on the same device, and nobody else can see it.

Can it track costs for each crop separately?

Yes. Each field or plot is its own entry, with its own crop, area, cost and yield. The page shows the cost per unit of yield for each entry, and adds up totals across all entries. If you grow the same crop in two fields, enter it twice, once per field, so each field stays separate.

Does it work offline on my phone?

Yes, once the page has loaded. The planner runs entirely in your browser and needs no connection to add crops, build a task list or copy your plan. Your saved entries live in the browser on that phone, so they are there the next time you open the page.

Can I print my weekly task list?

Yes. Press the copy button to turn your plan, including the task list, into plain text, then paste it into any document or note and print it from there. The copied text includes your crops, your totals and your tasks, so the printed page holds the whole plan.

What records should a small farm keep every week?

Keep four things: what was planted and where, what was spent and on what, what was harvested, and what jobs were done. A weekly note of these four, kept honestly, tells you more about your farm at the end of the season than any single number. This planner holds the first three as entries and the fourth as your task list.
