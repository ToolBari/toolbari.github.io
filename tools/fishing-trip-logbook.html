What this logbook does and who it is for

Every angler has a favourite spot and a story about the day the fish would not stop biting — and most anglers forget the details within a month. This logbook keeps the details: each trip recorded with its date, place, weather and catch, so you can see your totals, your best days and the patterns that only show up over a season.

It is built for anyone who fishes and wants to remember: a weekend angler, a keen match fisher, or a family that fishes together on holiday. You do not need any special knowledge — just the trip and what you caught.

What it will not do is find fish for you. The logbook records your own trips; it has no live data, no fishing forecast and no connection to anyone else's catches. Its value is in your own history: the patterns in your trips, not anyone else's.

How to use the logbook on your phone or computer

Log one trip at a time. Pick the date, type the place, and choose the weather. Then add the catch: species, size in centimetres and count, tapping Add this catch to the trip for each species you caught. When all the catches for the trip are added, tap Save this trip and it joins the logbook.

A trip with no catch is still worth saving. Tap Save this trip with no catches added, and the blank day is recorded alongside the good ones — blank days teach you as much as good ones, because they show which conditions do not work.

Your trips are saved only in this browser, so they are still here when you come back. Nothing is uploaded or shared. The summary shows your total trips, total fish and species count, your best trip so far and your totals by species. The copy button copies the whole logbook as plain text, and each trip has a delete button.

If you log a trip by mistake, delete it with the button beside it — the totals update straight away. The Clear all trips button removes the whole logbook at once, so use it only when you mean to start again.

What to record on every trip

The date and the place are the two things every entry needs, because without them the rest of the record is useless. A catch with no date cannot be compared with another, and a catch with no place cannot be repeated. Type the place name clearly enough that you will recognise it in a year — Lakeview Pier, not the lake.

Weather matters more than most anglers think. Fish feed differently in sun, cloud, rain and wind, and the pattern only shows up when the weather is recorded every time. The logbook has a weather choice for each trip — pick the one that best describes the day, and the patterns will emerge over a season.

For the catch, record the species, the size of the biggest fish of that species and how many you caught. Size in centimetres is the simplest measure, and the biggest fish is the one worth remembering. If you catch nothing, record that too — a blank trip is data, not a failure.

If you want more detail, keep a separate note of the bait and tackle that worked. The logbook does not have a box for it, but a line in the place name or a note kept alongside the logbook is enough — the bait that produced on one trip is worth trying again on the next, and the bait that failed is worth avoiding.

Reading your own patterns

After a season of trips, the logbook starts to answer questions. Which weather gives you the best days? Which spot produces most often? Which species do you catch most, and where is the biggest one? The totals by species and the best-trip summary answer the easy questions at a glance.

Look for the patterns that repeat. If cloudy days at one spot keep producing, that is a pattern worth following. If a spot that was good in spring goes quiet in summer, that is a pattern too — fish move with the season, the water temperature and the food available.

Be careful not to read too much into a few trips. Three good days in a row can be luck, and one bad day can be bad luck. Patterns need a season of entries to mean anything, and even then the fish do not read the logbook. Use the patterns as a guide for where to try next — not as a promise of what will happen.

Compare spots and seasons, not just trips. A spot that is good in spring may be poor in summer, and a species that bites at dawn may not bite at noon. The logbook shows the totals, but the thinking is yours: look at which trips produced, which did not, and what they had in common. The answer is often simpler than you expect.

A worked example: a season of trips

Imagine a season of eight trips. Four were at Lakeview Pier and four at Mill Pond. Lakeview produced 22 fish across the four trips, with the best day — 9 fish on a cloudy day in May — standing out clearly. Mill Pond produced 9 fish in four trips, with two blank days.

The species totals tell their own story: 18 bass, 9 perch and 4 roach, with the biggest bass at 41 centimetres caught at Lakeview on that cloudy May day. The weather pattern is clear too: the three cloudy trips averaged 7 fish, while the three sunny trips averaged 2.

None of that guarantees the next trip. But it tells the angler where to start: Lakeview, on a cloudy day, fishing for bass. That is what a logbook is for — not predicting the future, but remembering the past well enough to make a better guess.

Common mistakes that weaken a logbook

The first mistake is recording the catch but not the conditions. A list of fish with no dates, places or weather is a trophy list, not a logbook — it cannot tell you where to fish next, because it does not say where the fish were caught or what the day was like.

The second mistake is no place name. The lake is not a place — it is a dozen places, and the fish were in one of them. Name the spot clearly enough to find it again: the pier, the reeds by the car park, the bend below the bridge.

The third mistake is only logging the good days. A logbook with only good days is a highlight reel, and it teaches nothing about what does not work. Log the blank days too — they are the entries that show which spots and conditions to avoid, and a logbook that only remembers success is a logbook that lies.

What the logbook cannot do

It cannot tell you where the fish are today. Fish move, weather changes, and a spot that produced last week can be empty this week. The logbook shows where the fish were, not where they are — use it to choose where to try, not to expect a catch.

It cannot check the rules for you. Fishing licences, catch limits, closed seasons and protected species vary by place and by season, and they change. Always check the local rules before you fish, and respect them — the logbook records your trips, but it does not make them legal.

It cannot replace the habit of looking. The best anglers watch the water, the weather and the birds as much as they watch their logbook. Use the logbook to remember, and use your eyes to fish — the two together catch more than either alone.

It cannot share your logbook with anyone else. The logbook lives only in your browser, and the copy button is the way to share it — paste the text into a message to a fishing friend, or keep it in a document. Sharing your best spots is your choice, and some anglers keep their best spots to themselves.

Questions people ask

What should I record on a fishing trip?

The date, the place, the weather, and the catch: species, size in centimetres and count. A trip with no catch is worth recording too — blank days show which conditions do not work.

How do I find my best fishing days?

The logbook shows your best trip so far and your totals by species. After a season of entries, compare trips by weather and place to see which conditions give you the best days.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your trips are saved only in this browser using local storage. Nothing is uploaded, synced or shared, and clearing your browser data will remove them — so copy the logbook if you want a lasting record.

Can I log more than one catch per trip?

Yes. Add each species separately with its size and count — tap Add this catch to the trip for each one, then save the trip. The logbook totals all the catches for the trip together.

What if I catch nothing?

Save the trip anyway, with no catches added. A blank day is useful data: it shows which spots and conditions do not produce, and a logbook that only remembers good days teaches nothing.

Can I export my logbook?

The copy button copies the whole logbook as plain text — trips, catches, totals and best trip — ready to paste into a message or a document. There is no file download; the logbook lives in your browser.
