The exposure triangle in plain words

Every photo is an amount of light collected over an amount of time at an amount of sensitivity. The aperture is the size of the opening in the lens: a wide opening (a small f-number such as f/2.8) lets in a lot of light, and a narrow opening (a large f-number such as f/16) lets in a little. The shutter speed is how long the opening stays open: a long exposure (say, a second) collects a lot of light, and a short one (say, 1/1000 second) collects a little. The ISO is how sensitive the sensor is: a high ISO needs less light, and a low ISO needs more.

Those three settings trade against each other, and that trade is the exposure triangle. If you open the aperture by one stop — doubling the light — you must halve the light somewhere else to keep the same exposure: either halve the shutter time or halve the ISO. If you want a faster shutter to freeze action, you must open the aperture or raise the ISO to make up the lost light. There is no free lunch in exposure: every gain in one corner costs something in another, and the art of photography is choosing which corner pays.

Each corner has a side effect beyond brightness, and that side effect is usually why you change it. Aperture controls depth of field: a wide aperture blurs the background, a narrow one keeps it sharp. Shutter speed controls motion: a fast shutter freezes action, a slow one blurs it. ISO controls noise: a low ISO gives a clean image, a high ISO gives a grainy one. So the triangle is not only about brightness — it is about choosing which side effect you want and paying for it with the other two settings.

What EV means

Exposure value, EV, is a single number that describes how bright a combination of settings is. At ISO 100, EV is the base-2 logarithm of the aperture squared divided by the shutter time in seconds: EV equals log2 of (N squared over t). A bright daylight scene needs a high EV, around 15; a dim indoor scene needs a low EV, around 5 or 6. Two combinations with the same EV give the same exposure, which is why EV is the anchor this calculator works from.

ISO shifts the scale: each doubling of ISO adds one to the EV, because a sensor twice as sensitive needs half the light. The full formula the calculator uses is EV at ISO 100 equals log2 of (N squared over t) minus log2 of (ISO over 100). So f/8 at 1/125 second and ISO 100 gives an EV of about 13.0, and the same aperture and shutter at ISO 200 give an EV of about 12.0 — the same light, counted against a more sensitive sensor.

You do not need to memorise the formula to use EV. Think of it as a brightness score: if two combinations have the same score, they give the same exposure, and the calculator's job is to find the combination with the same score as your starting point. The working shown under the result lists the EV of your current settings and the steps used to solve for the new one, so you can follow the arithmetic.

Worked example

Start with f/8, 1/125 second and ISO 100 — a typical daylight setting. The EV is log2 of (8 squared divided by 0.008), which is log2 of (64 divided by 0.008), which is log2 of 8000 — about 13.0. Now suppose you want a softer background, so you open the aperture to f/2.8 and let the shutter speed adjust, keeping ISO locked at 100. The new shutter time is the new aperture squared divided by (2 to the power of the EV, times ISO over 100): 2.8 squared is 7.84, and 2 to the power of 13.0 is about 8000, so the shutter time is 7.84 divided by 8000 — about 0.00098 seconds, or about 1/1000 second.

The nearest standard shutter speed is 1/1000 second, which is what you would dial in. The exposure is the same as before, but the picture is different: the wider aperture gives a shallower depth of field, so the background is softer, and the faster shutter freezes motion better. That is the whole trade in one example — the same brightness, bought with a different combination, chosen for its side effects.

Try the reverse: from the same starting point, suppose you want a slower shutter to blur moving water, say 1/15 second, and you let the aperture adjust with ISO locked. The aperture is the square root of (shutter time times 2 to the power of EV times ISO over 100): the square root of (0.0667 times 8000), which is the square root of 533, or about f/23. That is beyond most lenses, which stop at f/22 — and the calculator will tell you the exact value and the nearest standard one, so you can see when a combination is not practical and pick a different trade, such as lowering the ISO first.

The three scenarios explained

Sports freeze — f/2.8, 1/1000 second, ISO 800 — is built for action. The fast shutter stops a running player or a bird in flight, the wide aperture gathers enough light for that short exposure, and the moderately high ISO makes up the rest. If the light drops, the first thing to give is usually the ISO: raising it to 1600 or 3200 keeps the shutter fast, at the cost of more noise. Lowering the shutter to 1/500 second is the alternative, but a fast-moving subject may start to blur.

Night — f/2, 1/30 second, ISO 3200 — is built for low light. The aperture is wide open, the shutter is as slow as you can hand-hold without blur, and the ISO is high. From here the trades are tight: a slower shutter needs a tripod, a wider aperture may not exist on your lens, and a higher ISO adds noise. The calculator shows exactly what each trade costs, so you can pick the least painful one for the shot you want.

Portrait blur — f/1.8, 1/250 second, ISO 100 — is built for a soft background in good light. The wide aperture melts the background, the shutter is fast enough to freeze a person who is mostly still, and the low ISO keeps the image clean. In bright sun the shutter may need to go faster still to avoid overexposure, and the calculator will show that: change the ISO to 100 and let the shutter adjust, and you can see how fast it needs to be.

Stops: the language photographers use

Photographers talk about stops: one stop is a doubling or halving of light. Opening the aperture one stop (say, from f/5.6 to f/4) doubles the light. Halving the shutter time (say, from 1/125 to 1/250 second) halves the light. Doubling the ISO (say, from 200 to 400) doubles the sensitivity, which is the same as doubling the light. The standard aperture scale — f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16, f/22 — is built in stops: each step doubles or halves the light, which is why the numbers look uneven (each is the previous one times the square root of two).

Stops are useful because they make the triangle easy to think about without a calculator. If you open the aperture two stops, you must close the shutter two stops, or drop the ISO two stops, or one stop of each. Most cameras also offer third-stop steps between the full stops, which is why the calculator's exact answer and its nearest standard answer can differ slightly — the nearest standard value is the nearest full stop, and your camera may offer a third-stop step closer still.

The worked example above is three stops of aperture (f/8 to f/2.8 is about three stops) paid for by three stops of shutter (1/125 to 1/1000 is three stops). Counting stops is a habit worth building: it turns exposure from arithmetic into instinct, and the calculator is there for the times when instinct needs checking.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is changing one setting and forgetting to compensate. Opening the aperture for a softer background without speeding up the shutter overexposes the photo — the picture comes out too bright, and the highlights wash out. The triangle only works when the trade is made: every change needs its matching change, and this calculator exists to find it.

The second mistake is paying with the wrong corner. Raising the ISO to keep a fast shutter in low light is often right, but raising it when you could have opened the aperture instead adds noise for nothing. Opening the aperture when you could have slowed the shutter instead throws away depth of field for nothing. Before you change a setting, ask which side effect you can afford: noise, blur or a thin band of focus — and pay with the one that hurts least.

The third mistake is trusting the exact answer over the standard one. Cameras only offer standard steps, so an exact answer of 1/970 second is really 1/1000 second on your camera, and an exact aperture of f/6.3 is really f/5.6 or f/8. The difference is far too small to see in the photo. Use the nearest standard value the calculator shows, and treat the exact value as the arithmetic behind it.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot measure the light in your scene — it works from the settings you enter, and it assumes they give the exposure you want. It does not know whether your starting point is correctly exposed, under-exposed or over-exposed; it only finds combinations that match it. If your starting point is wrong, every answer it gives will be equally wrong, so check your exposure on the camera's meter or screen first.

It also cannot account for the quirks of real cameras at the extremes. Very long exposures can suffer from reciprocity failure, where the sensor needs more light than the formula says. Very short exposures and very small apertures can behave slightly differently from the simple equation. And it cannot choose for you: the triangle offers many combinations with the same exposure, and the right one depends on the photo you want. The calculator shows the trade; the choice is yours.

Questions people ask

What is the exposure triangle?

The exposure triangle is the three settings that control a photo's brightness: aperture, shutter speed and ISO. They trade against each other — open the aperture and you must speed up the shutter or lower the ISO to keep the same exposure. Each setting also has a side effect: aperture controls depth of field, shutter speed controls motion blur, and ISO controls noise.

What is EV?

EV, or exposure value, is a single number that describes how bright a combination of settings is. At ISO 100 it is the base-2 logarithm of the aperture squared divided by the shutter time. Two combinations with the same EV give the same exposure, which is why this calculator uses EV as the anchor when it solves for a new setting.

Why does changing aperture change my shutter speed?

Because the total light must stay the same. Opening the aperture lets in more light, so the shutter must stay open for less time to collect the same total. The calculator finds the shutter time that keeps the exposure value unchanged, so the photo comes out equally bright with a different depth of field.

What is a stop?

A stop is a doubling or halving of light. Opening the aperture one stop, from f/5.6 to f/4, doubles the light. Halving the shutter time, from 1/125 to 1/250 second, halves it. Doubling the ISO, from 200 to 400, doubles the sensitivity. The standard aperture scale — f/1.4, f/2, f/2.8, f/4, f/5.6, f/8, f/11, f/16, f/22 — is built in full stops.

Should I raise ISO or open the aperture in low light?

It depends on which side effect you can afford. Opening the aperture costs depth of field — the background goes softer and focus gets harder. Raising the ISO costs image quality — the photo gets noisier. If your subject is still, a slower shutter on a tripod costs nothing but time. Pick the trade that hurts your shot least.

Does this work for any camera?

Yes — the exposure equation is the same for every camera, from a phone to a large-format camera. The standard settings differ a little (phones have fixed apertures, for example), but the maths of aperture, shutter and ISO is universal. The nearest standard value the calculator shows matches the standard steps on most cameras.
