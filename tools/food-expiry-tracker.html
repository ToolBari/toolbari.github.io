Best before vs use by — the difference matters

Two dates appear on food packs, and they mean different things. A use-by date is about safety. After the use-by date, the food may not be safe to eat, even if it looks and smells fine — harmful germs do not always announce themselves. Milk, fresh meat, fish and ready meals usually carry a use-by date, and it should be treated as a line, not a suggestion. Eating meat past its use-by date is a risk not worth taking for the price of a meal.

A best-before date is about quality, not safety. After the best-before date, the food may lose flavour, crunch or colour, but it is often still perfectly safe to eat. Biscuits, pasta, rice and tinned food usually carry a best-before date, and they can be fine for weeks or months past it if they have been stored properly and look and smell normal. Throwing away good food because of a best-before date wastes money and wastes food that someone could have eaten.

The difference matters in both directions. Treating a best-before date as a safety line throws away good food. Treating a use-by date as a quality hint risks illness. Some foods carry no date at all — fresh fruit and vegetables, for example — and for those, your eyes and nose are the only guide. When a pack carries both kinds of guidance, such as "eat within 3 days of opening", that opening date matters as much as the printed date: an opened pack ages faster than a sealed one.

How long common foods really last

Dates on packs are the best guide for the food in front of you, but it helps to know roughly how long things last once they are home. The table below is a rough guide for food stored properly — in a cold fridge, in a freezer that stays frozen, or in a cool, dry cupboard. The date on the pack always wins over this table.

Milk, openedFridgeA few days — follow the use-by date

Cooked rice and leftoversFridgeA day or two — reheat until steaming hot

Frozen meat and vegetablesFreezerMonths — but the quality fades over time

Two things shorten every number in that table: warmth and air. A fridge that is too warm lets food go off faster, and food left uncovered dries out and picks up smells. Keep the fridge cold, keep food covered or sealed, and put shopping away as soon as you get home — an hour in a warm bag counts against every item in it.

Worked example: a weekly fridge check in 5 minutes

Pick one evening a week — Sunday works for most people — and spend five minutes with the tracker. Open the page and look at the "Use first" box. Say it shows three things: yoghurt expiring tomorrow, chicken expiring in two days, and bread expiring in two days. That is the whole plan for the week, written for you.

The yoghurt becomes tomorrow's breakfast. The chicken gets cooked tonight, because two days is close enough to cook now and eat over the next day or two. The bread goes into the freezer, where it will keep for weeks instead of expiring in two days — freezing is the simplest rescue there is, as long as you remember it is there. Then delete what you ate during the week, add the new shopping with its dates, and you are done. Five minutes, once a week, and the bin stays nearly empty.

The habit works because it is small. A weekly check catches food while there is still time to use it. A monthly check finds food that is already gone. And the check before a big shop is worth even more: knowing what is already in the fridge stops you buying a second bottle of milk while the first one is still unopened, which is how most households end up throwing food away.

Storing food so it lasts longer

How you store food matters as much as when you bought it. Keep the fridge cold — food kept just above freezing lasts noticeably longer than food kept in a warm fridge, and a fridge stuffed so full that cold air cannot circulate is warmer than it looks. Put raw meat on the lowest shelf, where any drip cannot fall onto food you will eat without cooking. Keep leftovers in sealed containers, and let hot food cool a little before it goes in — a steaming pot warms the whole fridge.

Use the first-in, first-out rule: new shopping goes behind the old food, so the oldest item is always the one you reach first. It sounds fussy, but it is the single best habit for wasting less, because the food that goes off is almost always the food that got pushed to the back and forgotten. Label anything you freeze with the name and the date — frozen food all looks the same after a month, and a mystery bag at the bottom of the freezer usually ends up in the bin.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is trusting the date over your nose for milk. If milk smells off, it is off, whatever the date on the carton says — a warm journey home or a fridge left open can turn milk early. The date is a guide made for food stored properly; your senses are the check on whether it actually was. The same applies the other way: food a day past its best-before date that looks, smells and tastes fine is usually fine, and throwing it away wastes money.

The second mistake is freezing food on the last day and forgetting it. Freezing does stop the clock, but only if you remember the food is there and eat it within a reasonable time — frozen food still ages, slowly, and a bag nobody remembers is just a slower way of throwing food away. Label it, and put it where you will see it. The third mistake is buying more of what is already expiring: a second pack of something you already have too much of, bought because it was on offer. The offer is only cheap if you eat it.

The fourth mistake is ignoring the "eat within a few days of opening" line on the pack. An opened pack of ham or a opened jar of sauce ages much faster than a sealed one, and the printed date no longer applies once the seal is broken. When you open something, the clock that matters is the one that started at opening, not the one printed on the pack.

What this tracker cannot do

This tracker cannot smell your food or judge whether a particular item is safe to eat. It works from the dates you type in, and the dates are only a guide — food kept badly can go off before its date, and some food is fine just after a best-before date. The final judgment is always yours, using the date, the smell and the look together.

It cannot warn you automatically. There are no notifications and no alarms — you need to open the page to check, which is why the weekly five-minute habit matters more than the tool itself. It cannot read barcodes or dates from a photo, so adding items takes a few seconds each. And because everything is saved on this device only, a new phone starts with an empty list — use the Copy list button to keep a backup in your notes. Within those honest limits, it does one job well: it shows you what to use first, before it is too late.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between best before and use by?

A use-by date is about safety: after it, the food may not be safe to eat even if it looks and smells fine, so treat it as a line — milk, fresh meat, fish and ready meals usually carry one. A best-before date is about quality: after it, the food may lose flavour or crunch but is often still safe — biscuits, pasta and tinned food usually carry one. Throwing away food past its best-before date wastes good food; eating food past its use-by date risks illness.

Where is my data saved?

In your browser on this device, using local storage. Nothing is sent to a server, there is no account and no signup, and nobody else can see your list. The other side of that privacy is that clearing your browser data erases the list, and a new phone starts empty — so use the Copy list button to paste a backup into a note now and then.

Can I track freezer food too?

Yes — choose freezer when you add the item. Frozen food lasts for months, but it still ages slowly, and a bag nobody remembers is just a slower way of throwing food away. Give freezer items a date too — the date you froze them, or a date a few months ahead — and check the freezer list in your weekly five-minute check, just like the fridge.

What if I lose my phone?

The list lives only on that device, so it is lost with the phone — there is no cloud backup and no account to sign into on a new phone. That is the price of a tracker that sends nothing anywhere. The Copy list button is your backup: paste the list into a note or send it to yourself, and you can rebuild the list from it in a few minutes.

How often should I check?

Once a week is enough for most people — a five-minute check, ideally before the weekly shop, so you buy only what you need. Glance at the Use first box when you plan tonight's cooking, and check daily if you cook with fresh meat or fish often, because those go off fastest. A weekly habit catches food while there is still time to use it; a monthly check finds food that is already gone.

Is food safe after the date?

After a use-by date, no — do not risk it, because harmful germs do not always change how food looks or smells. After a best-before date, often yes, if the food looks, smells and tastes normal — the date is about quality, not safety. When in doubt, throw it out: the price of a meal is never worth a day of illness. And trust your senses as well as the date — milk that smells off is off, whatever the carton says.
