Random Team Generator

Split a list of people into fair random teams in one tap, with the option to keep chosen pairs apart or together. Paste the names, pick how many teams you want, and the generator deals them out as evenly as possible.

Teams are dealt round by round, so team sizes differ by at most one person. Apart and together pairs are honoured whenever it is possible — if two requests fight each other, the generator still returns fair teams and says so.

Random means random: the generator does not know who is strong, who is shy, or who played well last time, and it does not try to balance skill. If you need even skill across teams, sort that out yourself after the draw — or re-shuffle until the teams look fair to you.

How to use this generator

Paste your names into the box, one name per line. It does not matter if there are blank lines between names — they are ignored — and if a name appears twice by mistake, the duplicate is dropped, so nobody ends up on two teams. You can paste a list from a message, a note, or a spreadsheet column. Just make sure each person is on their own line and spelled the same way everywhere, because the apart and together boxes match names exactly as you typed them.

Then choose how to split. Pick "Number of teams" and type how many teams you want, or pick "Team size" and type how many people should be on each team. The generator works out the rest. If you ask for 4 teams from 24 people, you get 4 teams of 6. If you ask for teams of 5 from 24 people, you get 4 teams of 5 and one team of 4 — the teams are always as even as they can be, differing by at most one person. Tap "Make my teams" and the teams appear below, each one listed clearly with its members.

The two extra boxes are optional. In "Keep apart", type pairs of people who should not be on the same team, one pair per line, with the two names separated by a comma. In "Keep together", type pairs who should stay on the same team, in the same format. The generator shuffles first, then fixes the teams to honour your pairs whenever it can. The "Shuffle again" button makes a fresh random split with the same names and the same rules. The copy button copies the whole list of teams so you can paste it into a group chat.

Making teams fair

Picking teams by hand feels fair, but it usually is not. When captains take turns choosing, the same confident, well-known people get picked first, and the quiet ones get picked last. Everyone can see the order, and everyone knows what it means. Even when the person picking tries to be fair, they pick the people they know best, the people they played with last time, and the people standing nearest. That is human nature, and it is exactly what a random draw removes.

A random draw has one great advantage: nobody can blame the person who made the teams. The draw is the draw. There is no favouritism to argue about, no quiet suspicion that the organiser fixed it. That matters most with children and with mixed groups where people do not know each other well. A random split also mixes people up in a way that hand-picking never does. The same friends do not always end up together. People meet someone new. For a classroom, a training session or a friendly game, that mixing is half the point.

Random is not the same as balanced. A random draw can put all the fast runners on one team by pure chance, and no random generator can stop that, because it does not know who is fast. If skill balance matters more than randomness — a serious match, a competition with a prize — then random is the wrong tool, and you should seed the teams by hand. But for friendly games, classroom groups, ice-breakers and practice matches, random is fair in the way that matters: everyone had the same chance, and nobody chose.

Worked example: 24 students into 4 teams

A teacher has 24 students and wants 4 teams of 6 for a quiz. She pastes the 24 names, one per line, chooses "Number of teams", types 4, and taps the button. Behind the scenes, the generator shuffles the 24 names into a random order, then deals them out one by one, round by round: the first name goes to Team 1, the second to Team 2, the third to Team 3, the fourth to Team 4, the fifth back to Team 1, and so on. After six rounds, every team has exactly 6 students.

Now suppose two of the students are sisters who distract each other, and the teacher puts them in the "Keep apart" box. The generator shuffles and deals as before, then checks: are the sisters on the same team? If they are, it swaps one of them with a student from another team, and keeps swapping until they are separated or until it has tried many times. The teams stay the same size, because a swap moves one person each way. The same idea works for "Keep together": if two students who should stay together land apart, the generator swaps one of them into the other's team.

The teacher copies the teams and pastes them into the class group chat. The whole job takes less than a minute, and no student can say the teacher picked favourites — the shuffle did the picking. If the teacher wants a different mix next week, she pastes the same names and taps "Shuffle again". The draw is fresh every time, so the same teams are very unlikely to come up twice in a row.

Apart and together: keeping friends and rivals in check

The apart and together boxes solve the two most common team problems. Apart is for people who should not be together: two players who always argue, two friends who talk instead of working, a couple who would rather be rivals for once, or the two strongest players in a friendly game. Together is for the opposite: two beginners who feel braver as a pair, a new member and the friend who brought them, or two people who share one set of equipment.

There are limits, and it is honest to state them. If you ask for too many pairs, the requests can fight each other. Asking to keep A apart from B, B apart from C, and A apart from C, with only 2 teams, is impossible — with 2 teams and 3 people, two of them must share a team. The generator tries its best, and if a request cannot be met, it still returns complete, fair teams with everyone placed exactly once, and says plainly that a request could not be honoured. A tool that silently dropped a name, or placed someone twice, would be worse than a tool that admits a limit.

One more rule: pairs only work when the names match exactly. If the list has "Mohammed" and the apart box has "Mohammad", the generator sees two different people. Copy names from the main list into the pair boxes rather than retyping them, and check the spelling before you tap the button. A pair naming someone who is not on the list is simply ignored — it cannot be honoured, and the generator does not guess who you meant.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is re-shuffling until you get the team you wanted. That is not random any more — it is hand-picking with extra steps, and everyone can tell. If you shuffle five times and keep the draw where your friends landed together, you have chosen the teams, not the generator. Shuffle once, accept the draw, and play. If the draw is genuinely broken — all the beginners on one team, for example — say so openly and shuffle again once, with everyone watching, rather than quietly shuffling until the answer suits you.

The second mistake is uneven teams by accident. If you ask for teams of 4 from 22 people, you cannot get equal teams: 22 divided by 4 is 5 teams of 4 and one team of 2. That last team of 2 is not a bug — it is arithmetic. Check the numbers before you start. If the count does not divide evenly, pick a team size that does, or accept that one team will be smaller and give it a small advantage in the game. This generator always makes teams as even as possible, differing by at most one person, but it cannot make 22 divide by 4.

The third mistake is forgetting that random does not mean balanced. A random draw can put the three tallest players on one basketball team. That is not a failure of the generator — it is what random means. If your game would be spoiled by one lopsided draw, split the strongest players by hand first, one per team, and then let the generator place everyone else. And remember that names are matched exactly: a typo in a pair box is a request the generator cannot honour, so check your spelling.

Using random teams for games, classes and work

Random teams work anywhere people need to be split quickly and fairly. In a classroom, they stop the same groups forming every lesson and give quiet students a fresh start. In sports, they settle the argument before the game starts, so nobody wastes the first ten minutes negotiating. At work, they mix departments for a training day or a team-building exercise in a way that feels fair to everyone, because no manager chose the groups.

For tournaments, random teams are a good starting point, but think about the format. A single random draw works well for a one-off game. For a league or a knockout, you may want to draw the teams once and keep them for the whole event, so the teams can build a rhythm. If you redraw every round, the teams never settle, and the results feel like luck rather than skill. For a quiz night or a pub-style game, redrawing each round is fine — the fun is in the fresh mix.

One practical tip: copy the teams before you start, and share them where everyone can see them. A list in the group chat ends arguments about who is on which team, and it means late arrivals can find their team without asking. If someone drops out at the last minute, remove their name from the list and draw again — do not just move someone else to fill the gap, because that changes the balance you started with.

What this generator cannot do

This generator cannot balance skill, because it knows nothing about the people except their names. It cannot tell who is strong, who is experienced, or who has played together before. It cannot remember past draws, so it cannot stop the same teams coming up twice, and it cannot keep a record of who has played with whom over a season. If you need that kind of memory, keep a simple note of each week's teams yourself.

It also cannot settle arguments about the rules of your game, choose a captain, or decide who goes first. It makes teams, and nothing else. That is deliberate: one tool that does one job well is more useful than a tool that tries to run your whole event. Use it for the draw, and run the game yourself.

Questions people ask

How many teams can I make?

As many as you have people for — you need at least 2 teams, and you cannot have more teams than people. With 24 names you can make anything from 2 teams of 12 up to 24 teams of 1, though teams of 1 are not much use. In practice, most people make between 2 and 8 teams. If you choose team size instead, the generator works out how many teams that gives you.

Can I keep two people apart?

Yes. Type the pair in the 'Keep apart' box, one pair per line, with the two names separated by a comma. The generator shuffles first, then swaps people between teams until the pair is separated, whenever that is possible. If your requests contradict each other — for example, three people who must all be apart with only 2 teams — the generator still returns fair teams and tells you a request could not be met.

Is it really random?

Yes. The generator shuffles the names with the standard Fisher-Yates shuffle, the same method used for shuffling a deck of cards, using the browser's built-in random number source. Every ordering of the names is equally likely. Nobody's name is favoured, and the order you paste the names in makes no difference to the result.

Can I use this for a tournament?

Yes, for drawing the teams at the start. It works well for a one-off event or for the first round of a tournament. For a league, draw once and keep the teams for the whole event so they can settle. The generator does not run a bracket or keep scores — it makes the teams, and you run the tournament from there.

What if the teams are uneven?

Teams are dealt round by round, so they differ by at most one person. If your numbers do not divide evenly — 22 people into teams of 4, for example — one team will be smaller, and that is arithmetic, not a mistake. Either pick a team size that divides evenly, or give the smaller team a small advantage in the game to even things up.

Do I need to sign up?

No. There is no account, no sign-up, and nothing to install. The names you paste stay in your own browser and are never sent anywhere. You can use the generator as many times as you like, on your phone or on a computer, and nobody sees your list except you and whoever you share the result with.
