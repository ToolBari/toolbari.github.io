Is your PDF real text or just a picture?

Every PDF looks the same on screen, but underneath there are two very different kinds. A real-text PDF was made from a document, so the words inside it are genuine text that a computer can read, search and copy. A scanned PDF is different: it is a set of photographs of pages, made by a scanner or a phone camera, and the words in it are only shapes of ink on a picture. Your computer cannot read those shapes as text until OCR software has a look at them. Knowing which kind you have saves you from trying the wrong method and wondering why nothing works.

The test takes ten seconds. Open the PDF, then try to select one sentence with your mouse, or press and hold on a word if you are on a phone. If you can highlight the words and copy them, the PDF holds real text. If the whole page behaves like a single picture and nothing highlights, it is a scan. A second test is to search the document for a word you know is on the page, using the find tool in your PDF reader. If the search finds the word, the text is real. Scanned PDFs also tend to be much larger files than real-text ones, because photographs take up far more room than words.

Why does it matter? Real text comes out clean in seconds, with no special tools at all. Scanned text needs OCR first, and OCR always makes some mistakes, so you should expect to spend a few minutes checking and cleaning the result. The good news is that both jobs are free, and the rest of this guide shows you exactly how to do each one.

Method 1: Copy the text straight out of the PDF

If your PDF holds real text, this is the whole job. Open the file in any PDF reader, select the text you want, copy it, and paste it where you need it. On a computer, click at the start of the text and drag to the end, or press Ctrl and A to select everything on the page. On a phone, press and hold on the first word, then drag the handles to cover the passage you want. Most readers, including the one built into your web browser, can do this without installing anything.

The pasted text is rarely perfect. It often arrives with a line break at the end of every printed line, which makes paragraphs look broken, and tables or columns may paste in a jumbled order. Do not worry about that yet: paste everything into a plain text editor or a blank document first, then tidy it up. The cleaner at the top of this page fixes double spaces, missing spaces after full stops and words split by hyphens at line breaks. For a long document, work through it a page at a time so you can check each part as you go.

Method 2: Use a free OCR tool for a scanned PDF

OCR stands for optical character recognition, which is a long name for a simple idea: software looks at a picture of words and works out which letters they are. It compares the shapes it sees with the shapes of letters it knows, and writes out the matching text. Modern OCR is good with clean printed pages and poor with blurry ones, which is why the quality of your scan decides the quality of your result. A sharp, straight, well-lit scan can come out almost perfect. A dark photograph of a page taken at an angle will give you a puzzle.

Three free tools handle this job well. PDF24 Tools is a free online set of PDF tools that includes OCR: you upload the scanned PDF, choose the language of the document, and download the result as a searchable PDF or as text. Google Docs offers a second route: upload the PDF to Google Drive, open it with Google Docs, and Docs runs OCR for you and shows the text as an editable document you can copy from. Adobe Acrobat online also offers free OCR for a scanned PDF in your browser. Whichever tool you choose, the steps are the same: upload the file, wait for the tool to read it, then copy or download the text it produces.

A few habits make OCR far more accurate. If you are scanning the pages yourself, scan at a good resolution, keep the page flat and straight, and make sure the light is even. If you only have a photograph, retake it straight above the page with no shadows across the text. Always choose the right document language in the OCR tool, because the software uses it to guess words it cannot read clearly. And check the first page of the result before you run a fifty-page file through: if the first page comes out badly, the rest will too, and you should improve the scan first.

Method 3: Turn the PDF into an editable document

Sometimes you do not just want the text; you want to change it. In that case the cleanest route is to convert the PDF into a Word document and edit it there. Our guide to converting PDF to Word explains three free methods step by step, including what each one keeps and loses. For a real-text PDF, conversion keeps most of the formatting, so headings, bold text and lists usually survive. For a scanned PDF, convert with OCR switched on, or run Method 2 first and paste the cleaned text into a fresh document. Editing in Word is far easier than editing inside a PDF, so this method is worth the extra step whenever the words themselves need to change.

Fixing the mistakes OCR makes

OCR mistakes follow patterns, which is why a simple cleaner can fix most of them. The letter O often comes out as the digit 0, and the letter l often comes out as the digit 1, because the shapes are so alike. Full stops lose the space after them, so sentences run together. Double spaces appear where the software was unsure. Words split by a hyphen at the end of a printed line stay split, so example arrives as exam- ple. The cleaner on this page fixes all four of these: it swaps 0 and 1 back to letters only when they sit inside a word, adds the missing space after a full stop, collapses double spaces, and rejoins hyphenated words.

The cleaner is deliberately careful with numbers. It never changes a digit that stands on its own or sits next to other digits, so years like 2026, page numbers like page 12 and quantities like 10 items pass through untouched. That caution matters, because a cleaner that rewrote every 0 and 1 would corrupt your dates and figures. If a number inside a word still looks wrong after cleaning, check the original page: the scan itself may be the problem, and no text rule can recover a letter the scanner never saw clearly.

Some mistakes no automatic tool can fix, and these are the ones to watch for in your proofread. OCR sometimes swaps one real word for another, turning from into form, and a spellchecker will not catch those because the wrong word is still a word. It can also drop a short word entirely or read two columns as one jumbled paragraph. Read the cleaned text once, slowly, against the original page. For anything important, such as names, dates and figures, check every one against the scan before you trust it.

What to do with handwritten pages

Handwriting is where OCR gives up. The software is trained on printed letters, and even neat handwriting varies too much from person to person for it to read reliably. If your pages are handwritten, the honest answer is that no free OCR tool will give you clean text. Your best options are to type the pages out yourself, which is slow but accurate, or to read them aloud into a speech-to-text tool and then tidy the transcript. For a short note, typing wins. For many pages, dictating is faster, and you can clean the result with the same proofreading habits this guide describes. Either way, budget real time for it: handwriting has no shortcut.

A checklist for clean extracted text

Before you send or publish extracted text, run through this short checklist. It takes two minutes and catches the errors that embarrass people most.

  • The text is complete: compare the number of pages or paragraphs with the original.
  • Names, dates and figures match the scan exactly.
  • No garbled characters are left where the OCR gave up on a word.
  • Paragraphs are whole: line breaks from the printed page are gone.
  • Tables and columns read in the right order, or are marked for fixing by hand.

If one item on the list fails, fix it now rather than later. Extracted text has a habit of being copied onward, and a mistake that leaves your desk is far harder to catch than one you fix at your own screen.

Common mistakes

Most extraction trouble comes from a handful of avoidable mistakes.

  • Photographing a page at an angle and expecting clean text. Scan flat and straight, or retake the photo from directly above.
  • Running OCR on the wrong language setting, which turns good guesses into nonsense.
  • Uploading a private document to the first converter site you find. Use a tool you trust, and never upload identity documents or financial papers to a site you do not know.
  • Trusting the extracted text without reading it. OCR errors hide in names and figures, exactly where they do the most harm.
  • Converting a scanned PDF to Word without OCR, then wondering why the Word file holds only pictures.

Avoid those five, and the job is straightforward: find out which kind of PDF you have, use the right method, clean the text, and read it once before you use it.

Questions people ask

What is OCR in simple words?

OCR stands for optical character recognition. It is software that looks at a picture of words, such as a scanned page, and works out which letters they are, so the picture becomes text you can copy, search and edit.

How do I know if my PDF is scanned or real text?

Open the PDF and try to select a sentence with your mouse or your finger. If you can select and copy the words, the PDF holds real text. If you can only select the whole page as one picture, or the search tool finds nothing, it is a scan and you need OCR.

Can I extract text from a PDF on my phone?

Yes. Most phones can open a PDF and let you copy real text straight out of it. For a scanned PDF, use a free online OCR tool in your phone browser, or upload the file to Google Drive and open it with Google Docs, which runs OCR for you.

Why does my extracted text have so many mistakes?

OCR reads shapes, not meaning, so a blurry scan, a tilted page, faint print or an unusual font all cause errors. The usual mistakes are swapped letters and digits, missing spaces and words split at line breaks. Clean scans give clean text, and a quick proofread catches the rest.

Does this page upload my PDF anywhere?

No. This page does not run OCR and it never asks for your file. The text cleaner on this page works entirely in your browser, and the free OCR tools in the guide are separate sites you visit yourself.

Can OCR read handwriting?

Ordinary OCR is built for printed text and it usually fails on handwriting, even neat handwriting. If your pages are handwritten, typing them out or dictating them with a speech-to-text tool gives a far better result than any OCR tool.
