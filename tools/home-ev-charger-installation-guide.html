        What this planner does and who it is for

        Charging an electric car at home is one of the best reasons to own one: plug in at night, wake up to a full battery, and never visit a public charger for everyday driving. But getting a charger fitted is not as simple as buying a box and plugging it in. The charger needs its own circuit, the right protection, a safe cable route, and a fixed mounting point near where the car parks — and the work must be done by a qualified electrician.

        This planner helps you get ready before you call anyone. Answer five questions about your home, your parking, your driving, your electrical supply, and the charger speed you want, and the planner builds three things: a checklist of what to check, the cost factors that will shape the price, and the questions to ask the electrician. It does not book an installer, it does not quote a price, and it does not replace a site visit — it makes the site visit useful, because you arrive knowing what matters.

        It is built for EV owners and anyone about to buy an electric car, whether they live in a house with a driveway or a flat with shared parking. The plan adapts to the answers: a flat gets questions about permission and shared wiring, a home with no fixed parking gets an honest note about the difficulty, and a home with an old fuse box gets a warning that the supply may need upgrading first.

        How to use the planner, step by step

        Work through the five questions in order. The home type matters because a flat or shared building usually needs permission and may have shared wiring that complicates the work. The parking question matters because the charger needs a fixed place near the car — a driveway or a garage is straightforward, street parking is harder, and no fixed spot may mean a home charger is not possible at all.

        Daily driving decides how fast the charger needs to be. If you drive under 30 km a day, slow overnight charging replaces the day's driving easily, and a faster charger is money spent for little benefit. Over 80 km a day, a faster charger earns its place, because slow charging may not refill a full day of driving overnight. The supply question is the one most people cannot answer — and that is fine: choosing I do not know puts the supply check on the electrician's list, which is where it belongs.

        What the output means

        The checklist is the practical heart of the plan. Each item is something to check, decide, or ask about — the parking spot, the cable route, the supply, the protection, and where the cable will hang when it is not in use. Items that appear because of your answers are the ones that matter most to your home: a flat's permission question, an old supply's upgrade warning, a high-mileage driver's case for a faster charger. Work through the list and tick off what you have sorted.

        The cost factors are not prices. They are the things that make one installation cost more than another: the distance from the meter to the charger, whether the supply needs upgrading, the type of charger, and any ground work. A short cable run in a modern house is the cheapest kind of job; a long run across a garden with an old fuse box is the most expensive. Knowing the factors lets you read a quote properly — you can see what you are paying for, and ask why if a factor you do not have appears in the price.

        The questions for the electrician cover the seven things every installer should answer before work starts: whether the supply can support a charger, what speed they recommend, where the charger should go, what protection it will have, what permission is needed, what is included in the price, and how long the work will take. An electrician who answers these clearly is an electrician worth hiring; one who cannot answer them is not.

        A worked example

        Imagine a family in a house with a driveway. They drive about 50 km a day, the fuse box is modern, and they want faster charging so the car is ready even after a long day. They fill in the planner: house, driveway, 30 to 80 km, modern supply, faster charger.

        The checklist tells them to confirm the charger can reach the car from the driveway wall, to check the cable route from the meter, and to ask about protection for the faster charger. The cost factors list the cable distance, the charger type, and the wiring the faster charger needs. The questions give them the seven points to raise with the electrician — and the first electrician they call answers them all clearly, quotes a price that matches the factors, and books a site visit.

        Now imagine the same family in a flat with street parking. The planner's checklist changes: it asks about permission from the building manager, warns that a charger needs a fixed mounting point and a safe cable route, and notes that on-street charging may need permission from the local authority. The plan does not pretend the job is easy when it is not — it tells them what stands in the way, so they can ask the right questions instead of discovering the problems after they have paid a deposit.

        Tips for a good result

        Get more than one quote, and give every electrician the same information — your answers from this planner, and the questions list. Quotes you can compare are worth more than quotes you cannot, and an electrician who has seen the questions before the visit gives a better answer than one who is asked on the spot. Ask what is included in each price, and what would cost extra.

        Think about the cable route before the visit. The shortest route from the meter to the charger is usually the cheapest and the safest, and a charger placed where the cable reaches the car without crossing a path or a walkway is a charger that gets used every day. A cable that has to cross where people walk is a trip hazard and a daily annoyance.

        Plan for the car you will have, not only the car you have now. If a second electric car is likely in a few years, ask the electrician whether the wiring can support a second charger later — it is cheaper to plan for it now than to rewire later. And keep the plan: file the copied text with the quotes and the installer's details, so the record of the decision is there if anything needs checking.

        Limits of the planner — what it cannot do

        This planner gives advice, not an installation. It cannot check your electrical supply, it cannot see your home, and it cannot say whether a charger will work in your situation — only a qualified electrician on a site visit can do that. It does not book an installer, it does not quote a price, and it does not replace professional advice.

        Costs in this planner are factors, never prices. The price of an installation depends on the home, the supply, the charger, and the local market, and any number this page gave would be wrong for most people. Get real quotes from qualified electricians, compare them on the same basis, and be wary of any quote that arrives without a site visit.

        Finally, the planner cannot grant permission. Flats, shared buildings, rented homes, and on-street parking may all need agreement from someone else — a building owner, a landlord, or the local authority — and that agreement is your job to get, with the electrician's advice. Electrical work is not a do-it-yourself job: a charger fitted badly is a fire risk, and the saving is never worth it.

        Questions people ask

        What do I need before installing a home EV charger?

        A fixed parking spot near the house, an electrical supply that can support a charger, and a qualified electrician. This planner's checklist covers the rest, including permission where it is needed.

        How much does a home charger install usually cost?

        It depends on the cable distance, whether the supply needs upgrading, and the charger type. This page gives cost factors, not prices — get real quotes from qualified electricians.

        Do I need a faster charger or is slow charging enough?

        For under 30 km a day, slow overnight charging is usually enough. Over 80 km a day, a faster charger is likely worth it because slow charging may not replace a full day of driving.

        Where is the best place to put the charger?

        On a wall near where the car parks, where the cable reaches the car without crossing a walkway. The electrician confirms the exact spot on the site visit.

        Do I need permission from anyone to install one?

        Sometimes. Flats and shared buildings usually need the building owner's agreement, rented homes need the landlord's, and on-street charging may need the local authority's permission.

        Should I hire a qualified electrician?

        Yes — always. A home charger needs its own circuit and proper protection, and the work must be done by a qualified electrician. Never attempt electrical work yourself.
