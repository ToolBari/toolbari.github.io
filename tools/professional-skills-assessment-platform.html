How the self-assessment works

This tool asks you to rate twenty statements about your working skills, five in each of four areas: communication, leadership, technical and problem-solving. For every statement you choose a rating from 1 to 5, where 1 means strongly disagree and 5 means strongly agree. The ratings are added up into an average for each area and one overall average out of 5.

Each area is given a plain label: strong at 4 or above, developing from 3 to below 4, and focus area below 3. The labels describe your own ratings, nothing more. The tool then picks your two lowest areas and offers two practical learning suggestions for each. Those four suggestions are your starting point.

The assessment is self-reported, which means it measures how you see yourself, not how skilled you actually are. That is a real limitation, and the tool is honest about it. People tend to rate themselves higher on skills they enjoy and lower on skills they find hard. The value is in the reflection and the suggestions, not in the numbers themselves.

The four skill areas

Communication covers explaining ideas clearly, listening before replying, writing clear messages, asking questions when you do not understand, and giving feedback without causing offence. These are the skills that make teamwork possible. A strong communicator saves everyone time, because fewer messages need to be repeated or corrected.

Leadership covers organising a small team, helping others who are stuck, taking responsibility when something goes wrong, deciding with unclear facts, and encouraging others to share ideas. Leadership here does not mean being a manager — it means the habits that help a group get things done, whatever your job title.

Technical covers learning new tools on your own, keeping files organised, fixing common problems with your computer or phone, understanding the software your work depends on, and keeping notes that help you repeat a task. These are practical digital skills, not programming or engineering.

Problem-solving covers breaking a big problem into smaller steps, checking the facts before choosing a solution, trying a different approach when the first one fails, staying calm when a problem is urgent, and learning from mistakes. These habits matter in every job, because every job involves problems that do not have an obvious answer.

Who should use this tool

This tool is for anyone who wants a quick, structured look at their own working skills — whether you are starting a job, changing careers, preparing for a review, or simply curious. It takes about ten minutes and gives you a profile you can think about or discuss with a manager or mentor.

It is also useful before writing a CV or preparing for an interview. The profile shows which areas you feel strongest in, and the suggestions give you concrete things to talk about: what you are working on and how. Interviewers often ask what you are trying to improve, and an honest answer backed by a plan is stronger than a vague one.

The tool is not a test and not a qualification. It does not measure your skills, and it cannot be used to compare you with other people. No employer should use it for hiring or promotion decisions, and no certificate comes with it. It is a personal reflection tool, nothing more.

How to use it step by step

Work through the twenty statements in order. Read each one and ask yourself how true it feels in your day-to-day work, not in an ideal week. Choose 1 if you strongly disagree, 2 if you disagree, 3 if you neither agree nor disagree, 4 if you agree, and 5 if you strongly agree.

Answer honestly rather than hopefully. Rating everything 5 gives you a flattering profile and no useful suggestions. Rating everything 1 does the opposite. The most useful profiles have a mix — some strengths, some areas to develop — because that is how real skills look.

When all twenty are rated, press the button to see your profile. Read the overall average first, then the four area scores, then the learning suggestions for your two lowest areas. Copy the profile if you want to keep it or discuss it with someone. Nothing you enter is stored or sent anywhere — the page works entirely in your browser.

Worked example

Imagine a shop assistant who rates all five communication statements as 5, and all the other statements as 3. Communication averages 5.0 out of 5, which is strong. Leadership, technical and problem-solving each average 3.0, which is developing. The overall average is 3.5 out of 5.

The two lowest areas are leadership and technical, tied at 3.0, so the tool offers suggestions for those two: offer to organise one small task for the team this week, ask a colleague what support they need, spend thirty minutes learning one feature of a tool used daily, and write a short guide for a task done often.

The assistant picks the first suggestion and organises the weekly stock check. A month later, the assessment is run again: leadership has risen to 3.6, because organising the stock check gave the assistant real experience. The overall average is now 3.7. The numbers moved because the behaviour changed — and that is the point of the exercise.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is rating yourself as you wish to be rather than as you are. It is tempting to choose 4s and 5s throughout, especially if you plan to show the profile to someone. But a profile with no weak areas gives you no suggestions and no direction. Honest 2s and 3s are more useful than flattering 5s.

Another mistake is rating yourself against other people instead of against the statements. The question is not whether you are better than your colleague — it is whether the statement is true for you. Compare yourself with the statement, not with the person next to you.

A third mistake is treating the result as a fixed verdict. Skills change with practice. A focus area today can be a strength in six months if you work on it. Run the assessment again after a few months and compare the profiles — the change is the interesting part.

Finally, do not share the profile as proof of skill. It is a self-rating, not a test result. Showing it to an employer as evidence would be misleading, and most employers would not accept it as such. Use it for yourself, and for conversations about your development.

Sharing your profile with a mentor

A profile becomes more useful when someone else sees it. A mentor, a manager or an experienced colleague can read your ratings and spot things you missed — a strength you rated too low, or a weak area they have seen you handle well. Ask them to read the profile and talk through it with you for fifteen minutes.

Prepare for the conversation by picking two things to discuss: your strongest area and the area you most want to improve. Bring the suggestions the tool gave you, and ask whether they sound like the right next steps. A mentor who knows your work can suggest better ones, or point you to a course, a book or a task that builds the skill faster.

If you do not have a mentor, a colleague works just as well. Swap profiles with someone you trust and discuss each other's results. Comparing notes often reveals that other people struggle with the same things you do, which makes the work of improving feel less lonely.

What to do with your profile

Start with the four learning suggestions. Each one is a small, concrete action you can do this week: explain one idea in three sentences, organise one small task, learn one feature of a tool, write down the facts before acting on a problem. Small actions get done; grand plans get postponed.

Then look at your strongest area. A strength is worth using deliberately. If communication is your strongest area, volunteer for the tasks that need it — writing the team update, explaining a change to customers, or helping a new colleague settle in. Using a strength builds confidence and helps the team.

Keep a copy of your profile. The copy button saves the scores and suggestions as plain text, which you can paste into a note or a document. When you run the assessment again in a few months, compare the two profiles. The comparison shows what improved and what still needs work — and that is the real value of the exercise.

If you want a deeper picture, ask a colleague or manager to rate the same twenty statements about you, and compare their view with your own. Differences between the two views are often the most interesting finding of all.

Questions people ask

What does this assessment measure?

It measures how you rate yourself on twenty statements about communication, leadership, technical and problem-solving skills. It is a self-report, not a test — it shows how you see yourself, not a measured level of skill.

Is the result a qualification or a certificate?

No. There is no certificate, no qualification and no proof of completion. The profile is for your own reflection and development, and it should not be used as evidence of skill for a job or a course.

How honest should I be with my ratings?

As honest as you can. The tool only helps if the ratings reflect how you really work. Rating everything highly gives a flattering profile with no useful suggestions; honest lower ratings point to concrete things you can improve.

Can my employer use this to judge me?

They should not. The assessment is self-reported, so it cannot fairly compare people or measure real skill. It is a personal reflection tool. If an employer asks for proof of skill, they need a proper test or a work sample, not a self-rating.

How often should I repeat the assessment?

Every few months is a good rhythm. Skills change with practice, so a single profile goes out of date. Repeating it shows whether the steps you took actually moved your ratings.

Do I need to share any personal data to use it?

No. Everything you enter stays in your browser and is cleared when you close the page. Nothing is stored, sent or shared, and there is no account or sign-up.
