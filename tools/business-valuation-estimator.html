How the estimator works

The estimator uses two simple methods that buyers of small businesses often start with. The profit method multiplies the business's annual profit — specifically its seller's discretionary earnings, explained below — by a multiple, giving a range from a low multiple to a high one. The revenue method does the same with annual revenue. Together, the two methods give a combined illustrative range.

Enter your annual revenue and annual profit, then adjust the multiples if you have reason to. The defaults — 2 to 3 times profit, and 0.5 to 1.5 times revenue — are illustrative examples, not market data. They are there so the tool works out of the box, not because they are the right multiples for your business.

The result is an estimate only, not financial advice, and not a price anyone will pay. A real valuation depends on far more than two numbers, and this page explains what the tool can and cannot see. Use the estimate as a starting point for a conversation with a qualified adviser, not as the answer.

All figures are plain numbers in whatever currency your accounts use. The tool never asks which currency, because the maths is the same in any currency — the multiples apply to the numbers you enter, and the result comes out in the same currency you put in.

What SDE means

SDE stands for seller's discretionary earnings. It is the profit of the business plus the owner's salary and any personal benefits the owner takes through the business, such as a vehicle or a phone. The idea is to show what the business earns for the person who owns it, before the owner decides how to pay themselves.

Buyers of small businesses use SDE because the owner's pay is often mixed up with the business's profit. One owner takes a large salary and shows a small profit; another takes a small salary and shows a large profit. SDE puts both businesses on the same footing by adding the owner's pay back in.

To work out SDE, take the business's profit for the year, add the owner's salary, and add any personal expenses paid by the business. If you are unsure what counts, an accountant can work it out from your accounts in a short conversation — and it is worth getting right, because the profit method depends on it.

Be careful not to double-count. If the profit figure already includes the owner's salary as an expense, adding it back gives the true SDE; but if the owner takes no salary and lives on the profit itself, there may be nothing to add. The accounts will show which case applies, and an accountant can confirm it in minutes.

Worked example: a business with 120,000 profit

Take a business with annual revenue of 500,000 and annual profit (SDE) of 120,000. With the default multiples, the profit method gives 2 times 120,000, which is 240,000, up to 3 times 120,000, which is 360,000. The revenue method gives 0.5 times 500,000, which is 250,000, up to 1.5 times 500,000, which is 750,000.

The combined illustrative range is the lowest figure from either method to the highest: 240,000 to 750,000. That is a very wide range, and it is honest about why: the two methods measure different things, and the real value depends on factors neither method can see.

Notice that the profit method gives a much narrower range than the revenue method here. That is typical when a business is profitable: profit is a better guide to value than revenue, because revenue says nothing about costs. A business with large revenue and thin profit is worth far less than its sales suggest.

Why the range is wide

The range is wide because business value depends on many things the tool cannot see. Two businesses with the same revenue and profit can be worth very different amounts: one may have loyal customers and long contracts, while the other depends on a single client who could leave tomorrow. The tool sees the numbers; it cannot see the difference.

Growth matters too. A business growing steadily is worth more than one standing still, because the buyer is paying for future earnings as well as past ones. The tool does not ask about growth, so it cannot reflect it — a growing business will tend to be worth more than the estimate, and a shrinking one less.

Risk matters most of all. A business that depends on its owner for every decision is worth less than one that runs without them, because the buyer cannot buy the owner. A business with one big customer is riskier than one with many small ones. These risks lower the multiple a buyer will pay, and the tool has no way to measure them.

The age of the business matters too. A business with ten years of steady accounts is easier to value than one with a single good year, because the buyer can see the pattern rather than guessing from one data point. The tool uses one year's figures, so a business with a long, stable history is likely worth more than the estimate suggests, and a business with one lucky year is likely worth less.

What multiples cannot see

Multiples cannot see the customers. A business with a long list of repeat customers is worth more than one that must find new customers every month, because repeat custom is income the buyer can rely on. The tool counts revenue, not the relationships behind it.

Multiples cannot see the team, the contracts, or the assets. A skilled team that stays after the sale adds value; a team that leaves with the owner does not. Long contracts add value; a lease about to expire does not. Equipment, stock and debts all change the real price, and none of them appear in the two numbers the tool uses.

Multiples cannot see the market either. The right multiple for a business depends on its industry, its size, and what buyers are paying at the time. The defaults on this page are examples only — the real multiple for your business might be higher or lower, and only someone who knows your market can say.

Using the estimate wisely

Use the estimate as a starting point, not a conclusion. It tells you roughly where a conversation about value might begin, and it gives you numbers to discuss with an adviser. It does not tell you what to ask for, what to accept, or whether to sell at all.

Compare the two methods. If the profit method and the revenue method give very different ranges, ask why: the gap usually points to something worth understanding, such as thin margins or unusually high costs. The estimate is most useful when it makes you ask questions, not when it gives you a number to quote.

Above all, talk to a qualified adviser before making any decision based on a valuation. An adviser can look at your accounts, your contracts and your market, and give you a view this tool never could. The estimate costs nothing and takes a minute; the adviser's view is worth paying for.

It also helps to run the estimator more than once, with different multiples, to see how sensitive the result is. If a small change in the multiple moves the estimate by a large amount, the value is uncertain — and knowing that the value is uncertain is itself useful information before any negotiation begins.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is using revenue when profit is thin. A business with large sales and small profit is worth far less than its revenue suggests, because the buyer is buying earnings, not sales. Always look at the profit method first, and treat the revenue method as a cross-check.

The second mistake is picking the highest multiple and calling the top of the range the price. The top of the range is the optimistic end of an illustrative estimate, not a valuation. A buyer will start at the bottom of the range and argue down, not up.

The third mistake is treating the estimate as a promise. Markets change, buyers differ, and the real price is what a buyer will pay on the day — which may be outside this range in either direction. The estimate is a guide for a conversation, not a guarantee of any figure.

Questions people ask

Is this a real valuation?

No. It is an illustrative estimate from two simple methods, using example multiples. A real valuation looks at your accounts, contracts, customers, team and market, and should be done by a qualified adviser.

What is SDE?

Seller's discretionary earnings: the business's profit plus the owner's salary and any personal benefits taken through the business. It shows what the business earns for its owner, and buyers of small businesses often use it as the basis for a multiple.

Which multiple should I use?

The defaults are illustrative examples, not market data. The right multiple depends on your industry, size, growth and risk, and only someone who knows your market can advise. If you have a figure from an adviser, enter it and the estimate will use it.

Why is the range so wide?

Because value depends on many factors the tool cannot see: customers, contracts, team, growth, risk and market conditions. The wide range is honest about that uncertainty — a narrow range from two numbers would be a false promise.

Can I use this to set an asking price?

No. The estimate is a starting point for a conversation with a qualified adviser, not a price. Setting an asking price from this tool alone would be a mistake in either direction.

Does the tool save my figures?

No. Nothing is stored anywhere — the figures stay in the page while you use it, and they are gone when you close it. Use the copy button if you want to keep the estimate.
