How to use the ovulation calculator

Enter the first day of your last period — the day the bleeding started, not the day it ended — and your average cycle length in days. If you are not sure of your cycle length, 28 is the usual default, but your own average is better: look back over the last three or four cycles and count the days from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. Press Calculate and the tool shows three things: your fertile window, your most likely ovulation day, and your next expected period. The Copy button copies all three as plain text.

The cycle length box accepts 21 to 35 days. That range covers the great majority of regular cycles. If your cycles are regularly shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, the estimate this tool gives will be less useful, and the honest advice is to track your cycles for a few months and talk to a doctor about what is normal for you.

Nothing you enter leaves your browser. There is no account, no storage on a server, and no history kept. The calculation happens on this page and the dates disappear when you close it.

What ovulation is, in plain words

Ovulation is the day an ovary releases an egg. It happens once in each menstrual cycle, roughly in the middle, and it is the day around which pregnancy is possible. The egg itself lives for about a day after release. Sperm, however, can survive inside the body for up to five days, which is why the fertile window is wider than a single day: the days before ovulation matter as much as the day itself.

A menstrual cycle is counted from the first day of one period to the first day of the next. In a 28-day cycle, ovulation usually happens around day 14. In a longer cycle, it happens later; in a shorter one, earlier. The useful rule is not that ovulation is in the middle of the cycle, but that it is about 14 days before the next period starts. That is the rule this calculator uses, and it is why the cycle length matters: a 32-day cycle and a 26-day cycle give different ovulation days even when the last period started on the same date.

The cycle is driven by hormones, and it is sensitive to the rest of life — illness, stress, travel, big changes in exercise or weight can all shift it by days. That is normal, and it is also the reason any calculator can only estimate. The dates here are a guide built on your average, not a promise about this particular month.

How the fertile window is counted

The fertile window is the six days when pregnancy is possible: the five days before ovulation and the ovulation day itself. The five days before count because sperm can wait in the body for up to five days; the ovulation day counts because the egg is there. The day after ovulation, the window closes — the egg has gone, and the next chance is the next cycle.

The calculator finds the window in three steps. First it adds your cycle length to the first day of your last period to find your next expected period. Then it counts back 14 days from that date to find the most likely ovulation day. Then it counts back five more days to find the start of the fertile window. So in a 28-day cycle that started on 1 January, the next period is expected on 29 January, ovulation is most likely on 15 January, and the fertile window runs from 10 to 15 January.

Within the window, the most fertile days are the two or three days before ovulation and the ovulation day itself. The earliest days of the window are less fertile than the later ones, because fewer sperm survive the full five days. If you are using these dates to plan, the middle of the window matters more than its edges — but the whole window is the honest answer to the question of when pregnancy is possible.

What to do with the dates

If you are trying to conceive, the fertile window is the time to aim for. The dates give you a plan instead of a guess: you know which days matter and which do not, and you can stop wondering about the rest of the month. Many people find that simply knowing the window takes a lot of the stress out of trying, because the uncertainty shrinks to a few days.

If you are tracking your cycle for general awareness, the next expected period is often the most useful date of the three. It tells you when to expect your period, when a late period is worth noting, and when to buy supplies. A period that arrives more than a few days after the expected date is common and usually nothing to worry about — but if it happens often, or if periods stop altogether, that is worth a conversation with a doctor.

What the dates cannot do is prevent pregnancy. This calculator must never be used as a contraceptive method. Ovulation can shift without warning, sperm can survive longer than expected, and a single misjudged day is enough. If you want to avoid pregnancy, use a reliable method of contraception and treat these dates as information only.

When the result can be off

The estimate is only as steady as your cycle. Irregular cycles are the biggest cause of error: if your cycle length varies by more than a few days from month to month, the average the calculator uses will be wrong for any single month, and the ovulation day could be several days earlier or later than shown. The same applies after coming off hormonal contraception, after childbirth, while breastfeeding, or around the menopause — all times when cycles are often irregular.

Illness and stress can delay ovulation in a particular month without changing the average. A bad cold, a bereavement, a long flight, or a sudden change in exercise can all push ovulation later, which pushes the next period later too. If your period is late and you were ill or stressed in the middle of the cycle, that is a likely explanation — but a pregnancy test is the only way to rule pregnancy out.

Body signs can sharpen the estimate. Cervical mucus becomes clear and stretchy around ovulation, and some people notice a small rise in body temperature the day after ovulation, or a mild ache on one side. None of these is proof on its own, but together with the dates they give a fuller picture than either does alone. If you are trying to conceive and nothing has happened after a year — or after six months if you are over 35 — see a doctor rather than relying on a calculator.

Questions people ask

How accurate is this ovulation calculator?

It is an estimate based on your average cycle. It is most useful when your cycles are regular — within a day or two of the same length each month. With irregular cycles, the ovulation day can be several days earlier or later than shown.

Can I use this calculator to avoid pregnancy?

No. This calculator must never be used as a contraceptive method. Ovulation can shift without warning, and a single misjudged day is enough for pregnancy. Use a reliable method of contraception and treat these dates as information only.

Why is the fertile window six days long?

Because sperm can survive inside the body for up to five days, and the egg lives for about a day after ovulation. The five days before ovulation plus the ovulation day itself make six days when pregnancy is possible.

What if my cycle is not 28 days?

Enter your own average cycle length — the calculator accepts 21 to 35 days. Ovulation is counted as 14 days before your next expected period, so a longer cycle gives a later ovulation day and a shorter cycle gives an earlier one.

What if my period is late?

A period a few days late is common and usually nothing to worry about — illness, stress and travel can all delay ovulation. If your period is more than a week late, take a pregnancy test, and see a doctor if periods stop or stay irregular.

When should I see a doctor about my cycle?

See a doctor if your cycles are regularly shorter than 21 days or longer than 35, if periods stop, if bleeding is very heavy or very painful, or if you have been trying to conceive for a year — six months if you are over 35 — without success.
