How to use this optimizer

Enter the length of the boards you buy, in millimetres, and the kerf of your saw — the width of wood the blade turns into sawdust with each cut. Three millimetres is a common kerf for a circular saw, but check your own blade if you know it. Then list the cuts you need, one per line, as length times quantity: 800 x 2 means two pieces of 800 millimetres.

Tap the button and the optimizer packs the cuts onto boards, longest first, and shows you the plan board by board: which cuts go on each board, how the pieces sit along it, and how much is left over. The diagram shows each board as a bar, with coloured segments for the cuts and a pale segment for the waste, so you can see the plan at a glance before you switch the saw on.

Copy the result if you want to take it to the workshop. The copy button puts the summary and the working into your clipboard, so you can paste it into a note or print it. Check the numbers against your tape measure before you cut — the plan is only as good as the lengths you typed in.

How the optimizer packs your cuts

The optimizer uses a method called first-fit decreasing, which is simple, fast and usually very close to the best possible plan. It starts by sorting all the pieces from longest to shortest, then takes each piece in turn and puts it on the first board that has room for it. If no board has room, it starts a new board. Long pieces go first because they are the hardest to place; short pieces fill the gaps they leave.

The method is deterministic: the same list of cuts always gives the same plan, so you can re-run it and check the result. It is not guaranteed to find the mathematically perfect packing — that problem has no fast exact solution for long lists — but for the cut lists of a real project it is very close, and the waste figure tells you honestly how close.

Kerf is part of the packing, not an afterthought. Between two cuts on the same board, the blade removes a kerf-width of wood, so the optimizer reserves that space. The last cut on a board has no kerf after it, because there is nothing left to separate. If you ignore kerf, a plan that looks perfect on paper can run out of board on the last cut.

What kerf is and why it matters

Kerf is the slot the saw blade cuts — the wood it removes as it passes through the board. A thin-kerf circular saw blade removes about 2 millimetres; a standard blade about 3 millimetres; a hand saw can remove more, depending on the set of its teeth. The kerf is small, but it adds up: five cuts on one board at 3 millimetres each remove 12 millimetres of usable length between the pieces.

That is why the optimizer asks for the kerf instead of guessing it. If you enter a kerf of zero, the plan will pack pieces edge to edge and the real cuts will come out short. If you enter a kerf larger than your blade's, the plan will waste a little wood but the pieces will be right. When in doubt, measure your blade or test it on scrap and measure the slot.

Kerf also explains why a cut list sometimes needs one more board than the arithmetic suggests. The total length of the pieces might fit on two boards, but the kerf between them pushes the total past the mark. The optimizer shows the kerf in the working, so you can see exactly where the extra board went.

Worked example: a bookshelf

Suppose you are building a small bookshelf from 2400 millimetre boards, with a 3 millimetre kerf, and you need two sides of 800 millimetres, two shelves of 600 millimetres and one back rail of 400 millimetres. The pieces sorted longest first are 800, 800, 600, 600 and 400, and the total cut length is 3,200 millimetres.

The optimizer places the first 800 on board one, then the second 800 beside it — 800 plus a 3 millimetre kerf plus 800 is 1,603 millimetres — then a 600, which needs another 3 millimetres of kerf and brings the used length to 2,206 millimetres. The remaining 600 does not fit, because 2,206 plus 3 plus 600 is 2,809, which is past the board. Board one holds 800, 800 and 600, with 194 millimetres of waste.

Board two takes the remaining 600 and the 400: 600 plus a 3 millimetre kerf plus 400 is 1,003 millimetres, leaving 1,397 millimetres of waste. Two boards in total, 1,591 millimetres wasted, which is 33.1 percent of the wood bought. The waste on board two is a long offcut — keep it, because a 1,397 millimetre piece is a shelf for the next project, not firewood.

Getting more from every board

The simplest way to waste less wood is to plan the project around the boards you buy. If your boards come in 2400 millimetre lengths, a design with 800 millimetre pieces divides neatly, while a design with 850 millimetre pieces leaves awkward offcuts on every board. Moving a dimension by a few centimetres can save a whole board.

Keep your offcuts sorted by length, and check them before you buy new boards. The optimizer plans from full boards, but a long offcut from the last project is a free board for the next one — enter its length as the board length and plan the small pieces from it. Many workshops waste more wood by forgetting offcuts than by cutting badly.

Cut the longest pieces first. If a long piece goes wrong, the remaining board is still long enough to try again; if you cut the short pieces first and then spoil a long one, there may be nothing left long enough to replace it. The optimizer's longest-first order is also good workshop practice.

Saw safety: the non-negotiables

A saw does not forgive inattention. Wear eye protection every time you cut — a single chip in the eye can end a day, or worse. Wear ear protection with power saws, and a dust mask when you cut MDF or any manufactured board. Keep your hands well clear of the blade line, and never reach over a spinning blade.

Clamp the board instead of holding it by hand. A clamped board cannot move, kick back or pinch the blade, and both your hands stay free to control the saw. Support long boards at both ends so they do not tip or bind as the cut finishes, and let the blade stop fully before you lift the saw away.

Check the board before you cut it. Nails, screws and staples in reclaimed wood destroy blades and throw sparks, and a knot in the cut line can deflect a blade. Mark the cut clearly, double-check the measurement, and cut on the waste side of the line so the piece keeps its full length.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is forgetting the kerf. A plan that packs pieces edge to edge looks efficient on paper and fails on the bench, because every cut removes a blade-width of wood. Enter your real kerf and let the optimizer count it.

The second mistake is measuring from the wrong end. If the board has a damaged or uneven end, square it off first and measure from the fresh cut — otherwise every piece inherits the error. A tape measure hooked over a rough end can easily be a few millimetres out.

The third mistake is cutting the pieces in the order they appear on the list instead of the order the plan shows. The optimizer's order is part of the plan: longest first, board by board. Follow it, and tick each piece off as you cut it, so a mistake shows up while there is still wood left to fix it.

What this optimizer cannot do

This optimizer cannot see your wood. It does not know about knots, warps, cracks or the damaged end of a board, and it cannot route a cut around a defect. Inspect each board before you cut, and move a piece to a cleaner board if the wood says so — the plan is a guide, not an order.

It also cannot promise the perfect packing. First-fit decreasing is close to optimal for most real cut lists, but a different arrangement might occasionally save a few centimetres. If the waste figure looks high, try reordering the project dimensions slightly, or check whether an offcut can cover the shortest pieces.

Questions people ask

What is first-fit decreasing?

It is a packing method: sort all the pieces from longest to shortest, then place each piece on the first board with room for it, starting a new board only when none has room. Long pieces go first because they are hardest to place, and short pieces fill the gaps. It is fast, repeatable and usually very close to the best possible plan.

How do I enter my cuts?

One cut per line, as length times quantity — for example 800 x 2 for two pieces of 800 millimetres. A line with just a number, such as 400, means one piece of that length. All lengths are in millimetres, and the board length uses the same unit.

What kerf should I enter?

The width your saw blade removes, in millimetres. A thin-kerf circular saw blade is about 2 millimetres and a standard blade about 3 millimetres. If you do not know, measure the slot left by a test cut in scrap wood, or use 3 millimetres as a safe starting point.

Why does the plan use more boards than I expected?

Usually kerf. The total length of the pieces may fit on fewer boards, but the kerf between cuts on the same board adds up and pushes the total past the board length. The working shows the kerf, so you can see exactly where the extra board went.

Can I mix different board lengths?

Not in one run — the optimizer plans from a single board length. To use offcuts, run the plan again with the offcut's length as the board length and enter the pieces you want from it. Plan the longest pieces from full boards first, then the short pieces from offcuts.

Is the plan guaranteed to be the best possible?

No, but it is usually very close. Finding the mathematically perfect packing for a long cut list has no fast exact solution, so the optimizer uses first-fit decreasing, which is proven to be near-optimal in practice. The waste figure tells you honestly how much wood the plan leaves over.
