How the 3-2-1 backup plan works

The 3-2-1 rule is the standard advice for protecting files, and it is simple to remember: keep three copies of your important data, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy stored off-site. Three copies means one failure cannot wipe out your files. Two kinds of storage means one type of failure — a dead hard drive, a lost phone — cannot take all three copies. One copy off-site means a fire, flood or theft at home cannot destroy everything.

This planner turns the rule into numbers. You enter how much data you have, how much of it changes each month, and how many months of old versions you want to keep. The planner works out how much storage each copy needs and how much storage the whole plan needs, and suggests a schedule: back up changed files daily, check the full backup weekly, and test restoring a file every month.

The planner counts storage only. It does not back up your files, and it does not upload anything. It tells you what to buy or what plan size to look for, and what to do each week to keep the plan working. The actual backup still needs a service, an external drive, or both.

The three copies explained

Copy one is the original files on your device — your computer, phone or tablet. This is the copy you use every day. It counts as one of the three, but on its own it is not a backup at all. If the device is lost, stolen or broken, copy one is gone.

Copy two is a local backup, usually on an external drive kept at home. It is the fastest copy to restore from, because it is right there. A local backup protects against a dead device, a deleted file, or a failed update. It does not protect against fire, flood or theft, because it sits in the same place as the original.

Copy three is the off-site copy, stored somewhere else — a cloud backup service, or a drive kept at a friend's house or at work. The off-site copy is the one that saves you when the worst happens. It also holds old versions: if a file is damaged or overwritten, you can go back to last month's version. The planner adds room for those versions based on how many months you want to keep.

Backup services compared by feature

Cloud services differ in what they do, and the difference matters. Some are sync services: they keep the same files on every device, so a change on your phone appears on your computer. Google Drive, Dropbox, iCloud and OneDrive all work this way. Sync is convenient, but it is not the same as backup — if you delete a file, the deletion can sync to every device, and if a file is damaged, the damaged version can replace the good one everywhere.

Other services are backup services: they copy your files to a safe place on a schedule, and keep old versions for months. Backblaze is an example of this approach — it copies a whole computer automatically and keeps old versions. Backup services are better for the off-site copy in a 3-2-1 plan, because they are designed to restore files rather than to share them.

Many people use both: a sync service for files they work on every day, and a backup service for the full copy. That combination covers the 3-2-1 rule well. When comparing services, look at what they keep — some keep old versions for a month, others for a year — whether they work on all your devices, and whether you can restore a single file without restoring everything. Prices change often, so this page does not list them; check the service's own site for current plans.

How to use it step by step

First, work out how much data you have. On a computer, check the size of your main folders — documents, photos, videos, downloads. On a phone, check the storage settings. Add the numbers together and enter the total in gigabytes. If you are unsure, round up — a plan with a little spare room is better than one that runs out.

Next, estimate how much of your data changes each month, as a percentage. If you add a few photos a week and edit some documents, 5 percent is a reasonable guess. If you record video or take many photos, it could be 20 percent or more. The planner uses this to work out how much room old versions need.

Then choose how many months of old versions to keep off-site. Six months is a good starting point: it covers most cases of discovering a damaged file late. Enter the number and press the button. The plan shows the size of each copy and the total, plus the schedule. Copy the plan if you want to keep it.

Worked example

Imagine a photographer with 500 GB of photos and documents. About 5 percent of the data changes each month — new shoots, edited files, new documents. She wants to keep six months of old versions off-site.

New or changed data each month is 500 times 5 percent, which is 25 GB. The off-site copy needs the original 500 GB plus six months of changes — 6 times 25 is 150 GB — which is 650 GB. The local backup copy needs 500 GB. The total for the plan is 500 plus 500 plus 650, which is 1,650 GB.

She buys a 2 TB external drive for the local copy, which leaves room to grow, and looks for a cloud backup plan that covers at least 650 GB. She sets the backup software to run every evening and puts a monthly reminder in her calendar to test restoring one file. The whole setup takes an afternoon, and the monthly test takes five minutes.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is having only one copy. A single external drive is not a backup plan — it is a second copy in the same place, and it fails at the same time as the original in a fire or a burglary. The off-site copy is the part people skip, and it is the part that matters most when things go badly wrong.

Another mistake is never testing a restore. A backup that has never been tested is a hope, not a plan. Files can be corrupted, backups can stop running without warning, and passwords can be forgotten. Restoring one file a month takes five minutes and proves the whole chain works — the backup ran, the file is there, and you can get it back.

A third mistake is confusing sync with backup. Sync services are wonderful for working across devices, but they can copy a deletion or a damaged file to every device in seconds. If your only copy is a sync service, you do not have a backup. The 3-2-1 rule needs a real backup copy with old versions, not just a synced folder.

Finally, do not forget the small things: passwords, two-factor backup codes, and the list of what is stored where. A backup you cannot unlock is no backup at all. Keep the passwords somewhere safe, separate from the devices they protect.

Questions people ask

What is the 3-2-1 backup rule?

Keep three copies of your data, on two different kinds of storage, with one copy off-site. Three copies survive a single failure, two kinds of storage survive one type of failure, and the off-site copy survives a fire, flood or theft at home.

How much storage do I need for a backup plan?

It depends on how much data you have and how much changes each month. This planner works it out: enter your total data, your monthly change rate and how many months of versions to keep, and it shows the size of each copy and the total.

What is the difference between sync and backup?

Sync keeps the same files on every device, so a change — including a deletion — appears everywhere. Backup copies your files to a safe place and keeps old versions, so you can restore a file from last week or last month.

How often should I back up?

Back up changed files daily, check the full backup weekly, and test restoring one file every month. Daily backups mean you lose at most a day of work; the monthly test proves the backup actually works.

Does this tool back up my files?

No. It is a planner, not a backup service. It works out how much storage your plan needs and what schedule to follow. You still need a backup service, an external drive, or both to hold the copies.

What if my data grows every month?

That is normal, and the planner allows for it: the monthly change rate grows the off-site copy over time. Re-run the planner every few months with your new totals, and check that your drives and plans still have room.
