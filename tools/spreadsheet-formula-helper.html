The ten formulas to learn first

If you learn only ten formulas, learn these: SUM, AVERAGE, COUNT, MAX, MIN, IF, COUNTIF, SUMIF, VLOOKUP and CONCAT. With those ten you can add up a column, find an average, count things, find the biggest and smallest, make a decision, add or count only the rows that match a condition, look up a price from a list, and join first and last names together. That covers most of what most people do in a spreadsheet most days.

Learn them in that order, one at a time, and practise each one on real data before moving on. Type the formula yourself rather than copying it, because typing it teaches your fingers where the commas and quotes go. Make a small practice sheet with ten rows of made-up numbers and try each formula on it. Ten minutes of practice with real data teaches more than an hour of reading about formulas.

Two habits make everything else easier. First, always start a formula with the equals sign: without it, the spreadsheet treats your formula as plain text. Second, use cell references instead of typing numbers into formulas: write =SUM(A1:A10), not =SUM(10,20,30), so that when the numbers change, the answer changes too. A spreadsheet that needs retyping every time the data changes is not doing its job.

Looking things up: VLOOKUP, XLOOKUP and INDEX with MATCH

Looking up is the job that confuses people most, because there are three ways to do it and they look different. VLOOKUP is the oldest: you give it the value to find, the table to search, which column to return, and FALSE for an exact match. It only searches the first column of the table, and it can only return columns to the right of that first column. Those two limits cause most VLOOKUP problems.

XLOOKUP is the newer replacement, available in Excel 365 and Excel 2021 and newer. You give it the value to find, the column to search, and the column to return, and it can look in any direction. It is simpler to write and harder to break. But Google Sheets does not have XLOOKUP at all, so a sheet that uses XLOOKUP will not work if you move it to Sheets. That is the single most important difference between the two programs for lookups.

INDEX with MATCH works everywhere — every version of Excel and Google Sheets — and it can look in any direction. MATCH finds the position of the value, and INDEX returns the value at that position from any column. It looks more complicated than VLOOKUP, but it is more flexible and it never breaks when you insert a column. If you share sheets between Excel and Google Sheets, INDEX with MATCH is the safe choice.

Adding and counting only some rows

SUM adds everything. SUMIF adds only the rows that match a condition. The condition goes in quotes: =SUMIF(A1:A10,">100") adds only the numbers over 100. To add numbers from one column where another column matches, put the numbers range last: =SUMIF(A1:A10,"East",B1:B10) adds the column B values for rows where column A says East. Getting the ranges in the wrong order is the most common SUMIF mistake.

SUMIFS, with an S, handles several conditions at once, and the order flips: the range to add comes first, then the condition pairs. =SUMIFS(C1:C10,A1:A10,"East",B1:B10,">100") adds column C where the region is East and the amount is over 100. COUNTIF and COUNTIFS work the same way but count rows instead of adding them, and AVERAGEIF and AVERAGEIFS average them. Learn one of these families and the rest follow the same pattern.

One detail catches people out: conditions that compare numbers need quotes around the whole comparison, including the number. Write ">100", not >100, and write "Yes" with quotes for text. Without quotes the spreadsheet reads the condition as a formula or a name and gives an error. If your SUMIF returns zero when it should not, check the quotes first.

Working with text and dates

Text formulas join, split and clean words. CONCAT joins cells together, and TEXTJOIN joins a whole range with a separator such as a comma. LEFT, RIGHT and MID take characters from the start, end or middle of a cell, which is useful for pulling codes and initials out of longer text. LEN counts characters, UPPER and LOWER change case, PROPER capitalises each word, and TRIM removes extra spaces — the invisible spaces that make lookups fail and comparisons return false.

Dates are numbers in disguise: every date is stored as a count of days from a starting point, which is why you can subtract one date from another to get the number of days between them. TODAY gives the current date, NOW gives the date and time, and YEAR, MONTH and DAY pull the parts out of a date. DATEDIF counts days, months or years between two dates, and NETWORKDAYS counts working days while skipping weekends. Build dates with the DATE formula rather than typing them as text, because different regions read typed dates differently and the same text can mean two different days.

The dollar sign deserves a mention, because it is the one character that confuses almost everyone. In a formula, the dollar sign locks a row or a column in place when you copy the formula to other cells. A reference like ＄A＄1 stays fixed on cell A1 no matter where you paste it, while A1 on its own moves. The character is the dollar sign on your keyboard, and the FAQ below explains exactly how it works.

Google Sheets vs Excel: what differs

For the 48 formulas in this library, almost everything works identically in Google Sheets and Excel. The differences that matter are few but real. XLOOKUP exists only in newer Excel; Google Sheets has no XLOOKUP, so use INDEX with MATCH there. IFS and TEXTJOIN need Excel 2019 or newer, though Google Sheets has both. UNIQUE, SORT and FILTER need Excel 365 or newer, and Google Sheets has all three with the same names.

There are smaller differences too. Google Sheets and Excel use different date systems by default in some settings, which can shift a date by a few years when moving a file between them — if your dates suddenly look wrong after moving a sheet, check the date system setting. Error messages differ in wording but mean the same things. And a few functions have different names in other languages, though the English names in this library work in both programs set to English.

The practical advice is simple: if you share sheets between the two programs, stick to the formulas that exist in both, and avoid XLOOKUP, UNIQUE, SORT and FILTER unless you know everyone uses the same program. Everything else in this library is safe in both, and each entry notes its own limits where they exist.

Common mistakes and what the error messages mean

The most common mistake is forgetting the equals sign, which makes the spreadsheet show your formula as plain text instead of running it. The second is mismatched quotes or brackets: every opening quote needs a closing quote, and every opening bracket needs a closing bracket. The third is a wrong range, such as summing A1:A9 when the data runs to A10 — always check that the range covers the data.

Error messages tell you what went wrong if you read them. A hash followed by DIV/0 means you divided by zero or by an empty cell. NAME with a question mark means the spreadsheet does not recognise a word you typed, usually a misspelled formula name or a missing pair of quotes. VALUE means a formula expected a number and found text, or the other way round. REF means a cell the formula pointed to was deleted. N/A means a lookup did not find a match. None of these means the spreadsheet is broken; each one points at the exact problem, and IFERROR can hide any of them behind your own message once you understand what is happening.

What this helper cannot do

This helper cannot write a formula for your exact sheet, because it cannot see your sheet. It gives you the pattern and a worked example, and you adapt the cell references to your own data. It cannot check your data, catch a wrong range, or tell you that you summed the wrong column. Those checks are yours to make, and the best check is the simplest: does the answer look reasonable? If a total looks ten times too big, it probably is.

It also cannot teach you everything about spreadsheets. It covers the 48 most-used formulas, not the hundreds that exist, and it does not cover pivot tables, charts, macros or scripting. For those, the built-in help in Google Sheets and Excel is genuinely good, and the formula names you learn here will help you search for the next thing you need. This helper is a starting point and a quick reference, not a course — and for finding the right formula in seconds, that is enough.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between VLOOKUP and XLOOKUP?

VLOOKUP is the older lookup: it searches the first column of a table and returns a value from a column to the right. XLOOKUP is the newer version: you give it the column to search and the column to return separately, and it can look in any direction. XLOOKUP exists only in Excel 365 and Excel 2021 and newer; Google Sheets does not have it, so use INDEX with MATCH there.

How do I add only some rows?

Use SUMIF for one condition, such as adding only numbers over 100, or SUMIFS for several conditions at once. The condition goes in quotes, for example greater than 100 written with quotes around it. To add numbers from one column where another column matches, put the numbers range last in SUMIF, but first in SUMIFS.

Why does my formula show an error?

The common causes are a missing equals sign, mismatched quotes or brackets, a wrong range, or a lookup that found no match. Read the error: dividing by zero, an unrecognised name, a value of the wrong type, a deleted cell, and no match each have their own message. IFERROR can show your own message instead of the error once you understand it.

How do I join text from two cells?

Use CONCAT with the cells and any separator, for example CONCAT with the first name, a space in quotes, and the last name. You can also join with the ampersand: first cell, ampersand, space in quotes, ampersand, last cell. For a whole range at once, TEXTJOIN joins every cell with a separator you choose.

What does ＄ mean in a formula?

The dollar sign locks a row or a column in place when you copy a formula. A reference with a dollar sign before the letter and before the number stays fixed on that exact cell wherever you paste it, while a plain reference moves. A dollar sign before the letter only locks the column, and before the number only locks the row.

Can I use these in Google Sheets and Excel?

Almost all of them, yes. The 48 formulas in this library work in both programs, with one big exception: XLOOKUP exists only in newer Excel and not in Google Sheets, so use INDEX with MATCH there. IFS, TEXTJOIN, UNIQUE, SORT and FILTER need newer Excel versions, but Google Sheets has all of them.
