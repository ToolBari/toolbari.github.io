What this tool does and who it is for

A vehicle insurance claim organizer is a step-by-step guide for the minutes and days after a car accident. It holds two checklists, one for the scene and one for the photos, and a form for the details to record: the date and time, the location, the other driver's details, and your own notes. The copy button turns it all into a summary you can send to your insurer or keep in your records.

This page is made for drivers: anyone who drives a car and wants to know what to do if an accident happens. It is also useful to read before anything happens, because the steps are hard to remember under stress. A driver who has read this page once will know what to do when it matters.

The organizer covers the three things that decide a claim: safety, records and reporting. Safety comes first, because no claim is worth a second accident. Records come next, because the details and photos taken at the scene are the evidence the claim rests on. Reporting comes last, because the insurer needs the facts promptly and in order.

Everything stays in your browser. There is no account, no server, and no one else who can see your notes. The ticks and details are saved on your device, so the summary is there when you come back. The page never asks for your policy number or your personal details beyond what you choose to type in.

How to use it, step by step

Start with the scene checklist. It lists the steps to take at the scene, in order: check everyone is safe, call emergency services if anyone is hurt, move to a safe place if you can, warn other traffic, and exchange details with the other driver. Tick each step as you complete it. The checklist is ordered so that safety always comes before paperwork.

Next, fill in the details form. Record the date and time, the location, the other driver's name and vehicle, and anything else you noticed. Write what happened in your own words, briefly and factually, while it is fresh. Do not admit fault and do not guess: state what you saw and what you did.

Then work through the photo checklist. It lists the photos to take: all vehicles from several angles, the damage close up, the road and any signs or markings, and the wider scene. Take more photos than you think you need. When the checklists are done, press the copy button to turn everything into a summary.

  • Take photos before vehicles are moved, if it is safe to do so
  • Record the other driver's details and any witness details at the scene
  • Tell your insurer promptly, and keep copies of everything you send

What the output means

The progress counts tell you how many scene steps and photo items are ticked. A scene checklist with 6 of 7 steps done is nearly complete, and the open item is named in the copied summary. The counts are a quick check at a stressful moment: if a step is unticked, there is still time to do it before you leave.

The copied summary is the real output. It holds the details you recorded, the scene steps you completed, the photos you took, and your notes, as plain text. Send it to your insurer, paste it into a message to a family member, or keep it in your own records. The summary is the claim's starting point.

The details form is the part that matters most later. A month after an accident, the date, the time and the location blur, and the other driver's name is forgotten. A form filled in at the scene, with the facts written down while they were fresh, is worth more than memory. Fill it in fully, even when the facts feel obvious.

Nothing in the output is legal advice or a promise about the claim. The page lists steps and records facts; it cannot say who was at fault, whether a claim will be accepted, or how much it will pay. Those answers come from your insurer and, if needed, from the law.

A worked example

Take a driver who is hit from behind at a junction on a Tuesday morning. She checks that everyone is safe, then calls the police because the other driver seems unwell. She moves both cars to the side of the road, where it is safe, and puts on the hazard lights.

She opens this page and works through the scene checklist, ticking each step: everyone safe, emergency services called, vehicles moved, hazard lights on, details exchanged. She fills in the details form: Tuesday, 8:40 in the morning, at the junction of Market Road and Hill Street, the other driver a man named Karim in a white van. She notes that the traffic light was green for her.

She works through the photo checklist: both vehicles from the front and the back, the damage to her rear bumper close up, the junction and the traffic light, and the wider scene showing the road markings. Eight photos in total, taken in a few minutes.

When she gets home, she presses the copy button and pastes the summary into a message to her insurer, along with the photos. The insurer has the date, the time, the location, the other driver's details, and the photos, all in one message. The claim starts from facts, not from a phone call made from memory a day later.

Tips for a good result

Stay calm and stay safe. An accident is stressful, and the first instinct is often to argue or to hurry. Take a breath, check everyone, and move to a safe place before anything else. A calm driver records better details than an angry one, and a safe driver avoids a second accident.

Take more photos than you think you need. Photograph every vehicle from several angles, the damage close up, the road, the signs, the weather, and the wider scene. A photo you do not need costs nothing; a photo you needed and did not take cannot be taken later.

Write down what happened while it is fresh. A short note written at the scene, with the time and the facts, is far more reliable than a memory recalled a week later. Write what you saw and what you did, not what you think the other driver was thinking.

Keep a pen and paper in the car, as well as this page. A phone battery can die, a screen can crack, and a signal can vanish at the worst moment. A notebook and a pen in the glove box cost nothing and work when nothing else does. Write the other driver's details on paper as well as in the form, so the record survives a dead battery.

If the other driver refuses to exchange details, or leaves the scene, note everything you can: the vehicle make, model and colour, the registration number if you saw it, and the direction it went. Tell the police and your insurer at once. A driver who leaves the scene has broken the law in most places, and the details you recorded are the start of finding them.

  • Do not admit fault at the scene; state the facts and let the insurer decide
  • Do not move an injured person unless they are in danger
  • Exchange details with the other driver and note any witnesses before anyone leaves
  • Tell your insurer promptly, even if the damage looks small

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This organizer cannot file a claim for you. It records details and builds a summary; the claim itself is filed with your insurer, by you, following the insurer's process. No page can do that job, and any page that claims to file claims on your behalf should not be trusted.

It cannot decide who was at fault. Fault is decided by the insurers and, if needed, by the law, from the evidence and the statements. This page helps you record the evidence; it does not judge it, and it never should.

It cannot replace local law. What you must do after an accident, whether you must report it to the police, and how long you have to tell your insurer all depend on where you are. Follow your local law, and ask your insurer about anything you are unsure of.

Questions people ask

What should I do first after a car accident?

Check that everyone is safe, and call local emergency services if anyone is hurt. Move to a safe place if you can, warn other traffic, and put on your hazard lights. Only then start recording details and taking photos. Safety always comes before paperwork.

What details should I record at the scene?

Record the date and time, the location, the other driver's name and vehicle details, any witness names, and a short note of what happened in your own words. Note the weather, the road conditions and any traffic signs. Write it down while it is fresh, and do not admit fault.

What photos should I take?

Photograph all vehicles from several angles, the damage close up, the road and any signs or markings, the wider scene, and the weather. Take more photos than you think you need: a photo you do not need costs nothing, and a photo you needed and did not take cannot be taken later.

When should I tell my insurer?

Tell your insurer promptly, on the day of the accident if you can, even if the damage looks small. Policies set time limits for reporting, and a late report can weaken a claim. You do not need all the details before you report: report first, then send the summary and photos.

What if the other driver has no insurance?

Record everything as usual: the other driver's name, vehicle and registration, photos, and witness details. Tell your insurer promptly and explain that the other driver appears uninsured. Your insurer will tell you what happens next, and your own policy may cover you. Do not try to settle it privately at the scene.

Can I print the claim checklist?

Yes. Press the copy button to turn the checklists, the details and your notes into a plain-text summary. Paste it into any document or note and print it from there, then keep the printed page with your claim file and your photos.
