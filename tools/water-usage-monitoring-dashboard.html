How to use the dashboard

This dashboard has two parts, and you can use either or both. The first part is the meter log: each time you read your water meter, add the date and the reading here. After two readings on different days, the dashboard works out how much water your home used between them, how many days that covered, and your average use per day. The more readings you add, the clearer the picture gets — weekly readings give a good running average, and monthly totals show how the seasons change your use.

The second part is the "where it goes" breakdown. Enter roughly how many litres per day each part of the home uses — shower and bath, toilet, washing and cleaning, garden and outdoor — and the dashboard shows the split as bars and percentages. These are your own estimates, so they are only as good as your guesses, but even rough numbers are useful: most people are surprised by which part of the home uses the most.

Everything you enter stays in this browser only, on your own device. This page does not read your meter, it does not connect to your water company, and nothing is uploaded anywhere. That also means your entries live and die with this browser: if you clear your browser data, your readings go with it, so use the copy button to keep a backup of your summary somewhere safe.

Reading your water meter in 2 minutes

Your water meter is usually near the boundary of your property, under a small cover in the ground, or inside near where the main pipe enters the home. Lift the cover, wipe the glass if it is dirty, and you will see a row of dials or digits. The black digits are the ones that count for billing — they show whole units, usually cubic metres or litres. The red digits, if there are any, show fractions of a unit, and you can ignore them for this dashboard.

The unit matters more than anything else. One cubic metre is 1,000 litres, so a reading of 123 cubic metres is 123,000 litres. If you mix the two up, your numbers will be wrong by a thousand times, and the dashboard will tell you that your home uses a swimming pool a day. Pick the right unit in the dropdown when you log a reading, and check the label on your meter if you are not sure — it usually says m3 or litres.

Take a photo of the meter each time you read it. A photo settles arguments with yourself later — was that a 6 or an 8? — and it gives you a record if a bill ever looks wrong. Read the meter at roughly the same time of day each time, and log the reading straight away while you are standing there, so the date is right.

What normal household use looks like

There is no single normal number for household water use, and any page that gives you one without asking about your home is guessing. A household of two people in a flat with no garden uses far less than a household of six with a garden, a washing machine running daily, and a leaky toilet. Climate matters too: hot, dry places use more water outdoors, and cold places use more for heating systems that lose water.

The honest way to find your normal is to build your own baseline. Log readings for two to four ordinary weeks — not a holiday week, not the week the builders were in — and take the average. That average is your normal, and it is the only number that matters for spotting change. A home that uses 400 litres a day is not wasteful if four people live there and the garden needs watering; the same 400 litres would be worth investigating in a one-person flat.

Worked example: suppose your meter reads 1,000 litres on 1 March and 1,300 litres on 11 March. That is 300 litres over 10 days, or 30 litres a day — far below what most homes use, which would suggest the readings were taken from a meter that only serves part of the home, or that a digit was misread. The dashboard does this arithmetic for you, but the example shows the idea: the difference between two readings, divided by the days between them, is your daily average.

Finding a leak from the numbers

A leak usually shows up as a slow, steady rise in your daily average with no change in how you live. If your average climbs by a third or more and nobody new moved in, nothing new was plumbed in, and the garden did not suddenly need more water, something is leaking. The dashboard makes this visible because it turns a feeling — "the bill seems high" — into a number you can watch week by week.

The overnight test is the simplest check. Read the meter last thing at night, when nobody will use water, and read it again first thing in the morning. If the reading has moved and nobody used water overnight, water is going somewhere it should not. A small move might be a dripping tap or a toilet that keeps running; a large move could be a burst pipe or a leak underground.

Check the usual suspects first: taps that drip, toilets that keep filling after a flush, and outdoor taps left open. A toilet that runs quietly can waste a surprising amount of water in a day, and it is easy to miss because it makes no noise. If the meter keeps moving with everything in the home turned off, that points to a leak in the supply pipe, and that is a job for a plumber — do not dig up your own pipes.

Saving water without noticing it

The easiest savings are the ones that change nothing about your day. Fixing a dripping tap costs little and stops a constant waste. Shortening a shower by two minutes saves water every single day without anyone feeling deprived. Running the washing machine and dishwasher only when they are full saves water and energy at the same time.

In the garden, timing matters more than volume. Watering early in the morning or in the evening loses far less to evaporation than watering in the middle of the day, so the same amount of water does more good. A layer of mulch around plants keeps the soil damp for longer, which means less watering overall. If you wash a car, a bucket and sponge use far less than a hose left running.

Small changes add up because water use is daily. Saving 20 litres a day — a shorter shower, a fixed drip — is over 7,000 litres in a year, which is real money on a metered bill and a real saving of a shared resource. The dashboard helps here too: when you can see your daily average fall after a change, the change feels worth keeping.

What the breakdown tells you

The breakdown answers a different question from the meter log. The meter tells you how much; the breakdown tells you where. In many homes the toilet and the shower are the two biggest shares, followed by washing and then the garden — but your home may be different, and the breakdown is the only way to know for sure.

Use the breakdown to decide where to act first. If the garden is half your total, a water butt and better watering timing will do more than any change indoors. If the shower dominates, a shorter shower or a more efficient shower head is the best first step. If washing is the biggest share, fuller loads are the answer. The point is to spend your effort where the water actually goes, not where you guess it goes.

Be honest with the estimates you type in. A breakdown built on guesses is still useful, but it is only a starting point. If you want better numbers, measure one activity for a week — time your showers, count the flushes — and update the breakdown. The dashboard will recalculate the split straight away.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is entering the wrong meter digits. Meter dials are small, the glass is often dirty, and a 6 can look like an 8. Read the black digits only, take a photo, and double-check the reading before you log it. One wrong digit can make a month of numbers meaningless.

The second is comparing a holiday week with a normal week. If the house was empty for ten days, the average for that period will be near zero — which is correct, but it is not your normal use, and it will drag your monthly total down. When you look at a month that includes a holiday, remember why it is low before you celebrate.

The third is drawing a conclusion from a single reading. One reading is a starting point, not a result. You need at least two readings on different days to get an average, and you need several weeks of readings before the average means anything. Patience is part of the tool.

The fourth is mixing up litres and cubic metres. It is the easiest mistake to make and the hardest to spot, because the dashboard will happily calculate with whatever you give it. If your daily average suddenly looks a thousand times too big or too small, check the unit first — it is almost always the unit.

The fifth is forgetting the garden. Outdoor use is seasonal, so a home that looks frugal in winter can use far more in summer, and that is normal. Log your readings through the year and the dashboard will show the pattern, instead of leaving you surprised by one big summer bill.

This dashboard works only from the readings you type in. It does not read your meter, it does not connect to your water company, and it cannot see a leak by itself — it only shows you what your own numbers say. If the numbers point to a leak, check the meter and taps yourself, and call a plumber if the meter keeps moving with everything off.

Questions people ask

How do I read my water meter?

Find the meter near your property boundary or where the main pipe enters the home, lift the cover, and read the black digits — they show whole units, usually cubic metres or litres. Take a photo each time so you can check the digits later.

How much water does a home use per day?

There is no single normal number — it depends on how many people live there, whether there is a garden, and the climate. Log readings for two to four ordinary weeks and your own average is the number that matters; compare future readings against it.

How can I tell if I have a leak?

Watch for a steady rise in your daily average with no change in how you live, then try the overnight test: read the meter at night and again in the morning. If it moved and nobody used water, water is going somewhere it should not.

What uses the most water at home?

In many homes the toilet and the shower are the biggest shares, followed by washing and the garden, but every home is different. Enter your own estimates in the breakdown above to see where your water actually goes.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your readings stay in this browser only, on your own device, and this page does not connect to your water company or any server. If you clear your browser data, your readings will be removed, so copy your summary to keep a backup.

How often should I log a reading?

Once a week is enough for most homes to build a useful average and spot a slow rise. If you think you have a leak, log daily for a week — the extra readings will show the pattern much faster.
