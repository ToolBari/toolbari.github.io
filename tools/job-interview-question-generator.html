How the question generator works
Type the job role you are applying for — Primary School Teacher, Registered Nurse, Junior Web Developer, or anything else — pick your experience level, and press the button. The generator gives you ten practice questions in three groups: four common questions that come up in almost every interview, three behavioural questions that ask for real stories from your work, and three questions specific to your role.
Each question comes with a model answer framework. A framework is not a script. It tells you what a good answer covers and in what order — the point to make, the example to give, and the note to end on — but the words and the stories are yours. That matters, because an interviewer can hear a memorised answer a mile away, and it never sounds like the person they would be hiring.
Copy the list and work through it. Answer each question out loud, on your feet if you can, and time yourself — most good answers run between one and two minutes. If you cannot answer a question, that is useful too: it shows you the story you still need to find before the interview.
STAR answers in plain words
Behavioural questions start with Tell me about a time when... and they are the ones most people find hardest. The STAR method is the simplest way to answer them well. STAR stands for Situation, Task, Action, Result — four parts, in that order, and most of your time goes on the third.
Situation. One line to set the scene: where you were, what was happening. The Christmas peak, and we were two staff short. That is enough. Do not spend a minute on background.
Task. One line about what you had to do: keep dispatch on time with no extra staff. Short and clear.
Action. This is the heart of the answer, and where most of your time should go. What did you actually do, step by step? I re-laid the fast-moving stock, moved two people to packing, and checked the board every hour. Be specific. Name what you did, not what the team did — the interviewer is hiring you, not your team.
Result. One line about what happened because of you: dispatch went out on time every day that week, and picking errors fell by a third. A number makes the result believable. If you do not have a number, say what changed: the customer stayed, the team calmed down, the work got done.
The three kinds of questions
Common questions open almost every interview: Tell me about yourself, Why do you want this job, What are your strengths, What is your biggest weakness. They sound simple, and that is the trap — a rambling answer to Tell me about yourself can lose the interview in the first minute. Keep each answer under a minute, with one clear point and one short example.
Behavioural questions ask for stories: Tell me about a time you solved a difficult problem, worked in a team, made a mistake, learned something quickly. Answer them with STAR, and keep the story real — an interviewer who has heard hundreds of answers can tell a made-up story from a true one.
Role-specific questions test whether you understand the actual job. A teacher is asked how they handle a student who will not listen. A nurse is asked how they prioritise when everything is urgent. A developer is asked how they debug a problem they have never seen. These are the questions that separate people who read the job description from people who did not — and the generator builds them from the role you type in, so they match the job you are actually applying for.

   Framework: Use STAR: own the mistake in one line, then spend your time on what you fixed and what you changed afterwards.

Real stories from practice count — the interviewer knows she is new, and wants to see how she thinks, not how long she has done it.

How to practise
Read the questions, then answer them out loud. Reading answers silently feels like preparation, but it is not — your mouth needs the practice as much as your brain. Stand up, set a timer for two minutes per question, and talk. If you run out of things to say at thirty seconds, you need a better story. If you are still talking at three minutes, you need to cut the background and get to the action.
Practise with someone if you can. A friend, a family member, anyone who will sit and listen and tell you honestly when an answer rambles. If nobody is available, record yourself on your phone and listen back. You will hear the ums, the repeated phrases, and the moment the answer loses its thread — and hearing it is the fastest way to fix it.
Prepare your four STAR stories before the interview: a problem you solved, a team you helped, a mistake you fixed, and something you learned quickly. Write each one down in four lines — situation, task, action, result — and practise saying it until it comes out smoothly. Then, in the interview, listen to the question and pick the story that fits. You do not need a story for every possible question. You need four good stories and the calm to choose between them.
Mistakes to avoid
Memorising a script. A memorised answer sounds flat, and if the interviewer asks a follow-up question, a memorised answer has nowhere to go. Learn the shape of your answer — the framework — and let the words come fresh each time.
Talking too long. Most good answers run one to two minutes. Longer than that, and the interviewer stops listening. Practise with a timer.
Badmouthing your last employer. Whatever happened, stay positive about the people you worked with. Talk about what you want next, not what you want to escape.
Claiming what you cannot prove. Only claim what you can back up with a real story. An interviewer will ask for the example, and a vague answer undoes the claim.
Not practising out loud. Reading this page is not practice. Saying the answers, on your feet, with a timer, is practice. Do it at least once before the interview — twice is better.


Questions people ask


How many questions should I practise?
Ten is a good start — the generator gives you four common, three behavioural and three role-specific questions. Practise all ten out loud, and prepare four STAR stories you can reuse across questions.
What is the STAR method?
A way to answer behavioural questions: Situation (one line), Task (one line), Action (most of your time — what you actually did), Result (what happened because of you). It keeps your answer short, specific and easy to follow.
Should I memorise my answers word for word?
No. Memorised answers sound flat and fall apart on follow-up questions. Learn the framework — the shape of the answer — and let the words come fresh each time, in your own voice.
What if the generator does not know my job role?
It still works. For roles it does not recognise, it builds role questions from the job title you type — what you know about the role, what experience fits it, and what you would do in your first month.
How long should each answer be?
One to two minutes is right for most answers. Shorter than thirty seconds usually means the answer is too thin; longer than three minutes means it needs cutting.
What should I ask the interviewer?
Ask something that shows you are thinking about the work — for example, what a good first month looks like in this role. Avoid questions the job description already answered.
