Investment Fee Calculator

See what investment fees really cost over time. Enter your starting amount, monthly contribution, expected return and fee, and the calculator compares your balance with fees and without them — and shows the total fees paid and the growth lost.

Compounding is monthly. The fee is taken each month as a share of the balance, and contributions are added at the end of each month. All amounts are plain numbers in your own currency.

Education only — this page is not financial advice, and it never promises a return. The return rate is your assumption, not a prediction: real investments go up and down, and past performance does not predict future results.

How to use this calculator

Enter your starting amount, what you add each month, the annual return you expect, the annual fee you pay, and the number of years. The calculator compounds monthly and shows two balances: what you would have with the fee, and what you would have without it. The difference is the cost of the fee, and it is almost always bigger than people expect.

The yearly table shows how the gap grows. In the early years the difference is small, because the balance is small and the fee is a share of it. As the balance grows, the fee grows with it, and the lost compounding piles up. By the later years the gap is usually the largest number on the page.

All amounts are plain numbers in your own currency. The calculator does not ask which currency you use, because the arithmetic is the same in any of them — enter your numbers as they are, and read the results the same way.

How fees compound against you

A fee looks small because it is quoted as a percentage: 1 percent sounds like almost nothing. But the fee is charged every year on the whole balance, and the money it takes would itself have earned returns in every following year. That is the compounding working against you, and it is why a small fee becomes a large cost over a long time.

Think of it as a leak in a bucket you are filling. Each year the fee takes a share of what is in the bucket, and the water that leaks out never comes back — and neither does the water it would have become. The longer you save, the more the leak matters, because the bucket is fuller and there are more years for the missing water to have grown.

The fee also grows with your success. When your balance is small, a 1 percent fee is a small amount; when your balance is large, the same 1 percent is a large amount. You pay more in fees precisely in the years when your money is working hardest, which is why comparing fees matters most for long-term savers.

Worked example: 1 percent over 30 years

Suppose you start with nothing, contribute 200 a month, expect a 7 percent annual return, pay a 1 percent annual fee, and save for 30 years. You put in 72,000 in total. Without any fee, the balance after 30 years would be 243,994. With the 1 percent fee, it is 200,679. The fee costs 43,315 — more than half of everything you contributed.

Look closer at that 43,315. The fees actually paid, added up month by month, are 21,593. The other 21,722 is growth lost: the returns the fee money would have earned if it had stayed invested. The fee costs you twice — once when it is taken, and again in all the growth it never gets to earn.

Now compare a smaller fee. With the same contributions and return but a 0.5 percent fee instead of 1 percent, the balance after 30 years is higher by roughly half the gap — the cost of the fee falls roughly in proportion to the fee itself. Halving the fee roughly halves the damage, which is why fee differences that look trivial on paper matter so much over decades.

A second worked example: the lump sum

The monthly-contribution example shows fees over a long saving life. A lump sum shows the same effect in miniature. Suppose you invest 10,000 once, add nothing more, expect a 6 percent annual return, pay a 0.5 percent annual fee, and leave it for 10 years. Without any fee the balance after 10 years would be 18,194. With the 0.5 percent fee it is 17,306. The fee costs 888 — and of that, 668 is fees actually paid and 220 is growth lost.

Notice how the split differs from the 30-year example. Over 10 years the fees paid are most of the cost, because there has been less time for the lost compounding to build. Over 30 years the growth lost catches up with the fees paid and passes it. The longer the horizon, the more the damage comes from compounding rather than from the fee itself.

The same example also shows why even a small fee deserves attention. Half a percent sounds negligible, and on 10,000 over one year it is 50 — barely noticeable. Over ten years it is 888, which is nearly 9 percent of the starting amount. Small percentages become real money when time and compounding get to work on them.

What counts as a fee

The fee in this calculator is the total annual cost of holding the investment, whatever it is called: an expense ratio, a management fee, an annual charge, or a platform fee. If you pay more than one, add them together and enter the total — a 0.5 percent fund fee plus a 0.45 percent platform fee is a 0.95 percent fee in practice.

Some costs are not annual percentages and do not belong here. A one-off charge to buy or sell, a fixed yearly account fee, or tax on gains are real costs, but they behave differently from a percentage fee, and this calculator does not model them. Enter only the percentage-based annual cost.

Check the real number before you trust a memory of it. Fund fees are printed in the fund's documents, and platform fees in the platform's pricing page. A fee you remember as 0.5 percent may have changed, and a fee quoted as a monthly figure is twelve times larger per year than it looks.

Reading the year-by-year table

The table shows the balance with fees and without fees at the end of each year, and the difference between them. In year one the difference is small — the balance is small and the fee has had only twelve months to work. By year ten the gap is noticeable; by year twenty it is large; by year thirty it dominates the picture.

The gap grows faster than the balance, because the fee takes a share of a growing balance and the lost growth compounds. That acceleration is the point of the table: a single final number hides the shape of the cost, and the shape is what persuades people to act.

Use the table to compare two fees. Run the calculator once with your current fee, note the final difference, then run it again with a lower fee and compare. The yearly rows show not just how much you save, but when the saving starts to matter.

Fees paid vs growth lost

The calculator shows two cost numbers, and they mean different things. Fees paid is the money actually taken from your account, added up over the years. Growth lost is the full cost: the fees plus the returns that money would have earned if it had stayed invested. Growth lost is always the bigger number, and the longer the time, the bigger the gap between the two.

In the worked example above, the fees paid over 30 years were 21,593, but the growth lost was 43,315 — almost exactly double. Half the damage was the fee itself, and half was the compounding the fee money never got to do. That is why a fee is not a one-off cost: it is a cost that keeps costing.

When you compare two funds, compare growth lost, not just the fee percentage. A fund charging 1 percent instead of 0.5 percent does not cost you 0.5 percent of your money — over thirty years it costs you roughly half the gap the example shows, which is far more than the percentage suggests.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is entering the return you hope for instead of a cautious one. The return is an assumption, not a promise, and a high assumed return makes the fee look smaller by comparison. Use a modest return, and the fee's true cost shows.

The second mistake is forgetting the platform fee. The fund's expense ratio is only part of the cost — if a platform charges you as well, add it in. A low fund fee on an expensive platform is not a low fee.

The third mistake is comparing fees over one year. Over one year, a 1 percent fee on a small balance is a small number, and it is easy to dismiss. Run the calculator over your real horizon — twenty or thirty years — and the number tells the truth.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot predict your return. It compounds the rate you enter, smoothly, every month — and real investments do not behave that way. They rise and fall, sometimes sharply, and the average over thirty years is not a promise about any single year. The fee, by contrast, is charged in good years and bad.

It also cannot tell you which fund to choose. A lower fee is not always better: funds differ in what they hold, how they are managed, and what risks they carry, and this page knows none of that. Use the calculator to understand the cost of fees, then judge the funds on their full merits.

Questions people ask

What is a typical investment fee?

It varies widely by fund and platform, from a small fraction of 1 percent a year for a simple index fund to 1 percent or more for an actively managed fund, before any platform fee. Check the documents of the fund you actually hold — this calculator does not assume any particular fee.

Are lower fees always better?

Not always. Fees are one cost among several, and funds differ in what they hold and how they are managed. A lower fee on a fund that does not suit your goals is not a bargain. Use this calculator to understand what a fee costs, then judge each fund on its full merits.

Do fees apply in years when my investment falls?

Yes. A percentage fee is charged on the balance whatever the market does, so you pay it in falling years as well as rising ones. That is one reason the cost compounds: the fee never takes a year off.

What about taxes on my investments?

This calculator does not include tax. Tax rules differ by country and by account type, and they change over time. The fee comparison here is before tax; your after-tax result will differ, and tax advice belongs to a professional in your country.

Can I change the return to see different outcomes?

Yes — run the calculator again with a lower or higher return and compare. A lower return makes the fee a larger share of the outcome, which is worth seeing. Use a modest return for planning, and remember that no return is promised.

Is this financial advice?

No. This page is education only. It shows the arithmetic of fees against the return and horizon you enter. It cannot tell you what to invest in, and it never promises a return. For decisions about your money, consider speaking with a qualified financial adviser.
