How to use this calculator

Enter your home energy use, your car travel, your flights and your diet, and the calculator estimates your yearly carbon dioxide output — your carbon footprint — in kilograms. The total is broken down by category, so you can see what counts most for you, and the three biggest cuts are picked from your own numbers, not from a generic list. Leave any box blank if it does not apply to you: no gas, no car, no flights — the calculator treats a blank box as zero.

The electricity and gas figures come from your bills: look for the monthly usage in kilowatt-hours for electricity and cubic metres for gas. If your bill shows a different period, divide it down to a month — a quarterly bill divided by three is close enough. The car distance is your monthly driving in kilometres, and the fuel economy is how far your car goes on a litre of fuel: 12 kilometres per litre is a typical petrol car, 15 is a typical diesel, and your own car may do better or worse.

Flights are counted per year, not per month, because most people fly a few times a year rather than every month. Count each flight separately — a return trip is two flights — and split them into short-haul (under about three hours) and long-haul (over about three hours). The diet choice covers the food you eat in a year: meat most days, meat at almost every meal, vegetarian, or vegan. Be honest rather than optimistic — the calculator is a planning tool, not a test.

The factors this calculator uses

Every carbon calculator is only as good as its factors, so here they are, all of them, so you can check them. Electricity is counted at 0.4 kilograms of CO2 per kilowatt-hour, a typical grid average — the real figure varies by country, because some grids run mostly on coal and others mostly on water, wind and sun. If your country's grid is cleaner than average, your real electricity figure is lower than this calculator shows; if it is dirtier, it is higher.

Gas is counted at 2.0 kilograms of CO2 per cubic metre burned. Petrol is counted at 2.31 kilograms per litre and diesel at 2.68 kilograms per litre — these are the standard published factors for burning a litre of each fuel, and they do not change by country, because a litre of petrol is a litre of petrol wherever you burn it. An electric car has no tailpipe emissions, so it is counted as zero here — but the electricity to charge it is counted in your electricity figure if you charge at home, which is where it belongs.

Flights are counted at about 150 kilograms of CO2 for a short-haul flight and about 1100 kilograms for a long-haul flight. Those are typical published averages for a single passenger on a typical route — a very short hop is less, a very long flight is more, and flying in a fuller plane shares the fuel between more people. Diet is counted per year: meat-heavy 2600 kilograms, average 2000, vegetarian 1400, vegan 1100. Those are typical yearly figures from published studies of what different diets produce, including the farming, transport and cooking of the food.

What counts most

For most people, the biggest categories are home energy, car travel and flights — and which one leads depends on how you live. A person who drives a long way every day will find the car at the top of the list. A person who flies twice a year will find the flights near the top, because a single long flight is a large share of a whole year's footprint. A person in a cold climate with gas heating will find the heating near the top. The calculator shows your own breakdown, because averages hide the answer that matters — yours.

Diet is the category people most often underestimate, because it is spread across every meal of the year. The difference between a meat-heavy diet and a vegan one is about 1500 kilograms a year — roughly the same as a long-haul flight. That does not mean everyone should change their whole diet: even one or two meat-free days a week moves the number, and the calculator's diet figures are yearly averages, so a partial change gives a partial saving.

Electricity is the category that varies most by country. In a country where the grid runs on coal, electricity can be the biggest single category; in a country where the grid runs on water power, it can be one of the smallest. The 0.4 factor used here is a middle-of-the-road average, so if you know your country's grid is much cleaner or much dirtier, take the electricity figure as rough and focus on the categories you can measure more exactly — the car, the flights and the diet.

Worked example

Take a household that uses 250 kilowatt-hours of electricity a month and 40 cubic metres of gas a month, drives 1000 kilometres a month in a petrol car doing 12 kilometres per litre, takes two short flights and one long flight a year, and eats an average diet. The electricity is 250 times 12 months times 0.4, which is 1200 kilograms a year. The gas is 40 times 12 times 2.0, which is 960 kilograms. The car uses 1000 divided by 12 litres a month, which is about 83 litres, times 12 months times 2.31, which is about 2310 kilograms.

The flights add two short flights at 150 kilograms each, which is 300, and one long flight at 1100, which is 1400 together. The average diet adds 2000 kilograms. The total is 1200 plus 960 plus 2310 plus 1400 plus 2000, which is 7870 kilograms a year — about 7.9 tonnes. That sits inside the typical range of 4000 to 10000 kilograms a year for a person in a middle-income country, and the breakdown shows the car as the biggest category, followed by diet and flights.

The three biggest cuts for this household follow from the breakdown. The car is the biggest category, so sharing lifts or combining trips helps most. Diet is second, so a couple of meat-free days a week helps next. Flights are third, so one fewer long flight a year is the third cut. The calculator picks these from the household's own numbers — a different household with different numbers gets different cuts, which is the whole point of a calculator rather than a generic list.

How to cut your footprint without spending money

The cheapest cuts cost nothing at all. Turn the heating down one degree and wear a jumper — heating is a large share of home energy, and one degree makes a real difference over a winter. Turn off lights and chargers when you are not using them, and switch the television fully off instead of leaving it on standby. Wash clothes in cold water and dry them in the air instead of a dryer. None of these needs a purchase, and together they move the electricity and gas numbers by a noticeable amount.

Travel is the next free win. Combine errands into one trip instead of three, share lifts with neighbours going the same way, and walk or cycle the short journeys that are barely worth starting the car for. A car uses the most fuel in the first few minutes, when the engine is cold, so short trips are the most wasteful per kilometre — replacing them is the cheapest cut in transport. If you fly, the free cut is to take one fewer flight a year, or to choose a closer destination for one holiday.

Food is the third free win, and it does not need a new diet. Eat what you buy — thrown-away food is wasted money and wasted carbon in equal measure. Plan meals, use leftovers, and buy only what you will eat before it goes off. One or two meat-free days a week lowers the diet figure without changing everything, and seasonal local food usually travels less than out-of-season imports. Small habits, kept all year, add up to more than one grand gesture.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is counting only one category and calling it the whole footprint. A person who counts only their flights, or only their car, misses most of their total — home energy and food are large shares that are easy to forget because they are spread out. This calculator covers the five big categories together, which is why the total is usually larger than people expect. A larger total is not a worse result — it is a more honest one.

The second mistake is treating the total as exact. The factors are published averages, the inputs are your own estimates, and the real figure for your life could be a fifth higher or lower. Use the total to compare options and to find your biggest categories, not as a precise measurement. If two plans differ by a small amount in the calculator, treat them as equal — the difference is smaller than the uncertainty in the factors.

The third mistake is focusing on the smallest category. Recycling is good, and it matters, but it changes the total by much less than driving less, flying less, or heating less — because most of your footprint comes from energy and food, not from the bin. Do recycle, but do not let it distract you from the big categories. The calculator's three biggest cuts point at the big ones on purpose.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot measure your real footprint — it estimates it from your inputs and from published average factors. It does not know your country's electricity grid, the exact efficiency of your car, or what you actually eat, and it does not count the things it does not ask about: the things you buy, the clothes you wear, the services you use, or the share of your workplace and your country's infrastructure. A full footprint would be larger than the number here, because it would count more of life.

It also cannot tell you what to do. Cutting your footprint is a matter of your own choices, your money and your circumstances — a cut that is easy for one person may be impossible for another. Use the calculator to see where your footprint comes from and which cuts are biggest for you, then pick the ones that fit your life. The honest number is the starting point, not the finish line.

Questions people ask

What is a carbon footprint?

A carbon footprint is the total amount of carbon dioxide your way of life produces in a year, measured in kilograms. It covers the energy you use at home, the fuel you burn travelling, the flights you take, and the food you eat. This calculator estimates it from your own numbers and standard published factors.

What counts most in my footprint?

It depends on how you live. For most people the biggest categories are home energy, car travel and flights, with food close behind. The calculator shows your own breakdown, because the answer that matters is yours — a long-distance driver and a frequent flyer have very different biggest categories.

Are the numbers exact?

No — they are estimates from standard published factors, and the page lists every factor it uses so you can check them. Your real figure could be a fifth higher or lower. Use the total to compare options and to find your biggest categories, not as a precise measurement.

How can I cut my footprint without spending money?

Turn the heating down one degree, switch off lights and standby power, wash in cold water and dry clothes in the air. Combine car trips, share lifts, and walk or cycle short journeys. Eat what you buy and try one or two meat-free days a week. Small habits kept all year add up.

Does recycling change the result much?

Less than most people expect. Recycling matters, but most of a footprint comes from energy, travel and food, not from the bin. Do recycle — and focus your biggest effort on the big categories the calculator shows, because that is where the real cuts are.

What is a typical yearly footprint?

A typical yearly footprint is roughly 4000 to 10000 kilograms per person, depending on the country and the way of life. The world average is around 4700 kilograms per person, but rich countries are far above it and poor countries far below. The calculator compares your total with that range.
