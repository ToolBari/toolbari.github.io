What this quiz maker does

A quiz is one of the simplest ways to test what you know, and this page lets you build one from scratch in a few minutes. You write the questions, you write four options for each question, and you pick which option is right. Then you play the quiz yourself, or you hand it to someone else to play. There is no account to create, nothing to download, and nothing to pay for. Everything happens right here in your browser, on the device you are already holding.

People use quizzes for all sorts of reasons. A student makes a quiz from class notes and plays it the evening before a test, because answering a question from memory is a much stronger way to learn than reading the same page again. A teacher makes a short quiz to check whether a lesson landed, and finds out in minutes which ideas need another explanation. A parent makes a fun quiz for the family at the weekend, and a group of friends makes one about each other just to see who pays the most attention. The same simple shape works for all of them: a question, four options, one right answer.

This maker keeps the whole process in one place. The builder is at the top of the page, the player is right below it, and your quiz is saved automatically as you go. You do not have to finish the quiz in one sitting. You can write five questions now, close the page, and add five more tomorrow, because your questions are saved on your device. When the quiz is ready, you can play it here, copy the whole thing as plain text to share with anyone, or print it out on paper. A printed quiz works well in a classroom, at a family gathering, or anywhere people would rather hold a sheet of paper than look at a screen.

The quiz you make belongs to you. It is not uploaded to a server, it is not shown to anyone else, and nobody can see your questions unless you choose to share them. That matters when the quiz is for a class, a workplace, or a private group. You stay in control of the words, the answers, and who gets to play.

How to use it on your phone and on a computer

Start by giving your quiz a title. The title is the first thing a player sees, so make it plain and honest: the subject of the quiz, and nothing more. A title like "Science questions for class six" tells a player exactly what to expect. A title that tries to be clever and says nothing about the subject only confuses people.

Next, write your first question. Type the question in the question box, then type four options in the four option boxes. One of those options must be the right answer, and the other three are the wrong answers, which quiz writers often call the distractors. Choose the right answer from the dropdown list, then tap the Add question button. Your question appears in the list below, and it is saved straight away. Repeat the same steps for every question you want to add.

On a phone, the whole thing works with your thumbs. The boxes are large, the buttons are large, and you can build a quiz while sitting on a bus or waiting in a queue. The keyboard on your phone will offer its usual tools, and you can move from one box to the next without pinching or zooming. On a computer, the same page works with a keyboard and mouse, and typing a long quiz is faster because you can move quickly between boxes with the tab key. The page looks the same on both, and your saved quiz is waiting for you whenever you come back to this page in the same browser.

Before you play, read through your list of questions. Each question in the list shows all four options, with the correct one marked, so you can check that you picked the right answer for every question. If you spot a mistake, tap the Remove button next to that question and add it again with the correction. It is much easier to fix a wrong answer now than to argue about it after someone has played the quiz and lost a point unfairly.

When you are happy with the list, tap Start quiz. The player shows one question at a time, with its four options as big buttons. Tap the option you think is right, and the next question appears. There is no timer and no pressure, so you can take as long as you like on each question. At the end you see your score, and below it a review of every question you got wrong, showing what you picked and what the right answer was. That review is the most useful part of the quiz, because it tells you exactly what to study next.

Writing a good quiz question

A good quiz question tests one idea and has one clear right answer. That sounds obvious, but it is the rule that most home-made quizzes break. If a question asks two things at once, a player can know one half and not the other, and then the question does not measure anything useful. Keep each question about a single fact, a single idea, or a single step. If you find yourself writing the word "and" in the middle of a question, ask yourself whether you are really writing two questions. If you are, split them.

The wording matters as much as the idea. Write the question the way you would ask it out loud, in plain words, and avoid trick wording. A question is not a riddle. Words like "always", "never", and "not" can turn a fair question into a trap, because a player who knows the subject can still get it wrong by misreading one word. If you do need a negative word, make sure the question is short enough that the player cannot miss it. Short questions are almost always better than long ones. A question that runs for three lines is usually hiding the real question inside a lot of extra words, and the extra words only give the player more ways to get confused.

The wrong answers matter just as much as the right one. A wrong answer that is obviously wrong teaches you nothing, because every player will skip it without thinking. The best wrong answers are believable: they are the answers a person would give if they half remembered the lesson, or mixed up two similar ideas. If the question asks for the capital of a country, the wrong answers should be other real cities in that country, not random words. If the question asks for a number, the wrong answers should be close to the right number, not wildly different. When all four options look reasonable, the player has to actually know the answer instead of guessing by elimination.

There are a few habits that make questions weaker, and they are easy to avoid once you know them. Do not make the right answer longer than the wrong answers, because players learn quickly that the longest option is usually the right one. Do not use "all of the above" or "none of the above" unless you have a strong reason, because those options change the question into something else entirely. Do not repeat a word from the question inside the right answer and nowhere else, because that gives the answer away. And do not make one option funny while the others are serious, unless the whole quiz is meant to be funny, because a joke option is a free clue.

It also helps to think about who will play the quiz. A quiz for young children should use short words and everyday ideas, and every question should be answerable from what they have actually been taught. A quiz for adults who know the subject can be harder, but it should still be fair: every question should have an answer that the player could reasonably know or work out. A question that nobody in the room can answer does not make the quiz harder, it makes it pointless. Aim for a mix: a few easy questions at the start so players feel welcome, most questions in the middle range, and one or two hard ones near the end for the players who really know the subject.

Playing and sharing your quiz

Playing the quiz here is the simplest way to use it. Tap Start quiz, answer one question at a time, and read the review at the end. The review shows each question you missed, the answer you picked, and the correct answer, so you can learn from the mistakes instead of just seeing a number. If you are studying, play the same quiz again after a day or two. The second time through, the questions you missed before will tell you whether the ideas have stuck. Repeating a quiz is one of the most effective ways to remember something, and it costs nothing but a few minutes.

If you want other people to play, the copy button gives you the whole quiz as plain text. The text version lists the title, every question numbered in order, the four options lettered A to D, and the answers at the end. You can paste it into a message, an email, a document, or a class group chat. Because it is plain text, it works anywhere: it does not need a special app, and it will look the same on any phone or computer. If you are sending it to players, you may want to remove the answer list from the bottom before you send it, so nobody can peek.

The print button opens your browser print dialog, so you can print the quiz on paper or save it as a PDF. A printed quiz is useful when you want people to answer without a screen, for example in a classroom or at a gathering where phones are put away. Print one copy for the players and keep one copy with the answers for yourself, so you can mark the papers quickly afterwards. If you print from a phone, most phones let you save the page as a PDF instead of sending it to a printer, which is a handy way to keep a copy of the quiz in your files.

One thing to remember about sharing: the quiz itself lives only in your browser. When you share the text version, you are sharing a copy of the words, not a live link to your quiz. If you change a question afterwards, the copy you already sent does not change with it, so send the text version again if you make an important fix. And if you want someone else to be able to edit the quiz, they will need to build their own copy from the text, because there is no shared editing on this page.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is writing a question with two right answers. It happens easily: you write a question, you pick one answer as correct, and then a player points out that another option is also true. When that happens, the question is broken, and no answer is fair. Before you finish a quiz, read every question and ask yourself whether any other option could be defended as correct. If it could, change the wording or change the option. A question should have exactly one answer that is clearly right, and three that are clearly wrong, even if the wrong ones are believable.

The second mistake is writing a question that nobody can answer. This usually happens when the question depends on a detail only the writer knows, or on a fact so obscure that even an expert would have to guess. A quiz is not a test of whether the player can read your mind. Every question should be answerable by someone who knows the subject reasonably well. If you are not sure whether a question is fair, try it on one person first. If they stare at you blankly, the question needs to change.

The third mistake is making the quiz far too long. Fifty questions in one quiz sounds impressive, but most players stop enjoying it after twenty, and the last thirty questions get answered in a rush or not at all. A short quiz that people finish is worth more than a long quiz that people abandon. Ten to twenty questions is a good length for most purposes. If you have more material than that, split it into two quizzes with different titles, and players can play them on different days.

A few smaller mistakes are worth watching for. Do not make every right answer the same letter, because players notice the pattern and start guessing it. Spread the right answers across all four options. Do not write questions that depend on each other, where getting one wrong makes the next one impossible, because that punishes a player twice for a single gap in knowledge. And do not write the quiz in a hurry and publish it without reading it through. A single spelling mistake in an option can give the answer away, and a missing word can make a question unanswerable. Read the list once from top to bottom before you share it.

What this tool cannot do

This page is a simple quiz maker, and it is honest about its limits. It does not run timed quizzes, it does not rank players against each other, and it does not collect scores from other people. There is no leaderboard and no way for someone else to submit their score to you. If you need a quiz with a timer or a scoreboard, you will need a different kind of tool, one that runs on a server and keeps track of players.

It also cannot check whether your questions are correct. The page trusts whatever you type: if you mark the wrong option as correct, the quiz will happily mark players wrong for giving the right answer. Only you can check the facts in your quiz, so read every question and every answer before you share it. And remember that the quiz is saved only on this device, in this browser. It will not follow you to another phone, and it will not survive a cleared browser. Copy the text version if the quiz matters to you.
