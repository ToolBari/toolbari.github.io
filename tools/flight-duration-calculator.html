Why flights take longer one way than the other

Every frequent flyer notices it: the flight out takes one time, and the flight back takes another. The main reason is wind. High above the ground, fast rivers of air called jet streams flow from west to east. A plane flying east rides a tailwind and gets there sooner. The same plane flying west pushes into a headwind and takes longer. On a long flight the difference can be an hour or more, and it is completely normal.

The route matters too. Planes do not fly in a perfect straight line. They follow air routes set by air traffic control, they go around bad weather, and they queue to land at busy airports. Two flights between the same cities can take different paths on different days. The time of year matters as well: the jet streams shift with the seasons, so a winter flight and a summer flight on the same route can differ by half an hour.

This calculator uses still-air speed — the speed of the plane through the air, with no wind added or taken away. Think of the answer as the middle of the range: with a tailwind you may arrive a little sooner, with a headwind a little later. That is why the airline's scheduled time is usually a little longer than this calculator's answer. The airline builds in a buffer for wind, queuing and the slow climb and descent at each end.

Layovers and what they add to your journey

Flight time is not the same as journey time. Flight time is the time in the air. Journey time is door to door: getting to the airport, checking in, waiting, flying, changing planes, collecting bags, and getting to your hotel or home. A flight that takes 3 hours can easily be an 8-hour journey once everything else is counted.

A layover — a stop where you change planes — adds more than the waiting time on the ticket. You need time to get off the first plane, walk to the next gate, and sometimes go through security or immigration again. In a big airport that walk alone can take twenty minutes. Airlines sell connections with as little as 45 minutes between flights, but that leaves no room for a late arrival. For your own comfort, allow at least 2 hours for an international connection, and more if you must change terminals or collect and re-check bags.

Overnight layovers are a different case. Some cheap tickets include a night in the connecting city, which can be a free mini-trip if you plan it — or a miserable night on an airport bench if you do not. Check whether the airport stays open overnight and whether you need a visa to leave the airport, even for a few hours. This calculator counts air time only. Add your layovers yourself to get the real journey time.

Worked example: Dhaka to Kuala Lumpur

Dhaka to Kuala Lumpur is about 2,520 kilometres. At a cruising speed of 850 kilometres per hour, the maths is simple: 2,520 divided by 850 is just under 3 hours — 2 hours and 58 minutes of air time. Say you leave Dhaka at 10:00 in the morning. Add 2 hours and 58 minutes and it is 12:58 by Dhaka time. Kuala Lumpur is 2 hours ahead of Dhaka, so the local arrival time is 14:58, just before 3 in the afternoon.

Now compare that with a real ticket. Airlines usually schedule this flight at about 4 hours, not 3. The extra hour is not a mistake. It covers taxiing out to the runway, the slow climb after take-off, the descent and approach, and a buffer for wind and queuing. Short flights have the same pattern: the climb and descent take a fixed chunk of time whatever the distance, so the scheduled time is always longer than the simple distance-divided-by-speed answer. Common mistakes

The most common mistake is forgetting the time-zone change. People add the flight time to the departure time and call that the arrival time — then arrive two hours early or late in their plans. Always ask: what time is it there? The time-zone difference is part of the answer, not an extra. Some countries also use daylight saving, shifting their clocks by an hour for part of the year. London is 6 hours behind Dhaka in winter but 5 hours behind in summer. Check the current difference before you travel.

The second mistake is trusting the distance alone. Two routes of the same length can take different times because the planes fly at different speeds, the winds differ, and one route may climb over mountains while the other flies over flat sea. Distance is the starting point, not the whole story. The third mistake is confusing flight time with journey time — booking a meeting one hour after landing, with no time for immigration, bags and the drive into the city. Leave a generous gap after landing, especially in a city you do not know.

A smaller mistake is using road distance instead of air distance. The road from Dhaka to Kathmandu winds through hills and is far longer than the flight path. Always use the air distance — the straight line over the ground — for flight maths. And remember that this calculator gives air time at cruising speed. The climb and descent are slower, so for short flights the real time is noticeably longer than the simple answer.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot show you live flight schedules, real departure boards, or what time a specific flight leaves today. It has no connection to any airline and no live data at all. It cannot predict the wind on your day of travel, so it cannot tell you whether your particular flight will be fast or slow. It cannot plan layovers, book tickets, or tell you which airline flies a route. For all of that, check the airline's own timetable or a flight booking site.

It also cannot account for the things that make real flights slower than the simple maths: the taxi to the runway, the climb, the descent, holding patterns above busy airports, and the route the pilots actually fly. Treat the answer as a good estimate of the air time — the part of the journey spent cruising — and add time for everything else. Used that way, it is a genuinely useful planning tool. Used as a promise of the exact landing time, it will disappoint you, and that would be the calculator's fault only in the sense that no simple tool can know the wind next Tuesday.

Questions people ask

How fast does a plane fly?

Most large passenger jets cruise at about 800 to 900 kilometres per hour, and 850 is a good typical figure to use. Smaller regional planes cruise more slowly, often around 700 kilometres per hour. The fastest speed is only reached at cruising height — take-off, climb and landing are all much slower, which is why short flights take longer than the simple maths suggests.

Why is the return flight longer?

The main reason is wind. High-altitude jet streams flow from west to east, so eastbound flights get a tailwind and westbound flights push into a headwind. On a long route the difference can be an hour or more. The route itself can differ too — air traffic control may send the two directions along different paths, and one direction may face more queuing at a busy airport.

How do I work out arrival time?

Add the flight time to your departure time, then add the time-zone difference between the two cities. For example, a 3-hour flight leaving Dhaka at 10:00 arrives at 13:00 by Dhaka time; Kuala Lumpur is 2 hours ahead, so the local arrival time is 15:00. Remember to check whether the destination uses daylight saving, which shifts the difference by an hour for part of the year.

What is the longest flight in the world?

The longest scheduled passenger flights take about 18 to 19 hours in the air, covering around 15,000 kilometres without stopping. The Singapore to New York route is usually named as the longest. Scheduled times change when airlines adjust routes, so treat any record as current for now rather than forever — and remember that 18 hours in a seat is a very different experience from 3.

Do layovers count in flight time?

No. Flight time is the time spent in the air. A layover is time on the ground between flights, and it is not part of the flight time — but it is very much part of your journey time. When you plan a trip, add the layover to the flight times to get the real door-to-door figure, and allow extra time for changing gates, security checks and collecting bags.

Can I use this for any route?

Yes. Choose the route from the list if it is there, or pick 'My own distance' and type any distance in kilometres or miles. The calculator works the same way for a 45-minute hop and a 15-hour long-distance flight. For very short flights, remember that the climb and descent take a bigger share of the time, so the simple answer will be further from the scheduled time.
