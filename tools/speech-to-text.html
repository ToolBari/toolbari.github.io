What speech to text does

Speech to text turns your spoken words into written text. You tap the microphone, you talk, and your words appear in the box on the page — ready to copy into a message, a note or a document. It is the fastest way to get words out of your head when typing is slow, awkward or simply not what you want to do.

This page uses the speech recognition built into your browser — the same technology your phone uses when you dictate a message. There is nothing to install, nothing to upload and no account to make. The listening happens through the browser, and the words come back as text in real time, so you can watch the transcript grow as you speak.

It is worth knowing what the tool is and is not. It is a dictation tool: it hears words and writes them down. It does not understand what you mean, it does not fix your grammar, and it does not add punctuation reliably. The transcript it gives you is a first draft of your words, and a quick read before you copy it will catch the mistakes that matter.

How to use it

On a phone, open this page in Chrome, tap the big microphone button, and speak. The first time you do this, the browser will ask permission to use your microphone — tap Allow. Speak in a normal voice, in short sentences, and watch the words appear. When you are done, tap Stop, read the text, fix anything wrong by typing in the box, and tap Copy.

On a desktop, the flow is the same: open the page in Chrome or Edge, tap the microphone, and speak into your computer's microphone or a headset. A headset usually gives cleaner results than a built-in laptop microphone, because it sits closer to your mouth and picks up less room noise.

The transcript box is editable, which matters more than it sounds. Speech recognition makes mistakes — a wrong word here, a missing comma there — and the fastest fix is usually to tap the word and type the right one. Do that before you copy, and the text you paste elsewhere will be clean. Copy often, too: the page does not save your words, and a closed tab takes the transcript with it.

Which browsers work

Speech recognition works in Chrome on Android and on desktop, and in Microsoft Edge. Those browsers have the speech recognition feature built in, and this page uses it directly. If you open this page in one of them, the microphone button works straight away.

Some other browsers do not support speech recognition yet. Firefox is the best-known example: it has no built-in speech recognition feature, so this page cannot listen in Firefox no matter what you tap. Older browsers and some in-app browsers — the small browser inside a social media app, for example — often lack it too.

If your browser does not support it, this page tells you plainly instead of pretending to listen. You will see a clear message saying so, with which browsers work. There is no fake transcript, no spinning wheel that goes nowhere, and no recording that never comes back. Open the page in Chrome or Edge and the microphone will work.

One more honest note: in Chrome, your speech is sent to the browser maker's speech service to be turned into text — that is how the browser's recognition works. The words come back to your browser as text. This page itself does not record, store or keep your voice. If you are dictating something private or sensitive, keep that in mind, and consider typing instead.

Tips for clear results

The single biggest factor is noise. A quiet room gives a clean transcript; a television in the background, traffic through an open window, or another person talking nearby all confuse the recogniser, because it cannot tell which sounds are yours. Close the door, turn the television off, and move away from the fan if you can. Five seconds of quiet before you start helps more than any setting.

The second factor is pace. Speak steadily, at the speed you would use to explain something to a friend — not fast, not slow, and not in a rush. Short sentences work better than long ones, because the recogniser can place each sentence as a unit. Pause briefly between sentences. If you run sentences together in one long breath, the words blur and the transcript blurs with them.

The third factor is clarity. You do not need to shout — shouting distorts your voice and makes recognition worse. Speak clearly, finish your words, and hold the phone at a natural distance, about a hand's width from your mouth. Unusual names and technical words will still come out wrong sometimes; that is normal. Say the name slowly, or type it in afterwards — fixing one word by hand is faster than repeating a sentence five times.

What it is good for

Speech to text is best for first drafts and quick notes. Dictating a message while you walk, getting the bones of an essay down before you forget the idea, taking notes in a meeting without looking at a screen — these are the jobs where speaking beats typing, because the words come out faster than your fingers can move.

It is also good for people who find typing hard: a sore wrist, a small phone keyboard, a language you speak better than you write. Dictation removes the keyboard from the job entirely, and the editable transcript means you can fix the result without retyping the whole thing.

It is less good for finished text that must be exact. A formal letter, a set of instructions, a document with names and numbers — all of these need a careful read after dictation, because the recogniser guesses at exactly the words that matter most. Use dictation to get the draft, then read it once with your eyes before you send it. That two-step habit — speak, then read — is what makes speech to text genuinely useful instead of merely fast.

It is also handy for capturing ideas the moment they arrive. An idea for a story, a line for a song, a reminder to call someone back — spoken into the page in ten seconds, the words are saved as text before the idea fades. Many people keep this page open in a tab for exactly that reason: it turns a passing thought into a written note faster than any keyboard can.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is shouting. People raise their voice when the transcript goes wrong, as if volume were the problem. It is not — clarity and quiet are the problem, and shouting makes both worse. If a word comes out wrong, slow down and say it again, or type it in.

The second mistake is background noise. A television, a radio, a fan, a busy street — all of them feed words into the recogniser that you did not say. The transcript then contains sentences you never spoke, and cleaning them out takes longer than the dictation saved. A quiet room is not a luxury for this tool; it is the main requirement.

The third mistake is expecting perfect punctuation. The recogniser returns plain words, and punctuation is often missing or wrong. Saying "full stop" or "comma" sometimes works in Chrome, but it does not always, and it interrupts your flow. The safer habit is to dictate the words, then add punctuation yourself when you tidy the text. It takes a minute, and the result reads properly.

The fourth mistake is long sessions. The tool is made for short bursts — a few sentences at a time. Long sessions can pause, lose words, or stop without warning, and a lost paragraph is worse than a slow one. Speak a few sentences, stop, copy your text, and start again. Copying often is the cheapest insurance there is.

Questions people ask

Does it work on my phone?

Yes, in Chrome on Android it works well — tap the microphone, speak, and watch your words appear. Make sure the browser has permission to use your microphone, and speak in a quiet place for the best results.

Which browsers work?

Chrome on Android and on desktop works, and Microsoft Edge works too. Some other browsers do not support speech recognition yet. If your browser is one of them, this page will tell you plainly instead of pretending to listen.

Is my voice recorded or uploaded?

This page does not record or store your voice. In Chrome, your speech is sent to the browser maker's speech service to be turned into text, and the words come back to your browser. If that worries you, do not dictate private or sensitive words.

Why does it get words wrong?

Speech recognition guesses your words from sound, so background noise, mumbling and unusual names all cause mistakes. Speak steadily, keep sentences short, and read the result once before you copy it.

Can it add punctuation?

Mostly, no. The recogniser returns plain words, and punctuation is often missing or wrong. Saying full stop or comma sometimes works in Chrome, but the safest habit is to add punctuation yourself when you tidy the text.

Can I use it for long recordings?

It is made for short bursts — notes, messages and first drafts. Long sessions can pause or lose words, so speak a few sentences at a time, copy your text often, and save your work as you go.
