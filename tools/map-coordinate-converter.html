What are coordinates?

Every point on Earth can be described with two numbers: latitude and longitude. Latitude says how far north or south of the equator the point is, from 90 degrees south at the South Pole to 90 degrees north at the North Pole. Longitude says how far east or west of the prime meridian, the line that runs through Greenwich in London, from 180 degrees west to 180 degrees east. Together, the two numbers pin down one exact spot on the planet.

Those numbers can be written in two ways. Decimal degrees write them as a single number with a decimal point, such as 23.8103 for the latitude of Dhaka. Degrees-minutes-seconds splits each number into three parts, like hours, minutes and seconds on a clock: 23 degrees, 48 minutes and 37 seconds north. Both ways describe the same point; they are just different ways of writing the same number, and this converter switches between them.

You meet decimal degrees most often on phones and websites, because they are easy to type and easy for software to read. You meet degrees-minutes-seconds on paper maps, in older books, in legal land descriptions, and on some GPS devices. Knowing how to move between the two means you can use a coordinate from any source in any tool.

How to use this converter

First pick the direction of the conversion at the top: decimal degrees to degrees-minutes-seconds, or the other way around. For decimal degrees, type the latitude and the longitude as plain numbers. North and east are positive, south and west are negative, so London is latitude 51.5074 and longitude -0.1278, with the minus sign marking west. The converter checks that latitude is between -90 and 90 and longitude is between -180 and 180, and tells you plainly if a number is out of range.

For degrees-minutes-seconds, type the degrees, minutes and seconds for each coordinate and pick the hemisphere letter: N or S for latitude, E or W for longitude. Minutes and seconds must be below 60, because 60 seconds make a minute and 60 minutes make a degree. Tap Convert and the result appears below the buttons, with the converted point and two map links that you can open yourself to check the point on Google Maps or OpenStreetMap.

The copy button copies the converted coordinates as plain text. Nothing you type leaves your device: the conversion happens in your browser, nothing is stored, and the map links are only opened if you tap them. The page works by touch alone on a phone, with large boxes and buttons.

Worked examples: Dhaka, London, Sydney

Dhaka in Bangladesh sits at latitude 23.8103 and longitude 90.4125 in decimal degrees. To convert the latitude, take the whole degrees, 23, then multiply the remaining 0.8103 by 60 to get 48.618 minutes. Take the whole minutes, 48, then multiply the remaining 0.618 by 60 to get 37.08 seconds. The result is 23 degrees, 48 minutes, 37.08 seconds north. The longitude converts the same way: 90 degrees, 24 minutes, 45 seconds east.

London sits at latitude 51.5074 and longitude -0.1278. The latitude becomes 51 degrees, 30 minutes, 26.64 seconds north. The longitude is negative, which means west: the size is 0.1278 degrees, which is 0 degrees, 7 minutes, 40.08 seconds, and the hemisphere letter is W. Going the other way, 51 degrees 30 minutes 26.64 seconds north is 51 plus 30 divided by 60 plus 26.64 divided by 3600, which is 51.5074.

Sydney in Australia sits at latitude -33.8688 and longitude 151.2093. The latitude is negative, so it is south: 33 degrees, 52 minutes, 7.68 seconds south. The longitude is 151 degrees, 12 minutes, 33.48 seconds east. Notice how the minus sign and the hemisphere letter carry the same information: -33.8688 and 33.8688 south are the same point, written two ways, and the converter keeps them consistent.

Decimal degrees vs DMS: when to use each

Use decimal degrees when you are typing coordinates into a phone, a website or a spreadsheet. They are one number per coordinate, there is nothing to split apart, and a minus sign is quicker to type than a hemisphere letter. Most modern tools expect decimal degrees, and pasting them is straightforward: two numbers, separated by a comma, latitude first.

Use degrees-minutes-seconds when you are reading from a paper map, an old book, a legal description, or a device that displays them. DMS is also easier to say out loud and easier to remember in rough terms: "23 degrees 48 minutes north" is easier to hold in your head than "23.8103". Surveyors and sailors still use DMS for that reason, and many GPS devices let you choose which format to display.

Precision is the same in both formats if you keep enough digits: six decimal places in decimal degrees is about 10 centimetres on the ground, and two decimal places of a second is about 30 centimetres. For most everyday uses — finding a place, sharing a meeting point, marking a photo — four decimal places, about 10 metres, is plenty. More digits do not help if the original measurement was not that accurate.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is swapping latitude and longitude. Latitude comes first in almost every tool, and it is the number between -90 and 90. If your point lands in the ocean when it should be on land, check whether the two numbers are the right way around: Dhaka at 90.4125 north, 23.8103 east would be in the Arctic Ocean, which is a clear sign of a swap. The converter checks the ranges and warns you when latitude is outside -90 to 90, which catches most swaps.

The second mistake is a missing minus sign. Forgetting the minus on a western longitude puts the point in the eastern hemisphere, on the opposite side of the world: New York at longitude 74 east instead of 74 west lands in Central Asia. The third mistake is the wrong hemisphere letter: writing N for a place in the southern hemisphere, or E for a place in the west. The letter and the sign must agree, and the converter uses the letter you pick.

The fourth mistake is mixing minutes and seconds with decimals: writing 23 degrees 48.618 minutes is correct, but writing 23.48618 as if it were degrees and minutes is not — the digits after the decimal point in decimal degrees are fractions of a degree, not minutes. The fifth is rounding too early: round only at the end, after the conversion, or the last digits will drift by metres.

Reading a point on a map

After converting, always check the point on a map. The two map links below the result open the point on Google Maps and on OpenStreetMap, and you open them yourself by tapping. Checking takes a few seconds and catches every mistake above: if the pin lands where you expected, the conversion is right; if it lands in the sea or on another continent, something was swapped, signed wrong, or typed wrong.

When you copy coordinates from Google Maps, right-click or long-press the point and the coordinates appear as decimal degrees, latitude first. That is the format this converter expects, so you can paste them straight in. From OpenStreetMap, right-click a point and choose to show its address, or read the coordinates from the address bar of the map link. Different tools show coordinates in different orders, so always check which number is latitude before you trust a pair.

One more habit is worth building: write down which format you are using whenever you share coordinates with someone. A pair of numbers without a label is ambiguous — 23.8103, 90.4125 could be decimal degrees, and 23 48 37 could be degrees, minutes and seconds. A short label takes a second to write and saves the other person from guessing.

What this converter cannot do

This converter changes how a coordinate is written. It does not find your location, it does not know where you are, and it does not check whether a point is sensible — a point in the middle of the ocean converts just as happily as a point in a city. It cannot tell you the name of a place from its coordinates, and it cannot give you directions. For those jobs, open the map links and use the map itself.

It also cannot fix a coordinate that was wrong to begin with. If the numbers you typed came from a misread map or a swapped pair, the converted numbers will be just as wrong, in the other format. The converter checks ranges and format, not truth. That is why the map check matters: it is the only step that compares the numbers with the real world, and it is the step you should never skip when the point matters.

Questions people ask

What are decimal degrees?

Decimal degrees write latitude and longitude as single numbers with a decimal point, such as 23.8103 for Dhaka. North and east are positive, south and west are negative. They are the format most phones, websites and spreadsheets use, because they are easy to type and easy for software to read.

What is DMS format?

DMS stands for degrees, minutes and seconds. It splits each coordinate into three parts, like a clock: 23 degrees, 48 minutes and 37 seconds north. There are 60 minutes in a degree and 60 seconds in a minute. DMS appears on paper maps, in older books and on some GPS devices.

Which comes first, latitude or longitude?

Latitude comes first in almost every tool: latitude, then longitude. Latitude is the number between -90 and 90, and longitude is the number between -180 and 180. If your point lands in the wrong place, the two numbers may be swapped — the converter checks the ranges and warns you.

Why is my point in the ocean?

Usually because latitude and longitude are swapped, a minus sign is missing, or the hemisphere letter is wrong. A missing minus on a western longitude puts the point on the opposite side of the world. Check the point on the map links below the result: if the pin is wrong, re-check the order, the signs and the letters.

How do I copy coordinates from Google Maps?

Right-click the point on a computer, or long-press it on a phone, and the coordinates appear as decimal degrees, latitude first. Tap them to copy. You can paste them straight into this converter. On OpenStreetMap, right-click a point to see its coordinates, or read them from the map link.

What does the minus sign mean?

A minus sign means south for latitude and west for longitude. London's longitude is -0.1278 because it is west of the prime meridian, and Sydney's latitude is -33.8688 because it is south of the equator. In DMS format the same information is carried by the letters S and W instead of a minus sign.
