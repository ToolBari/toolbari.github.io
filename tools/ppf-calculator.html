What PPF is

The Public Provident Fund is a long-term savings scheme backed by the Government of India, run through banks and post offices. You deposit money each year, it earns interest set by the government, and after 15 years you get the whole amount back — deposits plus interest — completely tax-free. It is one of the safest places in India to put long-term savings, because the money is guaranteed by the government and the return, while modest, is certain.

PPF's special feature is its tax treatment, known as EEE: exempt, exempt, exempt. The deposit qualifies for a deduction under Section 80C, so it reduces your taxable income in the year you deposit it. The interest is exempt from tax every year as it accrues. And the maturity amount is exempt from tax when you receive it. Very few investments are tax-free at all three stages, and that is the main reason PPF has survived for decades despite rates that are lower than some alternatives.

The scheme is designed for patience. The 15-year lock-in is long, and that is the point: PPF is for money you do not need soon — retirement, a child's education, a long-term goal. The lock-in forces the discipline that most savers struggle to maintain on their own, and the compounding does the rest.

The rules that matter

The deposit limits are strict. You must deposit at least 500 every financial year, and at most 1,50,000. If you miss the minimum in a year, the account becomes dormant — you can revive it by paying 500 plus a penalty of 50 for each year you missed, but the account cannot be closed early even then. If you deposit more than 1,50,000, the excess earns no interest and does not count for the 80C deduction, so there is no point in over-depositing.

The rate is currently 7.1 percent a year, unchanged since April 2020, and it is reviewed every quarter by the government. It can go up or down at a review, and the calculator lets you change the rate to see what a different rate would do over 15 years. A single percentage point over 15 years is worth a great deal — which is why the rate is worth watching.

Interest is calculated on the minimum balance between the 5th and the last day of each month. That has a practical consequence: if you deposit by the 5th of the month, you earn interest on that deposit for the whole month, and if you deposit after the 5th, you do not. A lump sum deposited in April earns interest for the full year; the same amount spread across the year in monthly deposits earns less. This calculator uses annual compounding — interest on the new balance each year — which is close to the April lump-sum case. If you deposit monthly, your real return will be slightly lower.

Partial withdrawal is allowed from the seventh financial year, and loans are available from the third to the sixth year at 1 percent above the PPF rate. After 15 years, you can extend the account in blocks of 5 years, with or without further deposits. The account cannot be closed before 15 years except in specific hardship cases, and premature closure carries a penalty.

The power of 15 years

The 15-year lock-in looks like a drawback until you see what compounding does over that time. Deposit 1,50,000 a year for 15 years and you have put in 22,50,000. At 7.1 percent, the maturity value is 40,68,209 — the interest alone is 18,18,209, nearly as much as everything you deposited. The interest does most of its work in the later years: in year 1 the interest is 10,650, but in year 15 it is 2,69,695 — more than the year's deposit.

That is the shape of compounding: slow at first, then fast. The first five years feel unrewarding — the balance after five years is 9,25,701 on deposits of 7,50,000. The second five years add far more, and the last five add the most of all. Anyone who closes the account early, or stops depositing, gives up the years where the compounding is strongest. The discipline of 15 years is what buys the return.

The same maths works at any deposit level. A third of the deposit gives a third of the maturity value — 50,000 a year for 15 years gives 13,56,070 on deposits of 7,50,000. The rate of return is the same; only the scale changes. That is why the advice about PPF is always the same: start early, deposit what you can, and leave it alone.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is depositing after the 5th of the month. Interest is calculated on the minimum balance between the 5th and the last day, so a deposit made on the 10th earns no interest for that month. Over 15 years of monthly deposits, that habit costs a noticeable amount. Deposit by the 5th, or deposit a lump sum in April.

The second mistake is depositing more than 1,50,000 in a year. The excess earns no interest and gets no tax deduction — it just sits there. If you have more than 1,50,000 to save in a year, put the rest somewhere else.

The third mistake is missing the 500 minimum in a year and letting the account go dormant. It can be revived, but it takes a visit and a penalty, and the account cannot be closed early even while dormant. Set a reminder every April: deposit at least 500, even in a bad year.

The fourth mistake is assuming the rate will stay at 7.1 percent for 15 years. It has been unchanged since April 2020, but it is reviewed every quarter and it can change. Use the rate box to test what your maturity value looks like at 6.5 or 7.5 percent, so a rate change does not surprise you. And the fifth mistake is withdrawing early: partial withdrawals from year 7 are allowed, but every rupee taken out early is a rupee that stops compounding in the years when compounding matters most.

Questions people ask

What is the current PPF interest rate?

The PPF rate is 7.1 percent a year, unchanged since April 2020. It is reviewed by the government every quarter and may change at a review. This calculator starts at 7.1 percent and lets you change the rate to test other figures.

How much can I deposit in PPF each year?

A minimum of 500 and a maximum of 1,50,000 per financial year. Less than 500 makes the account dormant; more than 1,50,000 earns no interest on the excess and gets no tax deduction.

What is the lock-in period for PPF?

15 years. After that you can extend the account in blocks of 5 years, with or without further deposits. Partial withdrawal is allowed from the seventh financial year, and loans from the third to the sixth year.

Is PPF tax-free?

Yes — PPF has EEE status: the deposit qualifies for a deduction under Section 80C, the interest is exempt from tax, and the maturity amount is exempt from tax when you receive it.

Why does it matter if I deposit after the 5th of the month?

Interest is calculated on the minimum balance between the 5th and the last day of each month, so a deposit made after the 5th earns no interest for that month. Deposit by the 5th, or as a lump sum in April, to earn interest for the full month.

Can I close my PPF account before 15 years?

Not normally. Premature closure is allowed only in specific hardship cases and carries a penalty. The account is designed as a 15-year commitment — partial withdrawals from year 7 are the way to access money early.
