Recipe Cost Calculator

Work out what a recipe really costs before you cook it or sell it. Add each ingredient with its pack price and pack size, set the servings, and the calculator shows the cost per ingredient, the total cost and the cost per serving.

Add one row per ingredient. Prices are plain numbers in your own money — no currency symbol needed. Keep the units the same inside a row: grams with grams, millilitres with millilitres.

Prices change from shop to shop and week to week, so this calculator works from the prices you type in — it cannot look up real shop prices. The selling-price suggestion is a rule of thumb, not a promise: what people will pay depends on your area, your customers and what else is on offer nearby.

How to use this calculator

Add one row for each ingredient in your recipe. For each row you type four things: the name of the ingredient, how much of it you use, what the whole pack costs, and how big the pack is. The calculator then works out what the amount you used actually costs. For example, if you use 800 grams of chicken from a 1 kilogram pack that costs 240, you type 800, 240 and 1000 — and the calculator tells you the chicken in your dish costs 192. The maths is simple, but doing it for eight ingredients by hand is slow and easy to get wrong, which is what this page is for.

The one rule to remember is to keep the units the same inside a row. If the amount used is in grams, the pack size must be in grams too. If the amount used is in millilitres, the pack size must be in millilitres. Mixing grams and kilograms in the same row is the fastest way to get a nonsense answer — 800 grams out of a 1 kilogram pack is not 800 out of 1. Set the number of servings, tap the button, and you get the cost of every ingredient, the total cost of the whole recipe, the cost per serving, and a selling-price suggestion. The copy button copies the whole result as plain text, so you can paste it into a note or a message.

You can add up to 8 ingredient rows. If your recipe has more ingredients than that, group the small ones together. Put all the spices on one row called "spices" with their combined cost, or leave out a pinch of salt — it costs almost nothing and will not change the answer. Start with the expensive ingredients, because they decide the answer. Getting the chicken right matters far more than getting the salt right.

The costs beginners forget

Ingredients are only the start. The oil you fry the onions in costs money. The gas or electricity that simmers a curry for an hour costs money. The water, the washing up, the container you serve it in — all of it has a cost, even when no money leaves your pocket at the moment you cook. People who cost a recipe for the first time almost always forget these things, and then wonder why the dish that "cost 200 to make" leaves them no money at the end of the month.

Then there is your time. If you cook for two hours to sell four plates, those two hours are part of the cost, even if you enjoy the cooking. A home cook can ignore this — the meal is its own reward. A seller cannot. Time spent cooking is time not spent doing anything else, and a price that ignores it is a price that pays you nothing for your work. The same goes for packaging and delivery: boxes, bags, foil, and the ride to the customer all cost money, and they add up fast on a small order.

Waste is the quietest cost of all. You pay for the whole onion, but the skin goes in the bin. You pay for the whole chicken, but the bones do not reach the plate. Rice sticks to the pot, oil stays in the pan, and a little of everything is lost in chopping and serving. None of this is your fault — it is simply how cooking works — but the money is spent all the same. A simple habit that many small sellers use is to add about 10 percent on top of the ingredient total for the small things they cannot be bothered to price one by one. It is not exact, but it is much closer to the truth than pretending those costs do not exist.

This calculator prices ingredients only. It cannot count your gas, your time or your packaging, because those depend on your kitchen and your way of working. What it gives you is the solid starting point: the ingredient cost, worked out properly. Add the rest yourself, honestly, and you will know where you stand.

Pricing food to sell

The selling-price suggestion on this page uses the times-3 rule: take the cost per serving and multiply by 3. The idea is simple. One third of the price covers the ingredients. One third covers everything else that running the food costs — gas, packaging, delivery, and your time. One third is your profit. It is a starting point that small food sellers have used for years, not a law of nature, and it works best as a first guess that you then adjust.

It breaks in two directions. On very cheap dishes it can overshoot: a plate that costs 20 to make cannot sell for 60 if the stall next door sells the same thing for 40. Customers compare, and they will not pay half as much again for the same food. On very expensive dishes it can overshoot the other way: a rich dish costing 300 per serving would suggest a price of 900, which may simply be more than your customers will pay, however good the food is. And if you sell through a delivery app, the app takes its cut first — if that cut is a fifth of the price, the times-3 rule may not leave you enough.

So check the market before you fix a price. What do similar dishes sell for near you? If your times-3 price is far above the local price, the answer is not to charge more and hope — it is to find cheaper ingredients, use a smaller portion, or pick a different dish. If your times-3 price is far below the local price, you may be undercharging and leaving money on the table. Price is set by what people will pay, not only by what the food costs you. The calculator tells you the floor — the price below which you lose money. The market tells you the ceiling. Your real price lives between the two.

Another way to say the same thing is the food-cost percentage: the ingredients should usually be about 30 to 35 percent of the selling price for a small food business. That is the times-3 rule written differently — if ingredients are one third of the price, the percentage is about 33. If your ingredients come to more than 40 percent of the price, the margin is thin and one bad week can wipe it out. If they come to less than 25 percent, either your ingredients are very cheap or your price is high for the area — both worth a second look.

Keeping track as prices change

Food prices move. Chicken is dear one month and cheaper the next. Onion prices can jump in a week. A recipe costed in January may be wrong by March, and a seller who never re-costs can slide into selling at a loss without noticing. The fix is a small habit: re-cost your best-selling dishes every few weeks, or whenever you notice a big price change at the shop.

You do not need to re-cost everything. The expensive ingredients decide the answer, so watch the top three: usually the meat or fish, the oil, and the main starch. If those three have not moved much, the dish has not moved much. Keep a simple note — on paper or on your phone — of what you paid for the main ingredients each time you shop. After a few weeks you will see the pattern: which prices bounce around, which climb slowly, and which stay steady. That note is worth more than any single calculation, because it shows you the direction prices are moving before they move your profit away.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is pricing only the main ingredient. People cost the chicken and call that the cost of the dish, forgetting the oil, the onion, the spices and the rice. Those extras are small one by one, but together they can be a third of the total. A dish is the sum of everything in the pot, not just the star of the show.

The second mistake is forgetting the pack size. "The pack costs 240" means nothing until you know how big the pack is and how much of it you used. A 240 pack that you use half of costs you 120; the same pack used almost whole costs nearly the full 240. Always pair the price with the size — that is why this calculator asks for both, on every row.

The third mistake is mixing units: grams in one column and kilograms in the other, or millilitres against litres. The calculator cannot know what you meant — it divides the numbers you give it. A quick check before you tap the button saves a wrong answer: are the amount used and the pack size in the same unit? The fourth mistake is using one old price forever. Prices move, and a calculation from three months ago is a guess, not a cost. Re-cost the dishes you sell most, and re-cost them often.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot look up real shop prices. It works from the prices you type in, so the answer is only as good as your numbers. It cannot count gas, electricity, packaging, delivery or your time automatically — those depend on your kitchen, and you must add them yourself. It cannot tell you what customers will pay: the selling-price suggestion is a rule of thumb, and the real price depends on your area and your customers.

It also cannot handle a recipe with more than 8 ingredient rows on the page — group the small ingredients together if your recipe is longer than that. And it cannot judge quality: the cheapest ingredient is not always the best value, and a dish made with poor ingredients at a low cost is still a poor dish. Use this calculator for what it does well — honest, line-by-line ingredient costing — and bring your own judgment to everything else.

Questions people ask

How do I price a dish to sell?

Start with the cost per serving from this calculator, then multiply by 3 as a starting point: one third covers ingredients, one third covers gas, packaging, delivery and your time, and one third is your profit. Then check what similar dishes sell for near you. If your price is far above the local price, find cheaper ingredients or a smaller portion rather than charging more and hoping. The calculator tells you the floor below which you lose money; the market tells you the ceiling.

Should I count gas and electricity?

Yes, if you sell food — the gas that simmers a curry for an hour is a real cost, even if it is hard to measure per dish. A simple way is to add about 10 percent on top of the ingredient total for gas, water, washing up and the small things you cannot price one by one. If you only cook for your family, you can skip it: the ingredient cost is the number that matters for a home cook.

What is a good food-cost percentage?

For a small food business, ingredients should usually be about 30 to 35 percent of the selling price — that is the times-3 rule written as a percentage. Above 40 percent the margin is thin and one bad week can wipe it out. Below 25 percent, either your ingredients are very cheap or your price is high for the area. Both are worth a second look before you settle on a price.

How do I cost an ingredient I buy in bulk?

Exactly the same way: divide the bulk price by the bulk size to get the price per gram or per millilitre, then multiply by the amount you use. A 5 kilogram bag of rice costing 350 is 70 per kilogram, so 400 grams costs 28. Type the bulk price and the bulk size into the calculator and it does the division for you. Buying in bulk usually lowers the cost per serving — one reason to re-cost after a big shop.

What if prices change every week?

Re-enter the prices for the ingredients that moved and run the calculator again — it takes a minute. You do not need to re-cost everything: watch your top three expensive ingredients, usually the meat or fish, the oil and the main starch, because they decide the answer. Keep a simple note of what you paid each time you shop, so you can see which prices bounce around and which climb steadily.

Can I use this for baking too?

Yes — flour, butter, sugar and eggs work exactly the same way. For eggs, work out the price per egg first: divide the pack price by the number of eggs in the pack, then use the number of eggs as the amount and 1 as the pack size. A cake that uses 4 eggs from a pack of 12 costing 144 uses eggs worth 48. The same rule applies to anything sold by count rather than by weight.
