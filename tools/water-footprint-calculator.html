Water Footprint Calculator

This calculator estimates your daily water footprint in litres, split into the water you use directly at home and the hidden water in your food and clothes. It also shows a yearly total and five practical savings picked for your answers.

This calculator gives an estimate, not a measurement. Hidden-water figures are published averages for food and clothing, not measurements of your own meals, and direct-use figures are typical values for common taps and appliances. Your real use depends on your home, your habits and where you live.

What is a water footprint?

A water footprint is the total amount of fresh water used to support one person's daily life. It has two parts. The first part is direct water: the water you can see yourself using, from the shower, the toilet, the washing machine and the kitchen tap. The second part is hidden water: the water used to grow, make and move the food you eat and the clothes you wear. Most people are surprised by how large the second part is. For many people, hidden water is ten times bigger than direct water.

Hidden water is real water. Growing the feed for a cow takes water. Washing and dyeing cotton takes water. Making a cup of coffee takes water long before the kettle boils. None of that water comes out of your tap, so it never appears on a water bill, but it still came from a river, a lake or an underground store somewhere. A water footprint adds both parts together so you can see the full picture instead of only the part you can watch running down the drain.

Why does it matter? Fresh water is not endless in most places. Rivers run low in dry seasons, underground water levels fall when more is pumped out than rain puts back, and whole regions argue over shared rivers. Knowing where your water goes does not fix any of that by itself, but it does show you which changes actually help. A shorter shower saves real litres. Eating less beef saves far more. Guessing gets the order wrong; measuring, even roughly, gets it right.

How to use this calculator

Fill in the boxes at the top of the page with your own typical numbers, then tap the Calculate button. The four boxes at the top cover direct water: how long you shower each day, how many times the toilet is flushed, how many loads of laundry you do each week, and how many times a day you wash up. The boxes below cover hidden water: how many servings of beef, pork and chicken you eat in a week, how many eggs, how many cups of coffee and tea a day, and how many new cotton T-shirts and pairs of jeans you buy in a month.

The result shows three numbers: your direct use in litres per day, your hidden use in litres per day, and your total. It also shows a yearly total and a plain-words comparison with a typical range, plus five practical savings picked for your answers. If a box does not apply to you, put in zero. If you are unsure about a number, guess the nearest ordinary value; the result is an estimate either way, and a rough honest guess is more useful than a blank box.

On a phone, the page works by touch alone: type the numbers, tap Calculate, and read the result. The copy button copies your result as plain text so you can paste it into a note or a message. Nothing you type leaves your device, and nothing is saved unless you copy it yourself.

Worked example: one ordinary day

Take Maya, who lives in a flat with a normal shower and a washing machine. She showers for 8 minutes a day, flushes the toilet 5 times, does 3 loads of laundry a week, and washes up twice a day. Her direct water is 8 minutes times 9 litres a minute for the shower (72 litres), plus 5 flushes times 6 litres (30 litres), plus 3 loads times 50 litres a week divided by 7 days (about 21 litres a day), plus 2 washing-up sessions times 10 litres (20 litres), plus 12 litres a day for drinking, cooking and cleaning. That comes to about 155 litres of direct water a day.

Her hidden water is much larger. She eats 2 servings of beef a week (2 times 2,300 litres, divided by 7 days, is about 657 litres a day), 1 serving of pork (about 129 litres a day), 3 servings of chicken (about 279 litres a day), 4 eggs a week (about 114 litres a day), 1 cup of coffee (140 litres) and 1 cup of tea (30 litres) a day, and buys 1 new cotton T-shirt a month (2,700 litres divided by 30 days is 90 litres a day). That adds up to about 1,439 litres of hidden water a day.

Her total is about 1,594 litres a day, or about 582,000 litres a year. The hidden part is about nine times the direct part. That is normal. It is also why the savings list matters: cutting her shower by 2 minutes would save 18 litres a day, while swapping one beef serving a week for chicken would save about 235 litres a day. Both help, but the food change helps far more, and the calculator shows that clearly.

Direct water: where it goes in your home

The shower is usually the biggest single direct use. This calculator uses 9 litres per minute, a typical figure for a standard showerhead. A low-flow showerhead uses less, an old powerful one uses more, and a bath uses roughly 80 litres in one go. Toilet flushing comes next: this calculator uses 6 litres per flush, typical for a modern dual-flush toilet on a full flush. Older toilets can use 9 litres or more per flush, and a half flush uses about half.

Laundry is counted at 50 litres per load, a typical figure for a modern front-loading machine. Top-loading machines often use more, and a half-empty load uses nearly as much water as a full one, which is why waiting for a full load is one of the easiest savings. Washing up is counted at 10 litres per session for hand washing in a basin; leaving the tap running while you wash up can use several times that, and a full dishwasher load typically uses about 10 to 15 litres, which is often less than hand washing the same plates under a running tap.

The remaining 12 litres a day covers drinking, cooking and cleaning: the water you drink, the water in cooking and rinsing food, mopping floors, wiping surfaces and washing hands. It is a fixed typical figure, not something you type in, because most people cannot estimate it directly. If your home has a garden that you water, a car you wash, or a leaking tap, your direct use will be higher than this calculator shows, and a leak is worth fixing first because it wastes water day and night.

Hidden water: the water in your food and clothes

Hidden water figures come from published averages of the kind the Water Footprint Network publishes. They are averages across many farms and factories, not measurements of your own food. A serving of beef is counted at 2,300 litres because growing the feed crops, giving the animal drinking water and cleaning the farm all take water over the animal's life. Pork is counted at 900 litres a serving and chicken at 650 litres, because pigs and chickens need less feed and less time to grow than cattle. An egg is counted at 200 litres, a cup of coffee at 140 litres and a cup of tea at 30 litres.

Clothes carry hidden water too, mostly from growing the fibre and dyeing the cloth. A cotton T-shirt is counted at 2,700 litres and a pair of jeans at 7,500 litres. Those numbers look shocking until you remember that cotton is a thirsty crop and that a pair of jeans is heavy: the water went into growing the cotton, spinning it, dyeing it and washing it several times before it reached the shop. Buying fewer new clothes, wearing them longer and buying second-hand all cut this part of the footprint without changing anything about how you live day to day.

Two things are worth remembering about hidden water. First, the figures vary by place: beef raised on rain-fed grass uses water differently from beef raised on irrigated feed, and the published average sits between the two. Second, hidden water is not a reason to feel guilty about eating or getting dressed. It is a way to see which choices matter most, so that when you do decide to change something, you change the thing that actually moves the number.

Five practical ways to use less water

First, shorten your shower. Two minutes less a day saves about 18 litres a day at the typical flow rate, and you will not notice the difference after a week. Second, wait for a full load before running the washing machine or dishwasher. Third, fix dripping taps and running toilets: a tap dripping once a second can waste more than 10 litres a day, and a leaking toilet can waste far more without anyone noticing.

Fourth, look at the food side. You do not have to give up anything to make a difference: swapping one beef meal a week for chicken or a plant-based meal saves hundreds of litres a day on average, and eating the food you already buy instead of throwing part of it away saves all the water that went into growing it. Fifth, buy fewer new clothes and wear what you have longer. A T-shirt worn twice as long halves its water cost per wear. None of these steps is difficult, and together they can cut a typical footprint by a large amount without any hardship.

Common mistakes when estimating water use

The most common mistake is counting only the water from the tap. Direct use feels like the whole story because you can see it, but for most people it is the smaller part. A second mistake is the opposite: counting hidden water and then treating the total as exact. Hidden-water figures are published averages, and your own beef, coffee and jeans may have used more or less water than the average. The total is a guide to size and order, not a meter reading.

A third mistake is using one unusual week as typical. A week with guests, a holiday, or a big wash of bedding is not your normal week. Think about an ordinary week instead. A fourth mistake is comparing your number with someone in a different climate or country and feeling good or bad about it. People in hot, dry places shower more and water gardens; people in cold places may use more hot water. The useful comparison is with your own number over time, as you change habits and watch the total fall.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot measure your real water use. It cannot read your water meter, it cannot see your taps, and it cannot know how much water your food really used. The factors it uses are published averages, and your home and your meals will differ from the average in ways no calculator can know. It also does not count the water used to make the electricity you use, to build your home, or to run the services you use every day, so your full footprint is larger than the number shown here.

It also cannot tell you whether your water use is sustainable where you live. A litre used in a wet region with full rivers is not the same as a litre used in a dry region with falling water levels, and this calculator does not know your local situation. Use it to see the shape of your water use and to find the biggest savings, not as a score to compare with strangers. If you want a precise number for your home, read your water meter over a month; that is the only real measurement available to most people.

Questions people ask

What is a water footprint?

A water footprint is the total fresh water used to support your daily life: the water you use directly at home, plus the hidden water used to grow your food and make your clothes and other goods. Hidden water is usually much larger than direct water, so a footprint that counts only tap water misses most of the picture.

What is hidden water?

Hidden water, also called virtual water, is the water used to produce something before you buy it. Growing feed for cattle, watering cotton fields, and processing coffee all take water. That water never comes out of your tap, but it still came from a real river, lake or underground store, and it counts as part of your footprint.

How much water does a shower use?

A typical shower uses about 9 litres per minute, so an 8-minute shower uses about 72 litres. A low-flow showerhead uses less, an old powerful shower uses more, and a bath uses roughly 80 litres in one go. Shortening your shower by 2 minutes a day saves about 18 litres a day at the typical flow rate.

Does eating less meat really save water?

Yes, and usually more than any change at home. A serving of beef carries about 2,300 litres of hidden water on average, while a serving of chicken carries about 650 litres. Swapping one beef meal a week for chicken saves roughly 235 litres a day on average. You do not have to give up meat entirely for the saving to be real.

How can I cut water use at home?

Shorten showers, wait for full washing machine loads, fix dripping taps and running toilets, and turn the tap off while brushing teeth or washing up. On the hidden side, waste less food, eat beef less often, and buy fewer new clothes. The calculator lists five savings picked for your own answers.

Is my daily use high or normal?

Direct household use is often around 100 to 200 litres a day, while a total footprint including hidden water often falls between 2,000 and 5,000 litres a day. Both ranges are broad published estimates, not targets. The useful comparison is your own number over time, as you change habits and watch it fall.
