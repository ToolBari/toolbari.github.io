What net worth is

Net worth is the simplest summary of a person's finances: everything you own, minus everything you owe. If your assets add up to more than your liabilities, your net worth is positive; if you owe more than you own, it is negative. It is a snapshot at one moment, not a judgement — it moves every time you are paid, pay a bill, or watch an investment change value.

The number matters because it is honest in a way income is not. A high salary with high debts can sit beside a negative net worth, while a modest salary with steady saving builds a positive one. Income is what flows through your hands; net worth is what stays.

Net worth is also slow. It does not jump in a day, and a single snapshot says less than a year of them. That is why this tracker keeps a history: the direction of the line matters more than any one point on it.

What counts as an asset

An asset is something of value that you own: cash in the bank, savings, investments, a pension, a home you own, a car, and anything else you could sell for a meaningful amount. Use realistic values, not hopeful ones — a car is worth what someone would pay for it today, not what you paid for it new.

Everyday belongings usually do not belong in the list. Clothes, furniture and gadgets lose value quickly and are hard to sell for a predictable amount, so counting them inflates the total with numbers you could never realise. If an item would not fetch a meaningful sum in a sale, leave it out.

A home is the trickiest asset. It is usually the biggest number on the list, but it is also the least liquid — you live in it, and selling it means finding somewhere else to live. Include it at a realistic market value if you want the full picture, and remember that the mortgage on it belongs on the other side of the ledger.

What counts as a liability

A liability is anything you owe: a mortgage, a car loan, a student loan, a credit card balance, a personal loan, and any money you owe to family or friends. Enter the amount outstanding today, not the original amount borrowed — the tracker wants the debt as it stands now.

Regular bills are not liabilities in this sense. Next month's rent or electricity bill is an expense, not a debt, until you owe it. Only money you already owe belongs on the list — otherwise every future bill would count against you twice, once as a liability and once when you pay it.

Be honest about the small debts. A credit card balance of a few hundred, a store card, a buy-now-pay-later balance — they are easy to forget and they add up. The tracker is private, so there is no audience to impress and no reason to leave anything out.

Worked example: a first snapshot

Suppose you have 5,000 in savings, 2,000 in investments and a car worth 4,000. Your assets add up to 11,000. You owe 800 on a credit card and 3,000 on a personal loan, so your liabilities add up to 3,800. Your net worth is 11,000 minus 3,800, which is 7,200.

Now suppose you pay off the credit card in full. Your savings drop to 4,200, so your assets fall to 10,200 — but your liabilities fall to 3,000, and your net worth stays exactly 7,200. Paying a debt with savings does not change your net worth, because both sides fall by the same amount. What changes is the interest you will pay from now on, which is where the real gain lives.

Suppose instead that you save another 1,000 from your wages. Your assets rise to 12,000, your liabilities stay at 3,800, and your net worth rises to 8,200. Saving new money is what moves the number up; moving money between your own accounts does not.

Reading the chart over time

The chart shows one bar per snapshot, in the order you saved them. A rising line means your assets are growing faster than your debts; a falling line means the opposite. Short-term wobbles are normal — investments move, a big bill lands, a bonus arrives — so look at the direction over months, not the change between two neighbouring bars.

Save snapshots on a schedule and the chart becomes a habit. The first day of the month is easy to remember, and a year of monthly snapshots is enough to see a real trend. If you only save a snapshot when the news is good, the chart will flatter you — save on the schedule, whatever the number says.

A negative net worth on the chart is not a failure, especially early in a career or while paying off study debt. What matters is the direction: a negative number climbing toward zero is progress, and the chart shows that progress plainly.

Your data stays on your device

This tracker has no account, no sign-up and no server. Your lists and snapshots are stored in your browser's local storage, on the device you are using, and no copy is sent anywhere. The page makes no network requests for your data — it cannot, because there is nowhere to send it.

That privacy has a trade-off: the data lives only on this device, in this browser. Anyone with access to your unlocked device and browser can open the tracker and see the numbers, because there is no password. If you share a device, use the clear button when you are done, or keep the tracker on a device only you use.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is counting hope instead of value: a car at its purchase price, a home at its best-ever estimate, an investment at its peak. Use today's realistic values, and the tracker will tell you the truth.

The second mistake is forgetting the small debts. A store card here, a buy-now-pay-later balance there — they add up, and leaving them out flatters the total. List everything you owe, however small.

The third mistake is checking too often and reacting to noise. Investments move daily, and a net worth that falls for a week is not a crisis. Snapshot monthly, read the trend, and let the chart do its work over time.

What this tracker cannot do

This tracker cannot give financial advice. It adds up the numbers you enter and draws a chart — it cannot tell you whether to pay off a debt or invest, whether to buy a home or rent, or what your money should do next. Those decisions depend on your whole situation, and this page does not know it.

It also cannot value your assets for you. A home, a car or a collection is worth what someone will pay, and the tracker takes your word for it. Enter honest values, and the number it shows will be honest too.

Questions people ask

Is my financial data safe on this page?

Your data never leaves your device — it is stored in your browser only, with no account and no server. Nobody else can see it over the internet. The trade-off is that anyone with access to your unlocked device and browser can open the page, and clearing your browser data deletes the tracker data too.

What if my net worth is negative?

Then you owe more than you own, which is common early in a career, while studying, or after buying a home with a mortgage. It is a snapshot, not a verdict. Save snapshots monthly and watch the direction: a negative number climbing toward zero is real progress.

Should I include my house in my assets?

Yes, at a realistic market value, if you want the full picture — and include the mortgage in your liabilities. Remember that a home is not money you can spend without selling it and finding somewhere else to live, so read the number with that in mind.

How often should I update the tracker?

Once a month is a good rhythm. Update the values, save a snapshot, and let the chart build over time. Updating daily adds noise without information, because investments and balances move in small ways every day.

What is a good net worth?

There is no single good number — it depends on your age, income, debts and where you live. A better question is whether your net worth is moving in the right direction over months and years. Compare yourself with your own past snapshots, not with anyone else.

Does this tracker give financial advice?

No. It is an education and record-keeping tool: it adds up your assets and liabilities and shows the trend. It cannot tell you what to do with your money. For decisions about debt, investing or a major purchase, consider speaking with a qualified financial adviser.
