How the planner works

Add your team members with their weekly capacity — the hours each person has available for this work — then add the tasks with their estimated hours and an assignee for each. The planner adds up the hours per person, shows each person's load as a percentage of capacity with a bar, and warns you about anyone over 100 percent.

When someone is overloaded, the planner suggests a rebalance: it looks for the person with the most spare capacity and suggests moving enough hours to bring the overloaded person back to capacity. The suggestion is a starting point for a conversation, not an order — the planner does not move tasks by itself.

The plan is saved in this browser, so it is still there when you come back. Copy the plan summary to share it as text. The summary lists each person's load and the suggestions, which is usually enough for a team discussion about the week ahead.

What capacity really means

Capacity is not the number of hours in a working week. A person who works 40 hours a week does not have 40 hours available for project tasks: meetings, email, breaks, training and the small jobs that fill every day all take time. For most office work, the real capacity for planned tasks is noticeably less than the hours on the contract.

The honest way to set capacity is to start from the working week and subtract the fixed commitments. If someone spends roughly a day a week in meetings, their capacity is closer to 32 hours than 40. Part-time members enter their real weekly hours, and anyone on leave that week enters a lower number or zero.

Be careful with capacity for creative or difficult work, too. Hours are not all equal: four hours of deep focused work is not the same as four hours of routine tasks, and a week packed with hard problems is more tiring than a week of familiar ones. The planner counts hours; you must judge the difficulty.

Capacity also changes from week to week, and the planner should change with it. A member with a public holiday, a training day or a school event has less capacity that week, and entering the lower number keeps the plan honest. Update capacities when you update the task list, and the bars will stay trustworthy.

Worked example: Ana at 115 percent

Take a team of two. Ana has a weekly capacity of 40 hours and tasks totalling 46 hours. Ben has a weekly capacity of 40 hours and tasks totalling 30 hours. Ana's load is 46 divided by 40, which is 115 percent — overloaded. Ben's load is 30 divided by 40, which is 75 percent — room to spare.

The planner suggests moving about 6 hours from Ana to Ben, because Ben has 10 hours spare and Ana is 6 hours over capacity. After the move, Ana has 40 hours, which is 100 percent, and Ben has 36 hours, which is 90 percent. Both are within capacity, and Ben still has a little room for the unexpected.

Notice what the suggestion does not do: it does not say which task to move. Moving 6 hours might mean one task of 6 hours or two tasks of 3 hours, and the choice depends on skills, deadlines and who the task belongs with. The planner shows the size of the move; the team decides which work actually moves.

Reading the load bars

Each person's bar shows assigned hours against capacity. Under 80 percent, the bar is green and the person has room to spare — they can take on more work or cover for someone else. From 80 to 100 percent, the bar is amber: the person is nearly full, and any new work should wait or go elsewhere.

Over 100 percent, the bar is red and the person is overloaded: more hours are assigned than the week holds. That does not mean the work is impossible — people do work overtime — but it does mean the plan as written cannot be finished inside the week. Something has to move: a task, a deadline, or the expectation.

Watch the team total as well as the individual bars. If the team's total load is over 100 percent, rebalancing cannot fix it — the team as a whole has more work than hours. That is a different problem with a different answer, covered in the section below.

One more thing the bars show is fairness. When one person sits at 60 percent while another sits at 110, the team notices, even if nobody says so. A visible imbalance breeds resentment faster than a heavy week does, so the planner's real job is not arithmetic — it is making sure the load is shared fairly enough that everyone can see it is fair.

Rebalancing without breaking flow

Move whole tasks, not fractions of tasks. Splitting one task across two people creates handover work, duplicated effort and confusion about who owns the result. If a task is too big to move whole, split it into two real tasks with clear boundaries first, then move one of them.

Respect skills and context. Moving a task to someone with spare hours but no knowledge of the work can cost more time than it saves, because the new owner must learn the background first. The best moves are tasks the receiving person already understands, or tasks simple enough that anyone can pick them up.

Ask before you move. A rebalance imposed without discussion feels like punishment to the person losing work and pressure to the person gaining it. A short conversation — 'Ana is over, Ben has room, can this task move?' — takes a minute and keeps the team on side. The planner suggests; people decide.

Finally, rebalance early in the week, not at the end of it. A move made on Monday gives the receiving person the whole week to do the work; a move made on Thursday gives them a day and a half, and the task will probably slip anyway. Check the bars when the week starts, and fix the overload while there is still time to fix it.

When the whole team is overloaded

Sometimes the team total is over 100 percent and no rebalance can fix it, because there are simply more hours of work than hours in the week. The honest answer then is not to work harder — it is to do less. Drop a task, delay a deadline, or say no to new work until the backlog clears.

Cutting scope is usually better than cutting quality. A task delivered late but well is worth more than a task delivered on time but rushed, and a team that is always overloaded will eventually deliver neither. Protect the important work by naming what will wait, and tell the people affected early.

If overload is permanent rather than a one-off week, the problem is staffing, not planning. No planner can make a team of three do the work of four indefinitely. Use the planner's numbers to make the case: a team total that stays above 100 percent week after week is evidence, not opinion.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is entering 40 hours of capacity for everyone, as if meetings and leave did not exist. Capacity that ignores reality produces bars that look fine and a team that is quietly drowning. Enter the hours genuinely available for this work.

The second mistake is forgetting the small tasks. A dozen half-hour jobs add up to a full day, and a plan that counts only the big tasks will always underestimate the load. Add the small tasks too, or add one task called 'small jobs' with the total hours they take.

The third mistake is rebalancing by spreadsheet alone. The planner shows hours, but it cannot see skills, motivation, or who is already tired from last week. Use the numbers as the starting point for a conversation, and let the people involved have the final say.

Questions people ask

What is a good load percentage?

Aim for under 100 percent, and ideally 80 to 90 percent, so there is room for the unexpected. A week planned to exactly 100 percent leaves no space for illness, urgent requests or tasks that take longer than estimated.

What counts as capacity?

The hours a person has available for this work in the week, after meetings, leave and other fixed commitments. It is usually less than the hours on their contract, so enter the realistic number rather than the nominal one.

Should I include part-time members?

Yes, with their real weekly hours. A part-time member with 20 available hours is entered with a capacity of 20, and the planner works out their load percentage against that figure, not against a full week.

What if someone is at 150 percent?

Move work, move a deadline, or drop a task — sustained overload burns people out and the quality of the work falls. The planner suggests moves to people with spare capacity, but if nobody has room, the work itself must shrink.

Does the planner move tasks by itself?

No. It suggests how many hours to move and to whom, based on spare capacity. You decide which tasks actually move, because only you know the skills, deadlines and context involved.

Is the data saved anywhere?

Only in your browser, using its local storage. Nothing is sent to any server and there is no account. Clearing the browser's stored data will remove the plan, so copy the summary if you need it elsewhere.
