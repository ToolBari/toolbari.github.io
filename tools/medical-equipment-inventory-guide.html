What this tool does and who it is for

A medical equipment inventory organizer is a list of the devices a clinic owns, with the dates that matter for each one: when it was bought, when it was last serviced, and when the next service is due. You add each device, pick a service interval, and the page works out the next service date and shows which devices are due soon or overdue. The list is sorted so the most urgent device sits at the top.

This page is made for small clinics, dental practices, physiotherapy rooms, and any small practice that owns a handful of devices and wants to stop relying on memory. It is also useful for a practice manager who has just taken over and needs to build a list of what the practice owns and when each item was last checked.

The organizer covers the devices a small practice usually owns: diagnostic devices, monitors, sterilizers, imaging devices, laboratory devices, and general equipment. Each device gets a name, a type, a last service date and a service interval in months. The page does the date maths and the sorting; you provide the facts.

Everything stays in your browser. There is no account, no server, and no one else who can see your inventory. The copy button turns the list into plain text you can paste into a document, save in a note, or print and pin up in the equipment room. The list is there when you come back, because it is saved on your device.

How to use it, step by step

Start by adding your first device. Type its name, such as Blood pressure monitor 2 or Autoclave, pick the device type, enter the date it was last serviced, and pick the service interval in months. Press Add device, and the entry appears in your list with its next service date, the days left, and a status: ok, due soon, or overdue.

Work through the list from the top. It is sorted by next service date, nearest first, so the device that needs booking soonest is always first. When a device is serviced, remove the old entry and add a fresh one with the new service date, so the next due date is worked out from the real date.

Use the copy button to take the inventory with you. It writes out every device, its last service, its next service and its status, as plain text. Paste it into a document for a staff meeting, or print it and keep it in the equipment room. The clear button empties the list, so only use it when you are ready to start fresh.

  • Add every device the practice owns, even the small ones, because a missed service on a small device still matters
  • Use the service interval from the maker's manual, not a guess, and check local rules for anything the manual does not cover
  • Update an entry on the day a service happens, not at the end of the month
  • Check the list at the start of each month, and book any device that is due soon

What the output means

The next service date is the last service date plus the interval you picked. A device last serviced on 10 January with a 6-month interval is next due on 10 July. The page adds whole months, and if the target month is shorter than the starting month, it uses the last day of that month, so a service on 31 January with a 1-month interval falls due on 28 February in a normal year.

The days left is the count from today to the next service date. A positive number means the service is still ahead. Zero means it is due today. A negative number means the service is overdue, and the device should be booked as soon as possible. The status follows from that count: due soon means the service falls within the next 30 days.

The order of the list is the most practical part. Because the nearest service sits at the top, a practice manager can see in seconds which device to book next, without opening a spreadsheet or a filing cabinet. That is the whole point of the tool: not the maths, but the order it puts your devices in.

Nothing on this page decides when a device must be serviced. The interval is yours to set, and it should come from the maker's manual and your local rules. The page counts days from the dates you enter; it does not know what your devices need, and it never claims to.

A worked example

Take a small clinic with three devices. A blood pressure monitor was last serviced on 15 January, with a 12-month interval. An autoclave was last serviced on 10 March, with a 6-month interval. A patient monitor was last serviced on 20 June, with a 3-month interval. All three go into the organizer, one at a time.

The next service dates are worked out by adding the intervals. The blood pressure monitor is next due on 15 January next year. The autoclave is next due on 10 September. The patient monitor is next due on 20 September. The list sorts itself by date, so the autoclave sits at the top, then the patient monitor, then the blood pressure monitor.

Suppose today is 20 August. The autoclave has 21 days left, so its status is due soon: time to book the service visit. The patient monitor has 31 days left, so it is still ok, but it will be due soon within days. The blood pressure monitor has months left, so it can wait.

Now suppose the autoclave is serviced on 5 September, five days early. The old entry is removed and a fresh one is added with 5 September as the last service date and the same 6-month interval. The next due date becomes 5 March, and the list re-sorts itself. The record stays honest because it follows the real service date.

Tips for a good result

Start with the devices that matter most if they fail. A sterilizer that stops working closes a clinic; a spare thermometer that stops working is an inconvenience. List the critical devices first, then work down to the rest, so the most important entries are in the list even if you run out of time.

Keep the maker's manual with the record. The service interval, the calibration steps and the warning signs all live in the manual, and the manual is the authority this page can never be. If the manual and your memory disagree, trust the manual.

Book a service as soon as a device shows due soon. Service visits take time to arrange, and a device that is due in 21 days can easily become a device that is overdue if the booking waits a week. The due soon status is a booking signal, not a waiting signal.

  • Record the serial number in the device name if you own two of the same device, so you can tell them apart
  • Note calibration dates in the same way as service dates, with their own interval
  • Keep a printed copy of the inventory in the equipment room, where staff can see it
  • Review the whole list once a quarter, and remove devices the practice no longer owns

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This organizer cannot tell you when a device needs servicing. The interval you pick should come from the maker's manual and your local rules. If you pick a wrong interval, the page will happily count days toward a wrong date, and it has no way to know.

It cannot check a device, calibrate it, or prove it is safe. It holds dates and names, nothing more. A device that is in date can still be faulty, and a device that is overdue can still be working. The page tracks the schedule; the checking is done by qualified people with the right tools.

It cannot keep your records safe for you. Your inventory lives in this browser only, which means it is lost if the browser data is cleared or you switch devices. Use the copy button regularly and keep the text somewhere safe. For official records, keep the maker's own paperwork as well.

This is an inventory organizer, not a medical device register and not medical advice. Service intervals come from the device maker's schedule and your local rules, not from this page: always follow the maker's instructions and local requirements, and keep the maker's own records as your official record.

Questions people ask

What equipment should a small clinic list first?

List the devices the clinic cannot work without: the sterilizer, the main diagnostic devices, and anything patients depend on at every visit. Then add monitors, laboratory devices and general equipment. Listing the critical devices first means the most important service dates are covered even if the rest of the list takes a while.

How often should medical devices be serviced?

Follow the maker's service schedule in the device manual, and check your local rules, because some devices have legal service requirements. Intervals vary widely: some devices need a check every few months, others once a year. This page lets you set any interval, but the right interval comes from the maker, not from this page.

Can I set reminders for calibration dates?

Yes, in the same way as service dates. Add the device with its last calibration date and the calibration interval, and the page works out the next calibration date and shows it in the list. If a device has both a service date and a calibration date, add it twice, once for each, with a clear name for each entry.

Does it store my data online?

No. Your inventory is saved in your own browser, on your own device, and nothing is uploaded anywhere. There is no account and no server. Use the copy button to move the list somewhere safe, because the browser copy is lost if the browser data is cleared.

Can I print the full inventory?

Yes. Press the copy button to turn the inventory into plain text, then paste it into any document or note and print it from there. The copied text includes every device, its last service, its next service and its status, so the printed page is a full inventory.

What records should I keep for each device?

Keep the device name and serial number, the purchase date, the last service and calibration dates, the service interval, and the name of the person or company that did the service. Keep the maker's manual with the record. This page holds the dates; keep the paperwork in a folder alongside it.
