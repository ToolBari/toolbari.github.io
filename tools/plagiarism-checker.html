What this plagiarism checker is

Plagiarism means presenting someone else's words or ideas as if they were your own. It can be deliberate, but it is usually an accident: you read a source, take notes, start writing, and a sentence from the source slips into your draft almost word for word. You paste a paragraph from your notes and forget the notes were copied from a website. None of these moments feels like cheating when it happens, but a reader, a teacher, or a checker will still see copied words on the page.

This page is a free self-check tool, and it is honest about what it does. It looks inside the text you paste and finds phrases that repeat within it, and it can compare two texts you paste and tell you how similar they are. Everything runs in your browser on your own device, so your text is never uploaded, never stored, and never sent anywhere. What this page does not do matters just as much: a static page cannot scan the whole web. It has no index of websites and no database of books and journals, so any page that claims to give you a full web plagiarism score from inside the browser alone is not telling the truth. This page checks your text against itself and against text you paste, and the guide below shows you how to do a real web check with the free tools that actually can.

How to use the checker

Using the checker takes less than a minute, on a phone or on a desktop computer. Paste or type your text into the first box. If you want to compare it with another text, paste the second text into the second box: a source you read, an earlier draft of the same piece, or shared notes you want to make sure you have not leaned on too closely. Then tap or click the check button, and the report appears below it.

The report has two parts. The first lists the repeated phrases found inside your text, each with the number of times it appears. The checker looks for phrases of five words or more, because shorter word combinations repeat in all writing and flagging them would only add noise. A repeated phrase is not proof of copying. It is a prompt to look closer: read the phrase, find where it appears, and ask whether it is there because you wrote it twice or pasted it from somewhere.

Check one section at a time if your document is long, so the report stays easy to read. The copy button copies the whole report to your clipboard, so you can work through the flagged phrases one by one.

What it can do

The repeated phrase check is the heart of this tool, and it is useful for a problem every writer has. Long documents drift. You write an introduction, then a section three pages later says the same thing in the same words, and you never notice because you wrote the two parts on different days. The checker catches that. It also catches the classic copy and paste accident: a paragraph pasted twice, a block of text moved to a new spot while the old copy stayed behind, or a sentence duplicated while you were reorganising. These are small mistakes, but they make a document look careless, and a reader notices them even when the writer does not.

The comparison check is useful in a different way. Paste your draft in the first box and a source you used in the second box, and the similarity score tells you how close the two are. A high score is a warning to go back and rewrite the matching parts properly, in your own words and your own structure. A low score is reassuring but not a guarantee, as the next section explains. You can also compare two versions of your own work, before and after a rewrite, to see how much actually changed.

What it cannot do

This tool checks your text against itself and against text you paste. It cannot scan the web — no page like this can. That sentence is the honest limit of this page, and it is worth understanding properly, because it is the line that separates a real tool from a fake one. A web check needs a huge index of web pages, books, and journals, plus the servers to compare your text against all of them in seconds. That is what the big checking services run, and it is not something a single web page can carry. If a page promises you a full web plagiarism percentage with no upload, no account, and no waiting, it is showing you a made-up number.

There are other limits too. This tool cannot tell whether a matching phrase is copied or coincidental. Common phrases, standard definitions, and ordinary ways of saying something repeat across perfectly honest writing, and no checker can know the difference on its own. It cannot detect paraphrased copying, where someone keeps the ideas and the structure of a source but changes the words, because the words no longer match. It cannot judge intent, and it cannot tell you whether a match matters. The similarity score compares words, not ideas or meaning. Two texts can say the same thing with different words and score very low. Treat every result from this page as a list of places to look, not as a final answer. The final answer comes from reading the flagged passages yourself and deciding, honestly, whether the words are yours.

How web plagiarism checkers work

A real web plagiarism checker works in two stages. First, it holds an index: a huge collection of web pages, articles, books, and student papers that it has gathered over time. Second, when you submit your text, it breaks the text into pieces and searches that index for matching pieces, then shows you where each match was found. The quality of a checker depends mostly on the size and freshness of its index, which is why the well known services all run on large servers. Free web checkers use the same idea with a smaller index or a limit on how much text you can check at once, and for most everyday writing that is enough.

Three free web checkers are widely used and easy to start with. Copyscape checks a web address or pasted text against the web and shows you the pages that contain matching content, which makes it a good choice when your writing is already published online. DupliChecker lets you paste text and check it against web sources, and it is often the simplest option for a quick check of a draft. Quetext also works from pasted text and highlights the matching parts, which makes it easy to see exactly which sentences need your attention. All three offer a free check with limits, and all three are real services with real indexes, which is why this page recommends them instead of pretending to do the job itself.

Using a web checker properly matters as much as choosing one. Check your finished draft, not an early version, so you are not fixing passages you will rewrite anyway. If the free check has a word limit, split your document into sections and check each one in turn. When the results come back, read every match instead of staring at the overall score. A match might be a quotation you already credited, a standard phrase, or a genuinely copied passage, and only reading it tells you which.

How to write without copying

The best way to pass a plagiarism check is to write in a way that makes copying unlikely in the first place, and the habit that helps most is also the simplest: take your notes in your own words. When you read a source, do not copy its sentences into your notes. Read a paragraph, close the source or look away from it, and then write down what it meant in the words that come naturally to you. If you cannot explain it without looking, you have not understood it yet. Read it again, and try once more. Notes written this way are already half of your draft, and they are yours from the start.

When you do need the exact words of a source, use them openly. Put them in quotation marks, keep them short, and say where they came from. A short quotation with credit is honest writing. What causes trouble is the middle ground: sentences that are almost the source's words, with a few words swapped, presented as if they were yours. That is still copying, and changing a few words does not change that. If a passage is worth using, either quote it properly or rewrite it properly, with your own sentence structure, your own examples, and your own order of ideas.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is changing a few words and calling the result original. Swapping some words for synonyms while keeping the sentence structure, the order of ideas, and the examples of the source is not paraphrasing. It is copying with a thin disguise, and a reader who knows the source will recognise it at once. Real paraphrasing changes the structure: start with the conclusion instead of the evidence, split one long sentence into two short ones, or explain the idea with an example from your own experience.

A second mistake is copying from your own earlier work without thinking about it. Reusing a paragraph you wrote last year in a new assignment can still be a problem, because the new piece is supposed to be new work. A third mistake is trusting a single number: a low score does not prove your work is original, and a high score does not prove it is copied. A fourth is checking only once, at the very end. Writing changes as you revise, so check after each major revision, and check the final version one last time before you send it.

Questions people ask

Can this page check my text against the whole web?

No. A page like this runs in your browser and has no access to the web, so it cannot compare your words with pages on the internet. It checks your text against itself and against a second text you paste. The guide on this page shows how to use the free web checkers for a real web check.

What does the repeated phrase check actually find?

It finds phrases of five words or more that appear at least twice in your text. A repeated phrase is often a sign that you pasted the same passage twice, or that you leaned on one source too heavily. Short everyday phrases are not flagged, because common word combinations repeat in all writing.

How does the similarity score work?

The score compares the words in your two texts and shows how much they overlap as a percentage. Two identical texts score 100, and two texts with no words in common score 0. It is a simple word overlap measure, not a judgement about whether copying happened.

Is my text sent anywhere when I use this checker?

No. The check runs entirely in your browser on your own device, and your text never leaves the page. Nothing is uploaded, stored, or shared. You can close the tab and your text is gone.

What is a safe similarity score between two texts?

There is no single safe number. A high score between your draft and a source you read means you should rewrite the matching parts in your own words. A low score does not prove your work is original either, because reworded copying still counts as copying. Use the score as a prompt to review, not as a verdict.

How do I check my work against the web for free?

Paste your finished text into a free web checker such as Copyscape, DupliChecker, or Quetext, which compare your words with pages on the internet. Free versions usually limit how much text you can check at once, so check your work in sections. Always read the matches yourself before you decide what to fix.
