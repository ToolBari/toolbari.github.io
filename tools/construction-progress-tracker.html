What this tracker does and who it is for

A construction project is a long list of jobs that must happen in order, and the easiest way to lose control of it is to stop watching the dates. This tracker keeps the watch for you: you log each milestone with the date it was planned and the date it actually happened, plus how complete it is, and the tracker shows the overall percent complete and which milestones have slipped.

It is built for anyone running a build — a homeowner managing their own extension, a small contractor juggling two jobs, or a site manager who wants a simple record without a full project system. You do not need any special knowledge: if you can name the jobs and guess their dates, you can use this tracker.

What it will not do is chase your builder or check the work for you. The tracker only knows what you tell it. If you mark a job 90 percent complete for a month, the tracker will believe you. Its value is that it makes the truth visible: slipped milestones show up in red, and the overall percent stops you from feeling ahead when you are behind.

How to use the tracker on your phone or computer

Add one milestone at a time. Type the name — Foundations poured, Roof on, First fix electrics — then pick the planned date and the percent complete so far. Leave the actual date blank until the milestone is really finished; when it is done, that is the date you record. Tap Add milestone and it joins the list.

The overall percent is the average of all your milestones' percents, shown as a number and a progress bar. Below it, the slipped list names any milestone whose planned date has passed while it is still unfinished and has no actual date. A slipped milestone is not a disaster — it is a prompt to ask what happened and what the new date is.

Your milestones are saved only in this browser, so they are still here when you come back tomorrow. Nothing is uploaded or shared. The copy button copies a plain-text summary of the whole project, which you can paste into a message to your builder or keep with your papers. The delete button on each row removes one milestone; the clear button removes them all.

Picking milestones that matter

A good milestone is a visible result, not a vague phase. Foundations poured is a milestone — you can stand on it and see it. Groundworks is not, because nobody can agree when groundworks ends. Pick milestones you can point at: walls up to first floor, roof watertight, windows in, plastering done, kitchen fitted.

Pick enough milestones to see progress, but not so many that updating them becomes a chore. For a small build, eight to twelve milestones is usually right. Fewer than five and the tracker tells you almost nothing between updates; more than twenty and you will stop updating it, which is worse than having no tracker at all.

Put the milestones in the order the work must happen, and give each one a planned date you actually believe. A planned date that everyone knows is impossible teaches the team to ignore the tracker. A realistic date that slips is useful information; a fantasy date that slips is just noise.

Updating it honestly

Update the tracker on the same day each week, from what you saw on site — not from what the builder told you on the phone. Walk the site, look at each milestone, and set the percent to what is really done. A job that is half done is 50 percent, even if the builder says it is nearly finished.

Record the actual date the day a milestone finishes, not a week later from memory. Dates blur quickly, and a record built from memory is a record you cannot trust when you need it — for example, when you are working out how far behind the build really is.

Resist the temptation to mark a job 90 percent complete and leave it there. The last ten percent of a job — the snagging, the tidying, the final fix — often takes longer than the first ninety, and a milestone stuck at 90 percent for a month is the clearest sign a job has stalled. If it is stuck, say so: set the real percent and let the slipped list do its job.

What to do when the build slips

First, find out why. A slipped milestone can mean bad weather, a late delivery, a builder stretched across two jobs, or a problem nobody told you about. The reason matters more than the delay itself, because the reason tells you whether the next milestone will slip too.

Second, agree a new date in writing. A slipped milestone without a new date is a milestone that will slip again. Ask the builder for the new date, write it down, and update the tracker — not by deleting the old planned date, but by recording what actually happened so the record stays true.

Third, look at what the slip does to the milestones after it. Jobs that depend on the slipped one — plastering after the roof, kitchen fitting after the electrics — move too. Update their planned dates as well, or the tracker will show a schedule that no longer exists. A tracker that shows the real schedule, even a late one, is useful; a tracker that shows a fantasy schedule is not.

Keep a short written record of every slip and every new date you agree. A few lines in a notebook — the milestone, the old date, the new date, the reason — protect you if the delay becomes a dispute about payment or about who caused it. Memories blur and builders change their story; a dated note does not.

A worked example: a small extension over twelve weeks

Imagine a small extension with six milestones: foundations (planned week 2), walls to first floor (week 4), roof watertight (week 6), windows in (week 7), plastering (week 9) and kitchen fitted (week 12). By week 6, the first three are done and the roof is 80 percent complete — the overall percent is the average of 100, 100, 100, 80, 0 and 0, which is 63 percent.

In week 7 the roof finishes, but the windows are late: the supplier delivers in week 8 instead. The windows milestone slips, the tracker flags it, and the plastering date moves from week 9 to week 10. The overall percent at week 8 is the average of 100, 100, 100, 100, 0 and 0 — 67 percent — and the slipped list names the windows and the knock-on delay to plastering.

Nothing in that example is a disaster, and that is the point. The tracker did not prevent the delay, but it made it visible the week it happened, with the new dates recorded. Without the tracker, the same delay would have surfaced in week 10 as a surprise.

Common mistakes that make tracking useless

The first mistake is marking a job 90 percent done for a month. The last part of a job takes longer than people expect, and a percent that never moves hides a stall. If a milestone has not moved in two weeks, something is wrong — find out what before updating the percent again.

The second mistake is tracking money but not time. A build can be on budget and three weeks late, or on time and over budget, and the two tell different stories. This tracker follows time and completion; keep your spending record separately and check both together once a week.

The third mistake is letting the tracker go stale. A tracker updated once a month is a history book, not a tool. Pick a day, walk the site, update the percents, and record the actual dates. Ten minutes a week keeps the tracker honest — and an honest tracker is the difference between knowing your build is late and finding out when it is too late to fix it.

Questions people ask

How do I track construction progress?

Log milestones with planned dates and update their percent complete each week from what you see on site. This tracker averages the percents into an overall figure and flags any milestone whose planned date has passed unfinished.

What is a good milestone?

A visible result you can point at: foundations poured, roof watertight, windows in. Vague phases such as groundworks make poor milestones because nobody can agree when they end. Pick eight to twelve milestones for a small build.

How often should I update it?

Once a week, on the same day, from a walk around the site. Record the actual date on the day a milestone finishes. A tracker updated monthly is a history book; a tracker updated weekly is a tool.

What if the build is behind?

Find out why, agree a new date in writing, and update the planned dates of the jobs that depend on the slipped one. A late schedule you can see is useful; a fantasy schedule is not.

Can I share the tracker?

The copy button copies a plain-text summary of the whole project, which you can paste into a message or keep with your papers. The tracker itself lives only in your browser and is not shared with anyone.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your milestones are saved only in this browser using local storage. Nothing is uploaded, synced or shared, and clearing your browser data will remove them — so copy the summary if you want a lasting record.
