What this tool does and who it is for

A breeding record keeper is a simple list of which animals were bred, when, and when their young are due. For each animal you record a name, the species, and the breeding date. The page then works out the expected due date from the typical gestation length for that species, counts the days left, and sorts the list so the nearest births come first. That is all it does, and for a small farm it is enough.

This page is made for small livestock keepers: a few cows, a flock of sheep or goats, a pen of pigs, or a horse or two. It is also useful for anyone who keeps breeding records on paper and wants a list that does the date maths for them. If you have ever lost track of which animal is due when, or found yourself counting days on a calendar, this tool takes that job off your hands.

The record keeper holds five species: cow, sheep, goat, pig and horse. Each has a typical gestation length built in: about 283 days for a cow, 147 for a sheep, 150 for a goat, 114 for a pig, and 340 for a horse. These are averages, labelled as such on the page, and real births vary around them. The page never claims a due date is certain, and it never pretends to be a vet.

Everything stays in your browser. There is no account and no one else sees your records. The copy button turns the list into plain text you can paste into a message, save in a note, or print and pin up in the barn. The list keeps working when you come back, because it is saved on your device.

How to use it, step by step

Start by adding your first animal. Type a name or tag number, such as Daisy or Ewe 14, pick the species, and enter the breeding date. Press Add animal, and the record appears in your list with its expected due date, the days left, and a status: upcoming, due soon, or overdue. Add each animal the same way, one at a time.

Read the list from the top. It is sorted by due date, nearest first, so the animal that needs watching soonest is always at the top. When a birth happens, remove that animal from the list, or keep the record and note the birth in your own notes. The list is yours to manage: the page does not decide what happens next.

Use the copy button to take the list with you. It writes out every animal, its breeding date, its due date and the days left, as plain text. Paste it into a message to a helper, save it in a note, or print it. If the season is over, the clear button empties the list so you can start fresh.

  • Enter the breeding date as the date the animal was actually bred, not the date you noticed it
  • Use a name or tag number you will recognise months later, when the due date comes round
  • Check the list at least once a week, and more often as a due date approaches
  • When a birth happens, record the real date in your own notes, because the real date is what matters for next time

What the output means

The expected due date is the breeding date plus the typical gestation length for the species. For a cow bred on 1 March, the due date is about 283 days later, in early December. The word expected matters: the due date is a guide, not a promise. Most births fall within a week or two of the date, and some fall further away.

The days left is the count from today to the due date. A positive number means the birth is still ahead. Zero means the due date is today. A negative number means the due date has passed, and the animal is overdue. The status follows from that count: due soon means the birth is within the next 14 days, and overdue means the date has passed with no birth recorded.

The order of the list is the most practical part. Because the nearest due date sits at the top, you can see at a glance which animal needs a clean pen, extra feed, or closer watching. That is the whole point of the tool: not the maths, but the order it puts your animals in.

Nothing on this page is a health judgement. A due date passing does not by itself mean something is wrong, and a birth on time does not by itself mean everything is fine. The page counts days; judging an animal's health is a vet's job, and this page says so wherever it matters.

A worked example

Take a small keeper with three animals. A cow named Daisy was bred on 1 March. A ewe, Tag 14, was bred on 20 April. A goat named Nanny was bred on 5 May. All three go into the record keeper, one at a time.

The due dates are worked out from the typical gestation lengths. Daisy the cow: 1 March plus 283 days is 9 December. Tag 14 the sheep: 20 April plus 147 days is 14 September. Nanny the goat: 5 May plus 150 days is 2 October. The list sorts itself by due date, so Tag 14 sits at the top, then Nanny, then Daisy.

Suppose today is 1 September. Tag 14 has 13 days left, so her status is due soon: time to prepare a clean pen and check her twice a day. Nanny has 31 days left, so she is upcoming, with no rush yet. Daisy has 99 days left, so she is well ahead, and the keeper can focus on the two nearer births.

Now suppose Tag 14 has not given birth by 20 September, six days past her due date. The page shows her as overdue. That does not prove anything is wrong, but it is the signal to call the vet and ask, rather than waiting and hoping. The record keeper cannot make that call; it can only make the lateness visible.

Tips for a good result

Write the breeding date down on the day it happens. A date remembered a month later is often wrong by days, and a wrong breeding date gives a wrong due date. If you are unsure of the exact date, enter your best estimate and note in your own records that it is an estimate.

Use names or tag numbers that will still make sense in nine months. Daisy is fine. Cow 3 is fine if you know which cow that is. A name that means nothing to you later is worse than no name at all, because it looks like a record while telling you nothing.

Keep a paper copy of the list where you work. The copy button makes plain text you can print and pin up. A list on the barn wall gets checked every day; a list in a phone gets checked when someone remembers to open it. Use both.

  • Check due-soon animals twice a day in the last two weeks, and keep a vet's number where you can reach it
  • Prepare the birth pen before the due date, not on the day
  • Record the real birth date when it happens, because real dates improve next season's planning
  • Remove animals that have given birth, so the list stays short and honest

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This record keeper cannot tell you whether an animal is pregnant. It counts days from the breeding date you enter, and it trusts that date completely. If the breeding did not take, or the date is wrong, the due date will be wrong too, and the page has no way to know.

It cannot predict the birth. Gestation lengths vary between animals, between breeds, and between seasons. A due date is a typical average, and a healthy birth can come days or even weeks on either side of it. Treat the date as a watch window, not a deadline.

It cannot replace a vet. If a due date passes with no birth, if an animal looks unwell, or if a birth is not going as it should, call a vet. This page keeps records; it does not diagnose, it does not advise, and it never should.

Questions people ask

What breeding records should I keep for each animal?

Keep four things: the animal's name or tag number, the species, the breeding date, and the expected due date. When the birth happens, add the real birth date and any notes about how it went. Those five facts, kept for every animal, are the whole breeding record.

How is the expected due date worked out?

The due date is the breeding date plus the typical gestation length for the species: about 283 days for a cow, 147 for a sheep, 150 for a goat, 114 for a pig, and 340 for a horse. These are averages, and real births vary around them, so treat the date as an estimate, not a promise.

Can I record more than one animal?

Yes. Add as many animals as you need, one at a time. The list sorts itself by due date, nearest first, so the animal due soonest is always at the top. You can remove an animal from the list after it gives birth, or keep the record for reference.

Does it work without internet?

Yes, once the page has loaded. The record keeper runs entirely in your browser and needs no connection to add animals or work out due dates. Your records are saved in the browser on that device, so they are there the next time you open the page.

Can I print the records?

Yes. Press the copy button to turn the list into plain text, then paste it into any document or note and print it from there. The copied text includes every animal, its breeding date, its due date and the days left, so the printed page is a full record.

What should I do if a due date passes with no birth?

Call a vet and do not wait. A birth a few days late can be normal, but a due date that has passed is the signal to ask a professional rather than hoping. Watch the animal closely, note any changes, and follow the vet's advice. Never try to hurry a birth yourself.
