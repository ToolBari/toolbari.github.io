Body Surface Area Calculator

Work out your body surface area in square metres from your height and weight. Enter both measurements and the calculator applies the Mosteller formula, with the Du Bois formula shown alongside for comparison.

Mosteller: BSA = square root of (height in cm x weight in kg / 3600). Du Bois: BSA = 0.007184 x height^0.725 x weight^0.425. One inch is 2.54 cm; one pound is 0.453592 kg.

The result is a measurement for education and curiosity. It is not a medical assessment, and it must never be used to work out a medicine dose. If a clinician needs your body surface area, they will measure and calculate it themselves.

The Mosteller formula

The Mosteller formula, published in 1987, is the simplest widely used way to estimate body surface area: take the square root of height in centimetres times weight in kilograms divided by 3600. The result is in square metres. Its simplicity is its strength — it needs no tables, no exponents and no special equipment, just a tape measure and a scale.

The formula is an estimate, not a measurement. True body surface area can only be measured by coating or scanning the body, which is impractical outside a laboratory. The formula approximates it from height and weight, and for most adults the approximation is close enough for the purposes it is used for.

Mosteller is the formula most clinicians reach for first, because it is easy to remember and easy to check. The Du Bois formula, published in 1916, is older and slightly more complex, using powers of height and weight instead of a square root. Both are shown here so you can see how little the choice matters for most people.

Worked example

Suppose a person is 170 centimetres tall and weighs 70 kilograms. The Mosteller formula multiplies 170 by 70, which is 11,900, divides by 3600, which is 3.31, and takes the square root, which is 1.82 square metres.

The Du Bois formula for the same person gives 1.81 square metres — a difference of one hundredth. That closeness is typical: for adults of ordinary proportions the two formulas agree within a few hundredths, and the difference is smaller than the error from measuring height or weight carelessly.

Try your own numbers and compare the two. If the gap between them is large, the likeliest cause is a measurement error, not the formulas — remeasure your height standing straight against a wall, and your weight on a flat, hard floor.

Mosteller vs Du Bois

The Du Bois formula came first, published in 1916 from measurements of just nine people — a fact worth knowing, because it explains why later researchers kept looking for better formulas. It uses height to the power 0.725 and weight to the power 0.425, multiplied by 0.007184, and it was the standard for most of the twentieth century.

The Mosteller formula came in 1987, as a simplification: the square root of height times weight divided by 3600. Studies comparing the two found them close for most adults, and Mosteller's simplicity won it wide use. Neither formula is exact, and both struggle at the extremes — very short, very tall, or very heavy bodies — where the relationship between height, weight and surface area is less predictable.

For everyday curiosity, either formula is fine, and this calculator shows both. For medical use, the choice belongs to the clinician, who will know which formula their field expects and whether an estimate is good enough for the decision at hand.

Why surface area scales differently from weight

Surface area and weight do not grow at the same rate, and that is why the formula needs both height and weight. If a person grew twice as tall in every direction, their weight would grow eightfold — twice as tall, twice as wide, twice as deep — but their surface area would grow only fourfold. Weight follows volume, and surface follows area, and the two diverge as bodies get bigger.

That is also why two people of the same weight can have different surface areas. A tall, lean person has more surface than a short, stocky person of the same weight, because the tall person's body is spread over a larger frame. Height matters to the answer, not just weight, and a formula that used weight alone would miss that difference.

Children show the same effect in reverse: a small child has much more surface area per kilogram than an adult, which is one reason children lose heat faster and why their medicine doses are scaled differently. The formula captures the shape of the body, not just its mass.

What body surface area is used for

Body surface area is used in medicine as a way of scaling the body — for working out drug doses for some medicines, for comparing kidney function between people of different sizes, and for describing the extent of burns. In each case the idea is the same: a bigger body has more surface, more blood and more tissue, and some things scale with surface area better than they scale with weight alone.

It is also used in research, to compare measurements between people fairly. A heart output or a kidney clearance that looks low for a large person may be normal for a small one, and dividing by body surface area removes the size difference so the comparison is meaningful.

None of those uses belongs to this page. The calculator shows the number and explains the formulas; applying the number to a medical decision is a clinician's job, and this page does not do it, hint at it, or help with it.

Measuring height and weight accurately

Height is easy to get wrong. Stand barefoot against a flat wall, heels together, back straight, looking straight ahead, and mark the top of your head. Measure from the floor to the mark with a tape held straight — a tape that sags or angles will read long. Measure in the morning if you want the most consistent reading, because the spine compresses slightly during the day.

Weight needs a flat, hard floor: a scale on carpet reads low, and a scale on an uneven floor reads whatever it likes. Weigh yourself in light clothing, at the same time of day, and step on the scale the same way each time. A single reading is fine for this calculator — it is not a medical measurement.

Small errors matter less than you might think. An error of one centimetre in height or one kilogram in weight changes the result by only a few hundredths of a square metre, which is smaller than the difference between the two formulas. Measure carefully, but do not chase precision the formulas cannot use.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is mixing units: entering height in inches while the unit selector says centimetres, or weight in pounds while it says kilograms. Check the selectors before you tap the button — a 70 inch height is a very tall person, and a 170 pound weight is a different person entirely.

The second mistake is using an old measurement. Height changes slowly with age, and weight changes faster — a figure from years ago gives an answer for the person you were, not the person you are. Measure fresh if the number matters to you.

The third mistake is treating the result as a health verdict. Body surface area is a size measurement, like height or weight. It does not diagnose anything, and a large or small number is not by itself a cause for concern.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you anything about your health. It turns height and weight into a surface-area estimate, and that is all it does. It cannot diagnose, assess risk, or recommend anything, and it must never be used to calculate a medicine dose.

It also cannot measure your real surface area. The formulas are estimates built from population averages, and an individual body can differ from the estimate. If a clinician needs an accurate figure, they will use their own methods — this page is for education only.

Questions people ask

What is body surface area?

It is the total area of the skin covering the body, measured in square metres. It cannot be measured directly without special equipment, so it is estimated from height and weight using a formula such as Mosteller or Du Bois.

Why does the calculator show two formulas?

Because the two most widely used formulas — Mosteller (1987) and Du Bois (1916) — give slightly different answers, and showing both makes the estimate honest. For most adults they agree within a few hundredths of a square metre.

What is a normal body surface area?

There is no single normal value, because it depends on height and weight. An average-sized adult is often around 1.7 to 1.9 square metres, but taller or heavier people are higher and smaller people are lower. The number is a size measurement, not a health verdict.

Can children use this calculator?

The formulas were developed mainly from adult measurements, and they are less reliable for children. If a child's body surface area is needed for medical reasons, a clinician should calculate it. This page is for general education only.

Does body surface area diagnose anything?

No. It is a measurement of size, like height or weight. It does not diagnose any condition, and a large or small value is not by itself a cause for concern. Any health question belongs to a clinician.

Why does it ask for height and weight?

Because the formulas estimate surface area from those two measurements. Height and weight together describe the body's size well enough for the estimate, and they are the only measurements the formulas need.
