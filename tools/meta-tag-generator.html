What each tag does, in plain words

A meta tag is a short line of code that sits in the head of a page, the part of the page that visitors never see. Search engines and social apps read these lines to understand what the page is about, and to decide what to show when the page appears in results or gets shared. You do not need to memorise what each tag does, because this generator builds all of them from your five boxes, but knowing the basics helps you write better titles and descriptions.

  • Title. The clickable headline in the search results, and the text shown on the browser tab. This is the most important tag on the page, because it is the first thing a reader sees and the first thing a search engine reads. A good title says what the page is about in plain words, and it fits within the length limit so it shows in full.
  • Description. The short summary that appears under the title in the search results. It does not change where a page ranks directly, but a good description gets more clicks, because it tells the reader what they will find and why it is worth opening. Write one clear sentence, not a list of keywords.
  • Keywords. A list of words taken from your title, lowercased, with repeats removed. Most search engines ignore this tag today, and it will not help a page rank, but it costs nothing to include, and this generator builds it for you automatically. Do not add extra words to it by hand; the title words are enough.
  • Robots. A short instruction that tells search engines the page may be indexed and its links followed. Indexing means the page may appear in search results, and following means the search engine may visit the pages your links point to. This is the normal setting for an ordinary page, and the generator includes it for you.
  • Open Graph. A set of tags that control how the page looks when someone shares it on social media or in a chat app. They set the shared title, the shared description, the page address, the site name and, when you add an image address, the shared picture. Without these tags, a shared link may show the wrong title or no picture at all.
  • Twitter Card. The same job as Open Graph, for Twitter. When you add an image address, the generator uses the large image card, which shows a big picture with the shared link. Without an image address, it uses the plain summary card, which shows the title and description without a picture.
  • Canonical. A link that tells search engines which address is the real one for this page, for the cases where the same page can be reached by more than one address. If your page has only one address, the canonical line is still harmless and helpful, because it removes any doubt about which address is the right one.

That is the whole set. The generator builds every one of these lines from the five boxes you fill in, and it leaves out the image lines when you leave the image box empty, so you never get a tag pointing at a picture that does not exist.

The length limits, and why they matter

Search results show about 60 characters of a title. A title longer than that gets cut off with three dots, and the cut part is the part people never see. The limit is not a rule that punishes long titles; it is a display limit, and a title that is too long still works, it just does not show in full. But a title that is cut off in the middle of its promise is a title that does less work for you, so keeping it under 60 characters is worth the small effort.

Descriptions get about 160 characters in the search results. The same idea applies: a description longer than that gets cut off, and the reader never sees the ending. Write one clear sentence that fits, and put the most important words first, so that even if a search engine shows a little less than 160 characters, the meaning survives.

The limits are about display, not about quality. A title of 45 characters is not better than a title of 58 characters just because it is shorter. What matters is that the title says what the page is about, that it fits in the space the search results give it, and that it reads like something a person would want to click. Use the length check on this page as a guide, not as a score to chase.

One habit makes the limits easy to respect: put the most important words first. If your page is about fresh coffee beans, start with fresh coffee beans, not with your site name or a greeting. If the end of the line ever gets cut, the beginning still carries the meaning, and the reader still knows what the page offers.

A worked example: one page, before and after

Imagine a page about fresh coffee beans, on a small shop's website. Before any work, the page has a title of Home and no description at all. That is how many pages start out: the site builder filled in a default title, nobody changed it, and the description box was left empty. In the search results, the page appears with the word Home and a few random words pulled from the page itself. Nobody reading that result knows what the page offers, and nobody has a reason to open it.

After a few minutes with this tool, the same page has a title of Fresh Coffee Beans Delivered to Your Door, which is 41 characters and fits easily, and a description of Order fresh coffee beans online with fast delivery and a simple guide to brewing a great cup at home, which is 101 characters and also fits. Now the search result shows a clear promise: fresh beans, delivery, and a brewing guide. The reader knows what they will get before they click, and the click they make is a click from someone who wants exactly that.

The tags the generator builds for the new version start with the title line, <title>Fresh Coffee Beans Delivered to Your Door</title>, followed by the description line, <meta name="description" content="Order fresh coffee beans online with fast delivery and a simple guide to brewing a great cup at home.">, and the sharing lines, including <meta property="og:title" content="Fresh Coffee Beans Delivered to Your Door">. Pasted into the head of the page, those lines are the whole change: nothing else on the page needs to move, and the page itself does not look any different to a visitor.

The lesson of the example is small but important. The before version was not broken; it simply said nothing. The after version says one clear thing, and that is enough. Most pages do not need clever titles. They need honest titles that fit, and descriptions that say what the page offers in one sentence.

What it cannot do

These tags describe your page, but they do not rank it on their own. A great title on a thin page still disappoints the reader who opens it, and a search engine that sees readers leave quickly will not keep showing the page. The tags open the door; the page itself has to be worth entering.

The generator cannot see your page. It builds tags from what you type, so check your spelling, check your page address, and read the title once aloud before you paste the tags in. A typo in the title will show up in every search result until you fix it, and a wrong address in the canonical line will point search engines at a page that does not exist.

It also cannot add the tags to your site for you. The generator gives you the lines to paste, and you paste them into your page's head section, or into the title and description fields your website builder provides. If you are not sure where the head section is, your builder's help pages will show you, and it is usually a one-time job per page.

Common mistakes

  • The same description on every page. Every page should have its own title and description that match that page. Repeating the same description everywhere tells search engines that your pages are all the same, and it wastes the chance to describe each one properly. Write each page its own lines, even when the pages are similar.
  • Keyword stuffing. Repeating a word in the title until it reads badly does not help, and it makes the page look careless. One mention of the main word, in a title that reads like a normal sentence, is enough. Search engines read the whole page, not just the title, and readers judge the title as a piece of writing.
  • No Open Graph image. A page without a sharing image still works, but a shared link with no picture looks plain next to every other shared link, and fewer people open it. If you have a clear picture for the page, add its address to the image box. If you have no picture, leave the box empty rather than pointing at a picture that does not belong to the page.
  • A title that promises what the page does not deliver. A title is a promise, and a reader who opens a page that breaks its promise will not stay and will not come back. Write the title after you know what the page actually says, not before, and make sure the page delivers the thing the title names.
  • Forgetting the canonical when a page has two addresses. Some pages can be reached by more than one address, for example with and without a trailing slash, or through an old address that still works. The canonical line tells search engines which address is the real one, so the page's value is not split between two copies of the same page.

Questions people ask

What are meta tags?

Meta tags are short lines of code in the head of a page that describe the page to search engines and social apps. Visitors do not see them on the page, but search engines read them to understand what the page is about.

How do I use this meta tag generator?

Type your page title and description, add the page address and your site name, and press Generate. The tool builds the full set of tags, which you copy and paste into the head section of your page.

How long should my title be?

Keep your title under 60 characters so it shows in full in search results. A title longer than that gets cut off, and the cut part is the part people never see.

How long should my meta description be?

Keep your description under 160 characters, because search results show about that much. Write one clear sentence that says what the page offers and why a reader should open it.

What is an Open Graph image?

An Open Graph image is the picture that appears when someone shares your page on social media or in a chat. A page without one still works, but a clear image makes the shared link look much better.

Do I need a different description on every page?

Yes, every page should have its own title and description that match that page. Repeating the same description everywhere tells search engines that your pages are all the same, and it wastes the chance to describe each one properly.
