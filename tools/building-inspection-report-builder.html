What this report builder does and who it is for
A building inspection report is a written record of what you found when you looked at a property properly — area by area, problem by problem. This builder gives you the structure: five areas to check, a condition for each one, and space for notes about what you actually saw. When you are done, it turns your notes into a clear report you can print or copy.
It is built for anyone who needs to look at a property with care: a buyer viewing a house, a landlord checking a flat between tenants, a tenant recording the state of a place on moving day, or a homeowner planning repairs. You do not need building knowledge to use it — you need your eyes, your phone camera, and the patience to look in every corner.
What it is not matters as much as what it is. This page does not inspect the building for you, and its report is not a professional survey. A surveyor is trained to spot structural movement, hidden damp and unsafe wiring, and their report carries weight that a self-made checklist does not. Use this builder to record what you see; use a surveyor when the decision is serious.
How to use the builder on your phone or computer
Start by typing a label for the property — a street name or a flat number — and the date. Then work through the five areas in order: roof, walls, floors, plumbing, electrics. For each one, set the condition to Good, Needs attention or Problem, or leave it as Not checked if you could not see it. Then write a short note about what you saw.
Your entries are saved only in this browser as you type, so you can walk around the property, fill in what you see, and come back later to finish. Nothing is uploaded anywhere. When every area you could check is done, tap Build my report. The report appears below the tool with a summary of how many problems you found.
The copy button copies the whole report as plain text, ready to paste into a message or a document. To print it, use your browser's print function — the report is plain text and prints cleanly. The clear button wipes everything, including the saved copy in the browser, when you want to start a fresh inspection.
The areas to check, in order
Start with the roof, because roof problems cause most of the damage elsewhere. From the ground, look for missing or slipped tiles, sagging lines, and gutters that overflow or hang loose. Inside, look at the ceilings under the roof for stains or damp patches — they usually mean water is getting in somewhere above.
Then the walls, inside and out. Outside, look for cracks, crumbling render and damp at the base of the walls. Inside, look for cracks around doors and windows, damp patches, peeling paint and mould — especially in corners and behind furniture. A small crack can be harmless settling; a wide or growing crack is a reason to get a professional opinion.
Floors come next: check for uneven boards, springy spots, cracked tiles and damp under sinks and around baths. Then plumbing — run the taps, flush the toilets, look under sinks for leaks and water stains, and check the water pressure. Finally the electrics: look for old fuse boxes, cracked sockets, flickering lights and any smell of burning. Never open an electrical panel yourself — note what you can see and leave the rest to an electrician.
What counts as a real problem
A real problem is something that affects safety, structure or the building's ability to keep water out. Damp that is spreading, a roof that leaks, a floor that bounces underfoot, wiring that smells of burning — these are problems, and they belong in the report with the clearest note you can write about where they are and how bad they look.
Cosmetic issues are different. A scuffed wall, a dated bathroom suite or a chipped tile may bother you, but they do not threaten the building. Mark them as needing attention if you want them recorded, but do not let them drown out the real problems. A report that lists everything as a problem helps nobody.
When you are unsure, say so in the note. A problem note that says cracked plaster above the kitchen window, about as long as a hand, not sure if it is growing is far more useful than a note that just says crack. The next person to read the report — a builder, a surveyor, a future you — needs the detail to judge it.
Taking photos that help
Photos turn a good report into a useful one. Take one wide photo of each area so the reader can see where the problem is, then one close photo of the problem itself. A close-up of a damp patch without the wide shot leaves the reader guessing which room it is in.
Put something in the close photo for scale when it helps — a coin, a hand, a tape measure. A crack that looks huge in a close-up may be tiny in real life, and a scale object stops the reader from overreacting or underreacting. Make sure the photo is in focus and lit: a blurry photo of a dark corner proves nothing.
This page does not store your photos — it only records the words you type. Keep the photos on your own phone or computer, and name them by area so you can find them later. If you send the report to someone, send the photos with it and say which note each photo belongs to.
A worked example: a small flat, honestly inspected
Imagine a two-bedroom flat. The roof is not visible from inside and the flat is on the ground floor, so the roof is marked Not checked with a note saying roof not accessible, no ceiling stains seen. The walls are marked Needs attention: a damp patch behind the bathroom door, about the size of a dinner plate, with a note that it feels dry to the touch today.
The floors are Good — even boards, no springy spots — with a note that the kitchen tiles are chipped near the door. The plumbing is marked Problem: a slow drip under the kitchen sink, with a note that a bowl underneath catches about a cup of water a day. The electrics are marked Needs attention: the fuse box is old, with a note to ask an electrician whether it should be replaced.
The report that comes out is short and honest: one problem, two areas needing attention, one area good, one not checked. Anyone reading it knows exactly what to fix first — the drip — and what to ask a professional about — the damp patch and the fuse box. That is what a good inspection report does.
Common mistakes that weaken a report
The first mistake is checking the paint but not the damp. Fresh paint hides damp, mould and cracks, and a quick look at a tidy room misses what is behind the furniture. Look in corners, behind doors, under sinks and inside cupboards — the places where problems hide.
The second mistake is forgetting the roof. It is out of sight, so it is easy to skip, but roof problems cause most of the expensive damage in a building. If you cannot see the roof, say so in the report rather than leaving it blank — Not checked with a note is honest, while a blank looks like you forgot.
The third mistake is vague notes. A note that says bad wall helps nobody. A note that says damp patch on the north bedroom wall, about the size of a hand, paint bubbling tells the reader where it is, how big it is and what it looks like. Write notes for someone who has never seen the property — because the person reading your report may be you, six months later, with no memory of the visit.


Questions people ask


What is a building inspection report?
A written record of what was found when a property was looked at area by area: the condition of the roof, walls, floors, plumbing and electrics, with notes about any problems. It helps buyers, landlords and tenants agree on the state of a property.
Does this replace a professional survey?
No. This page makes a checklist report from what you see yourself. A professional surveyor checks structure, damp and safety in ways a walkthrough cannot, and only a surveyor's report counts for a purchase, a loan or a legal dispute.
What should I check first?
Start with the roof, because roof problems cause most damage elsewhere. Then walls, floors, plumbing and electrics. Work in order so you do not forget an area, and check the hidden corners — under sinks, behind doors, inside cupboards.
How do I note a problem clearly?
Say where it is, how big it is, and what it looks like: damp patch on the north bedroom wall, about the size of a hand, paint bubbling. A clear note helps a builder or surveyor judge the problem without visiting.
Can I print the report?
Yes. Build the report, then use your browser's print function — the report is plain text and prints cleanly. The copy button copies it as text if you want to paste it into a message or a document instead.
What do I do with the report?
Keep it with your records for the property. Use it to ask builders for quotes on the problems found, to agree repairs with a landlord or seller, or to show a surveyor what you already noticed before their visit.
