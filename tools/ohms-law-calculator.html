Ohm's law in plain words

Ohm's law is one of the simplest and most useful rules in all of electronics. It says that voltage equals current times resistance: V = I x R. Voltage is the push, current is the flow, and resistance is whatever stands in the way of the flow. Push harder, and more current flows. Add resistance, and less current flows. That is the whole law, and everything else is rearranging it: current equals voltage divided by resistance, and resistance equals voltage divided by current.

The water-pipe picture makes it easy to hold in your head. Imagine water flowing through a garden hose. The water pressure is the voltage — the push behind the flow. The amount of water coming out each second is the current. The narrowness of the hose is the resistance. Turn the tap up and the pressure rises, so more water flows. Squeeze the hose and the resistance rises, so less water flows, even though the pressure is the same. Swap the hose for a wider pipe and the resistance falls, so more water flows at the same pressure.

The three letters have their own units, named after the scientists who worked on them. Voltage is measured in volts (V), after Alessandro Volta. Current is measured in amps (A), after Andre-Marie Ampere. Resistance is measured in ohms, written with the Greek letter omega, after Georg Ohm, who found the law in the 1820s by careful experiment. You do not need to remember the history to use the law, but the names are worth knowing, because every circuit diagram and every electronics shop uses them.

Worked example: 12 volts and 2 amps

A small lamp runs on a 12 volt battery and draws 2 amps. What is the resistance of the lamp? Ohm's law rearranged says resistance equals voltage divided by current: R = V / I. Put the numbers in: R = 12 / 2 = 6 ohms. The lamp's resistance is 6 ohms. That is all there is to it — one division, and the answer is in front of you.

Now work out the power. Power is voltage times current: P = V x I = 12 x 2 = 24 watts. The lamp uses 24 watts, which means it turns 24 joules of electrical energy into light and heat every second. That number matters when you choose a battery or a power supply: a supply that can only deliver 10 watts will not run a 24 watt lamp properly, and a battery rated for 24 watts will last about half as long as one rated for 12 watts at the same voltage.

Try the other directions with the same numbers. If you know the lamp is 6 ohms and the battery is 12 volts, the current is V / R = 12 / 6 = 2 amps. If you know the lamp is 6 ohms and draws 2 amps, the voltage across it is I x R = 2 x 6 = 12 volts. The same three numbers, three ways round. That is the shape of Ohm's law: any two values give you the third, and the power follows from the voltage and the current. Once you have done it once by hand, the calculator is there to do it quickly every time after.

Power and why it matters

Power is the part beginners skip, and it is the part that causes the smoke. Every resistor turns electrical energy into heat, and the amount of heat is the power: voltage times current. A small resistor rated for a quarter of a watt will get hot and fail if you push a full watt through it. The calculator shows the power for exactly this reason — so you can check that your parts can handle it before you build the circuit.

There is a second way to work out power that is often more useful: power equals current squared times resistance (P = I x I x R), or voltage squared divided by resistance (P = V x V / R). These are the same rule rearranged, and they explain why a small rise in voltage causes a big rise in heat: double the voltage and the power goes up four times, not two. That is why a lamp made for 12 volts burns out quickly on 24 volts. It is not twice as bright — it is four times as hot, and the filament cannot take it.

When you buy a resistor, its power rating matters as much as its resistance. Common small resistors are rated for a quarter of a watt. If your circuit pushes half a watt through one, it will overheat, change value, and eventually fail — sometimes with a smell, sometimes with a tiny puff of smoke. Always pick a resistor rated for at least twice the power your circuit will push through it. The extra size costs almost nothing, and it is the difference between a circuit that works for years and one that fails on the first hot day.

Getting the units right

Electronics uses small numbers, and the prefixes are where the mistakes live. A milliamp (mA) is a thousandth of an amp: 1000 mA = 1 A. A microamp is a millionth of an amp. A kilohm is a thousand ohms: 1 kilohm = 1000 ohms. A megohm is a million ohms. A millivolt is a thousandth of a volt. The calculator works in plain volts, amps and ohms, so convert before you type, and convert the answer back afterwards if that is easier to read.

A quick habit saves most errors: always write the unit next to the number on paper before you reach for the calculator. "250 mA" is clear. "250" on its own is a trap, because you cannot tell whether it means 250 amps or 250 milliamps, and the two answers differ by a factor of a thousand. The same goes for resistor colour codes and printed values: a resistor marked 4K7 means 4.7 kilohms, which is 4700 ohms, not 47 ohms. The K stands for the decimal point as well as for kilo.

Power has its own prefixes too. A milliwatt is a thousandth of a watt, and a kilowatt is a thousand watts. A 24 watt lamp uses 24,000 milliwatts and 0.024 kilowatts. None of this is hard, but it is easy to get wrong in a hurry. Slow down for ten seconds, write the units down, and the calculator will give you the right answer the first time.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is mixing up milliamps and amps. A phone charger that delivers 2 amps is delivering 2000 milliamps. A small sensor that draws 20 milliamps draws 0.02 amps. Type the wrong one and the answer is wrong by a factor of a thousand — and the power is wrong by the same factor, which is how people end up buying resistors that are far too small or far too large for the job. Always convert milliamps to amps before you type.

The second mistake is forgetting that real circuits are not perfect. A battery's voltage drops as it drains, and it drops further when you draw a large current from it. A resistor's value changes as it heats up. Wires have a little resistance of their own, and in a long wire that little bit adds up. Ohm's law is exact for an ideal resistor, and close enough for most real ones, but if your measured current is ten percent away from the calculated value, the law is not wrong — your parts are just real, not ideal.

The third mistake is dividing by zero. Resistance of zero means a short circuit: the current is limited only by the battery and the wires, and in theory it is infinite. In practice it is very large, very fast, and dangerous — wires melt, batteries swell, and things catch fire. The calculator refuses to divide by zero, and you should refuse too. If your numbers give a resistance of zero, something is wrong with the circuit, not with the maths.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot handle alternating current circuits, where the voltage and current change direction many times a second and where capacitors and inductors add a kind of resistance that changes with frequency. It cannot model motors, transformers, or circuits where the load changes as it runs. It cannot tell you whether a circuit is safe, and it certainly cannot make mains electricity safe to touch. For alternating current and for anything connected to wall power, you need a different tool and, for real work, a qualified electrician.

It also cannot choose components for you or draw a circuit diagram. It solves one equation, shows the steps, and gives you the power — and it does that well. Use it to check your homework, to size a resistor, to work out what a battery can drive, and to build the habit of checking the numbers before you build. For the rest of electronics, the calculator is a starting point, not the whole workshop.

Questions people ask

What is Ohm's law?

Ohm's law says that voltage equals current times resistance: V = I x R. It links the three basic quantities in a circuit — the push (voltage), the flow (current) and what stands in the way (resistance). If you know any two of them, the law gives you the third. It was found by Georg Ohm in the 1820s and it is still the first rule every electronics student learns.

What are volts, amps and ohms?

Volts measure voltage, the push that drives current around a circuit. Amps measure current, the flow of charge past a point each second. Ohms measure resistance, how much a part resists the flow. A 12 volt battery pushing 2 amps through a 6 ohm lamp is a typical example: 12 = 2 x 6. Remember that 1000 milliamps = 1 amp and 1000 ohms = 1 kilohm.

How do I find resistance?

Divide the voltage by the current: R = V / I. If a lamp runs on 12 volts and draws 2 amps, its resistance is 12 / 2 = 6 ohms. Enter the voltage and the current in the calculator, leave resistance blank, and it works it out for you with the steps shown. Make sure the current is in amps, not milliamps, before you type it in.

What is power in a circuit?

Power is how fast a circuit uses energy, measured in watts. It equals voltage times current: P = V x I. A 12 volt lamp drawing 2 amps uses 24 watts. Power also tells you how much heat a part must get rid of — a resistor rated for a quarter of a watt will overheat if you push a full watt through it. Always check the power rating, not just the resistance.

Why is my resistor hot?

A resistor turns electrical energy into heat, and the heat it makes is the power: voltage times current. If it is hot, you are pushing more power through it than its rating allows. Work out the power with this calculator, then use a resistor rated for at least twice that power. If the resistor is too hot to touch, switch the circuit off and let it cool before you change anything.

Can I use this for AC circuits?

Not directly. Ohm's law as shown here is for steady direct current. In alternating current circuits, capacitors and inductors add a kind of resistance that changes with frequency, and the simple V = I x R rule needs extra maths. This calculator is for learning and for low-voltage DC electronics. Never use it as a guide for mains electricity — that work needs a qualified electrician.
