What this page gives you

A project is any job with a beginning, an end and more than one step: opening a shop, organising an event, building a website, running a school fair. Projects fail for ordinary reasons. Nobody wrote the plan down, nobody knew who was doing what, or nobody noticed the deadline until it was too late. A template fixes all three problems at once, because it gives you the shape of the plan before you start filling it in.

This page gives you eight free templates that cover a whole project from start to finish. There is a one-page project plan, a task list with an owner and a due date on every task, a weekly status report, meeting notes, a risk list, a budget sheet outline, a launch checklist and a handover note. The templates are deliberately short. A ten-page plan is a plan nobody reads. Each template here fits on one page when filled in, because a plan you can read in two minutes is a plan people will actually follow. The templates work for a business project, a school project, a family event or a community job. The words are plain and the shapes are simple, so anyone on the team can use them without training.

How to use a template

Start by reading the template once before you copy it, so you know what it asks for. Then tap its Copy button. The button changes to Copied for two seconds, and the template text is on your clipboard. Paste it into whatever you use for notes: a document, a notes app, or even a message to yourself. Then work through it from top to bottom, replacing each bracketed part with your real details.

The bracketed parts are the heart of every template. A bracket like [Project name] means you write your project name there. A bracket like [Owner] means you write the name of the person responsible. When you are done, read the template once from the top. If any bracket is still there, fill it in or delete that line. A finished template should have no brackets left in it. That final read is the most important step, because a template with [Your Name] still in it, sent to a client, looks careless.

On a phone, the templates are easy to copy and paste into a notes app. On a desktop computer, paste them into a document and save the document with a clear name, such as the project name and the date. Keep one copy of each filled-in template where the whole team can find it. A plan that lives only on one person's phone is not a plan the team can follow.

The eight templates, grouped by use

The planning templates come first. The one-page project plan is where every project should start. It holds the goal, the owner, the start and end dates, the main deliverables, the key dates, the people and the biggest risk, all on one page. If you cannot fill in a one-page plan, the project is not ready to start. The budget sheet outline sits beside it: a simple list of budget categories with a planned amount and a spent amount for each one. It is an outline, not an accounting system, and it is enough for a small project.

The running templates keep the project moving. The task list is the daily workhorse: every task with an owner and a due date, so nothing is forgotten and nobody can say they did not know it was theirs. The meeting notes template turns a meeting into decisions and actions, with a name and a date on every action. The risk list is the template most people skip and most people need: a short list of what could go wrong, how likely it is, and what you will do about it.

The reporting and closing templates finish the job. The weekly status report is a short update you can send to anyone who needs to know how the project is going: what was done this week, what is next, and what is blocked. The launch checklist is the final check before something goes live, a list of small jobs that are easy to forget and painful to miss. The handover note is for the end, when the project passes to someone else: what is done, what is still open, and who to ask about it.

Running a small project without a paid app

You do not need a paid project app to run a small project well. You need a plan, a list, a regular check-in and a way to share all three with the team. These templates give you the plan and the list. The check-in can be a short weekly message using the status report template. The sharing can be a shared document or a group chat. The tools do not matter nearly as much as the habit of writing things down and checking them.

Start with the one-page plan. Fill it in with the team, not alone, because a plan the team helped write is a plan the team will follow. Then build the task list from the plan. Every deliverable in the plan becomes one or more tasks, and every task gets an owner and a due date. A task with no owner is a task nobody does. A task with no due date is a task that never gets done.

Then keep the rhythm. Once a week, update the status report and send it to everyone involved. When a meeting happens, take notes with the meeting notes template and send the actions the same day. When something goes wrong, add it to the risk list instead of hoping it goes away. When the project ends, write the handover note while everything is still fresh. None of this takes long. The whole weekly routine is about fifteen minutes, and it saves hours of confusion.

Worked example: a small project from plan to handover

Suppose a school is organising a book fair. The organiser opens the one-page project plan and fills it in: the goal is a one-day fair with twenty stalls, the owner is the organiser, the start date is six weeks before the fair and the end date is the fair day itself. The deliverables are the stall list, the posters, the volunteer rota and the takings record. The key dates are the poster deadline, the stall confirmation deadline and the fair day. The biggest risk is rain, and the plan for that is a covered hall as a backup.

From the plan, the organiser builds the task list. Book the hall, with the organiser as owner and a due date in the first week. Design the poster, with a volunteer as owner. Confirm the stalls, with a second volunteer as owner. Each week the organiser sends a short status report to the volunteers: what was done, what is next, and what is blocked. When the team meets, the organiser takes notes with the meeting notes template and sends the actions the same evening.

In the last week, the organiser runs the launch checklist: hall confirmed, tables counted, posters up, volunteers reminded, takings box ready. After the fair, the organiser writes a handover note for next year's organiser: what worked, what did not, the stall list, and the contact for the hall booking. Next year starts from a finished note instead of from nothing. That is what the templates are for.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a ten-page plan nobody reads. A plan is a tool for the team, not a display piece. If your plan is longer than one page, cut it until it fits. The second mistake is a task with no owner. A task that belongs to everyone belongs to nobody. Every task needs one name beside it, and that person needs to know it is theirs.

The third mistake is never updating the status. A plan written on day one and never touched again is a decoration. The status report exists so the plan stays alive. Update it every week, even if the update is short. The fourth mistake is skipping the risk list because it feels negative. Listing risks is not pessimism, it is preparation. A risk written down with a plan beside it is a problem half solved.

The fifth mistake is copying a template and leaving the brackets in. A finished document should have no square brackets left in it. Read every template once from the top after filling it in, and remove or fill every bracket you find. The sixth mistake is using the wrong template for the job. A status report is not a plan, and a task list is not a handover note. Pick the template that matches the job you are doing right now.

What templates cannot do

Templates give you a starting shape, not a finished plan. They cannot decide your goal for you, set your dates for you, or do the work. They cannot replace talking to your team, and they cannot fix a project whose goal nobody agrees on. If the team disagrees about what the project is for, no template will save it. Agree on the goal first, then fill in the plan.

The templates also cannot fit every project exactly. A very large project with many teams needs more than a one-page plan, and a project with legal or safety rules needs documents written for those rules. Use these templates as a starting point and change them freely. Add a section, remove a section, rename a heading. A template you changed to fit your project is a template you will actually use.
