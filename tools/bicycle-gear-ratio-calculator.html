How to use this calculator

Enter your chainring teeth, the cassette cog you want to check, your wheel size and your cadence — the number of times you turn the pedals each minute. The calculator gives you the gear ratio, the gear inches, and the speed you would ride at that cadence, in kilometres per hour and miles per hour. If you also list your full cassette, it builds a table for every cog, so you can see the whole range of gears at a glance.

The wheel size matters because gear inches include the wheel diameter: the same chainring and cog give different gear inches on a 26 inch wheel and a 29 inch wheel. Pick your wheel from the list, or choose Custom and type the diameter in inches if your wheel is an unusual size. The diameter is the outside of the tyre, not the rim, so a fat tyre on a small rim can measure more than you expect.

Cadence is the number most riders guess at. If you do not know yours, count your pedal turns for 15 seconds and multiply by four, or ride at a comfortable pace and count for a full minute. Most riders settle between 70 and 90 turns a minute on the flat. Enter your real cadence, not the one you wish you had — the speed figures are only as honest as the cadence you type in.

What gear ratio and gear inches mean

Gear ratio is the simplest number: chainring teeth divided by cog teeth. A 50 tooth chainring with a 17 tooth cog gives a ratio of 2.94, which means the back wheel turns 2.94 times for each turn of the pedals. A higher ratio means a harder gear — more speed for each pedal turn, but more effort to push it. A lower ratio means an easier gear — less speed per turn, but easier on hills and easier to start from a standstill.

Gear inches turn the ratio into a distance, so you can compare gears across different wheel sizes. The formula is the gear ratio times the wheel diameter in inches: a ratio of 2.94 on a 27 inch wheel gives 79 gear inches. The name comes from the old penny-farthing bicycles, where the gear was the size of the front wheel itself — a 79 inch gear would have needed a wheel 79 inches across. The number is old, but it is still the clearest single way to compare one gear with another.

Speed follows from gear inches and cadence. Each turn of the pedals moves the bike forward by gear inches times pi — the distance the wheel rolls in one turn. Multiply by your cadence and you have the distance per minute, and the calculator turns that into kilometres per hour and miles per hour. The same gear at 90 turns a minute is half as fast again as at 60, which is why cadence matters as much as the gear itself.

Worked example: a road bike

A road bike has a 50 tooth chainring, a 700c wheel of about 27 inches, and the rider pedals at 90 turns a minute. In the 11 tooth cog the ratio is 50 divided by 11, which is 4.55, and the gear inches are 4.55 times 27, which is 123. At 90 turns a minute that gear gives about 53 kilometres per hour — a fast descent gear, far too hard for flat riding for most people.

In the 17 tooth cog the ratio is 50 divided by 17, which is 2.94, and the gear inches are 79. At 90 turns a minute that is about 34 kilometres per hour — a comfortable cruising gear for a fit rider on the flat. In the 28 tooth cog the ratio is 1.79, the gear inches are 48, and the speed at the same cadence is about 21 kilometres per hour — a climbing gear for a long hill, where keeping the pedals turning matters more than going fast.

The full cassette table shows the whole story at once: the jumps between gears, the gears that overlap, and the gears at each end of the range. A good cassette has small jumps in the gears you use most, so you can always find a comfortable cadence, and bigger jumps at the ends, where you only need the gear for a hill or a descent. If two gears in the table are almost the same, one of them is doing no useful work.

Worked example: a city bike

A city bike has a 38 tooth chainring, a 700c wheel, and the rider pedals at 70 turns a minute — a relaxed pace for streets and cycle paths. In the 18 tooth cog the ratio is 38 divided by 18, which is 2.11, and the gear inches are 57. At 70 turns a minute that gives about 19 kilometres per hour, which is a comfortable city speed: fast enough to get somewhere, slow enough to stop for traffic.

In the 13 tooth cog the ratio is 2.92, the gear inches are 79, and the speed at 70 turns a minute is about 26 kilometres per hour — the gear for an open stretch of path with no junctions. In the 28 tooth cog the ratio is 1.36, the gear inches are 37, and the speed is about 12 kilometres per hour — the gear for starting off, for a bridge, or for riding with a heavy shopping load.

City bikes usually have a smaller chainring than road bikes, which is why the same cog gives a lower gear. That is deliberate: city riding means stopping and starting, and an easy gear that gets you moving quickly is worth more than a hard gear you never reach. If your city bike feels too hard to start, the answer is usually a smaller chainring or a bigger easiest cog, not stronger legs.

Worked example: a mountain bike

A mountain bike has a 32 tooth chainring, a 29 inch wheel, and the rider pedals at 80 turns a minute on a trail. In the 42 tooth cog — the biggest cog on a wide-range cassette — the ratio is 32 divided by 42, which is 0.76, and the gear inches are 22. At 80 turns a minute that is about 9 kilometres per hour: slow, but that is the point. On a steep, rough climb, going slowly in a low gear beats walking, and a gear this low lets the rider keep turning the pedals instead of grinding to a stop.

In the 11 tooth cog the ratio is 2.91, the gear inches are 84, and the speed at 80 turns a minute is about 32 kilometres per hour — the gear for a flat fire road or a gentle descent. The range from 22 to 84 gear inches is what a wide cassette is for: the same bike can climb a steep trail and still move on the flat without the rider spinning uselessly.

The 29 inch wheel raises every gear a little compared with a 26 inch wheel on the same chainring and cog, because the wheel is bigger. That is one reason mountain bikes moved to bigger wheels: the same gearing gives a little more speed, and the bigger wheel rolls over roots and rocks more easily. Gearing for hills vs flat speed

Hills want low gears and flat roads want high ones, and the right setup depends on where you actually ride. If your rides are mostly flat, you need a tight range of gears around your cruising speed, with small jumps so you can always find the right cadence. A wide-range cassette with a very low gear is wasted on flat roads — you will never use the bottom gears, and the jumps between the gears you do use will be bigger than they need to be.

If your rides include real hills, you need a low gear that lets you climb without grinding. A good rule is that your lowest gear should let you climb your steepest regular hill at a cadence of at least 60 turns a minute. If you find yourself standing up and pushing hard on every hill, your lowest gear is too high — fit a bigger easiest cog or a smaller chainring. There is no shame in a low gear: professional riders use very low gears on mountain stages, because spinning is faster than grinding.

The mistake is to buy gearing for the rider you want to be instead of the rider you are. A huge chainring and a tiny easiest cog look fast in the shop, but if they leave you grinding up every hill at 40 turns a minute, they are slower than modest gearing you can actually spin. Use the calculator with your real cadence on your real hills, and let the numbers choose the gearing.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is only looking at the biggest gear. The top gear decides your fastest speed, but you spend most of your ride in the middle gears, and those are the ones that should have small, even jumps. A cassette with a huge top gear and big gaps in the middle is worse than a modest cassette with even steps — check the full table, not just the first row.

The second mistake is ignoring cadence. Two riders in the same gear at different cadences ride at very different speeds: at 60 turns a minute you go a third slower than at 90 in the same gear. If the calculator's speed looks wrong, check the cadence first — most riders overestimate how fast they pedal, and a realistic cadence gives a realistic speed.

The third mistake is forgetting the wheel size. The same chainring and cog give different gear inches on different wheels, so comparing gears between two bikes without the wheel size is meaningless. Always enter the wheel size, and remember that a fat tyre adds to the diameter — measure the outside of the tyre if you want the numbers exact.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you how fast you will actually ride. It works out the speed for a given gear and cadence on flat ground with no wind — and real riding has hills, wind, traffic, and a rider who gets tired. It cannot measure your fitness, your aerodynamics, or the weight of your bike and bags, and all of those change the speed more than the gearing does. Use it to compare gears and to plan a setup, not as a promise of speed.

It also cannot choose your gearing for you. The right gears depend on where you ride, how fit you are, and what you enjoy — a racer, a commuter and a tourer on the same roads would choose differently. The calculator shows you the numbers for any setup you type in, so you can compare them honestly before you spend money on parts.

Questions people ask

What is gear ratio?

Gear ratio is chainring teeth divided by cog teeth. A 50 tooth chainring with a 17 tooth cog gives a ratio of 2.94, so the back wheel turns 2.94 times for each turn of the pedals. A higher ratio is a harder gear; a lower ratio is easier, especially on hills.

What are gear inches?

Gear inches are the gear ratio times the wheel diameter in inches. They turn a gear into a distance, so you can compare gears across different wheel sizes. A ratio of 2.94 on a 27 inch wheel gives 79 gear inches. The name comes from the old penny-farthing, where the gear was the front wheel itself.

What gearing is best for hills?

Low gearing — a small chainring, a big easiest cog, or both. Your lowest gear should let you climb your steepest regular hill at 60 turns a minute or more. Spinning a low gear up a hill is faster and kinder to your knees than grinding a high gear.

How does wheel size change the result?

Gear inches include the wheel diameter, so the same chainring and cog give higher gear inches on a bigger wheel. A 29 inch wheel gives higher gears than a 26 inch wheel with the same parts. Always enter the wheel size, measured at the outside of the tyre.

What cadence should I aim for?

Most riders settle between 70 and 90 turns a minute on the flat, and a little lower on hills. Beginners often pedal too slowly in too hard a gear; try a lower gear and a faster spin. Count your pedal turns for 15 seconds and multiply by four to find your real cadence.

Do I need the whole cassette table?

It helps. The table shows the jumps between gears, the gears that overlap, and the range from your easiest to your hardest gear. A good cassette has small jumps in the gears you use most. If two gears in the table are almost the same, one of them is doing no useful work.
