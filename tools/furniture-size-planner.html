Furniture Size Planner

How to use this checker

Start by choosing the furniture type. The checker fills in typical sizes — a three-seater sofa is 210 long, 90 deep and 85 high, a double bed is 200 by 160 by 55, a wardrobe is 180 by 60 by 200, a dining table is 150 by 90 by 75 and a desk is 120 by 60 by 75. These are starting points only. Always replace them with your own measurements, because real furniture varies: one maker's three-seater sofa can be 20 centimetres longer than another's. If you are buying new, the size is on the shop's page or on the box. If you already own the piece, measure it with a tape.

Next, enter the room size, the doorway size and — if the piece must go up stairs or in a lift — the stair or lift size. The stairs boxes are optional: leave them blank and the checker skips that check. Tap the button and the checker gives you three verdicts, one for the room, one for the doorway and one for the stairs, each with the exact clearance in centimetres. A positive clearance is spare room; a negative clearance is the shortfall — how many centimetres too big the piece is. The copy button copies the whole result so you can paste it into a message or keep it for delivery day.

One thing to get right from the start: measure the furniture twice — once with its legs on, and once with them off. The legs-on size is how the piece sits in the room, and that is the size that matters for the room check. The legs-off size is how the piece travels, and that is the size that matters for the door and the stairs. Legs usually add 10 to 15 centimetres of height, and that difference is often exactly what decides whether a sofa fits through a door.

The three checks that matter

Most people check one thing — the room — and forget the other two. The room check asks whether the piece fits on the floor, in either direction. A sofa 210 long and 90 deep fits a room 400 by 300 easily, and it also fits turned the other way. The checker tries both orientations and reports the smallest spare margin, which is the number that matters: a piece with 190 centimetres to spare on one side and 5 on the other is a tight fit, not a generous one.

The doorway check is the one that catches people out. A piece passes through a door in one of three ways. Upright: its depth fits the door width and its height fits the door height. Turned on its side: its height fits the door width and its depth fits the door height. Or tilted: the diagonal of its depth-by-height face fits under the door height, while its narrowest side fits the door width. The checker tries all three and reports the clearance for the way that works — or the shortfall if none of them works. The doorway height is usually about 200 centimetres, which is generous; the doorway width is usually the problem.

The stairs check uses the same logic as the doorway, applied to the stairwell or the lift. Stairs are harder than doors, because the piece must also turn at the landing and clear the ceiling above the steps. A straight stair with a wide landing is easy. A narrow stair with a tight turn at the top can stop a piece that passed every door in the building. If the piece must go upstairs, measure the landing and the turn as well as the stair width — and if there is a lift, measure the lift door, not just the lift cabin, because the door is always narrower.

Worked example: a three-seater sofa through a 75 cm door

Take a three-seater sofa 210 centimetres long, 90 deep and 85 high, and a doorway 75 centimetres wide and 200 high. The room is 400 by 300, so the room check passes easily: 210 fits in 400 and 90 fits in 300, with 190 centimetres to spare on the long side. The problem is the door. Upright, the sofa's depth of 90 centimetres must pass through a 75-centimetre opening — it does not fit, and it is 15 centimetres too wide.

Turn the sofa on its side and the height becomes the passing width: 85 centimetres against a 75-centimetre opening — still 10 centimetres too wide. Tilt it and the numbers change again: the diagonal of the 90-by-85 face is about 124 centimetres, which fits comfortably under the 200-centimetre door height, but the narrowest side of the piece is still 85 centimetres, and that is still wider than the 75-centimetre opening. So the sofa does not fit through this door as it stands, in any position.

Now take the legs off. Sofa legs are usually about 15 centimetres, so the height drops from 85 to about 70. Turn the sofa on its side again: 70 centimetres against a 75-centimetre opening — it fits, with 5 centimetres to spare. That is the answer, but it comes with a condition: turning a 210-centimetre sofa on its side needs hallway room to swing it, and 5 centimetres of clearance is tight, so the door frame needs protecting on delivery day. Try it in the checker: it starts with these exact figures, so one tap shows the "does not fit" result; then change the height to 70 and tap again to see the fit.

Measuring a doorway properly

Measure the doorway at its narrowest point, not at its widest. Door frames narrow the opening, and the door itself — even when open — takes up space if it cannot fold flat against the wall. Measure the clear opening with the door fully open: from one side of the frame to the other, and from the floor to the top of the frame. Measure the width in two places, near the floor and near the top, and use the smaller number. Old houses are the worst for this: frames warp, floors slope, and a doorway can be a centimetre narrower at the top than at the bottom.

Measure the hallway too. A piece that fits the door still needs room to turn into it. The hallway in front of the door should be at least as wide as the piece is long, or the piece cannot be angled into the opening. This is where long pieces fail: a 210-centimetre sofa needs 210 centimetres of hallway to swing. If the hallway is narrow, the piece may need to go in at a steep angle, and the diagonal that matters is no longer the one the checker calculates — it is the diagonal of the whole piece against the hallway walls.

Finally, measure the path from the front door to the room, not just the room's own door. The narrowest door on the whole route decides whether the piece gets in. A wide bedroom door means nothing if the front door or the stairwell is narrower. Walk the route with a tape before delivery day, write down every width, and use the smallest one in the checker. Delivery drivers are patient, but they cannot make a sofa smaller.

What to do when it does not fit

Start with the easy fixes. Take the legs off — that saves 10 to 15 centimetres of height and is often enough on its own. Take the doors off their hinges — a door removed from its frame adds a few centimetres of clear width and removes the risk of scratching it. Remove any packaging, because boxes add bulk. If the piece has removable cushions, arms or shelves, take them off and carry them separately.

If it still does not fit, try the diagonal. A piece tilted at an angle presents a smaller profile to the door than it does standing flat, and the checker tests the tilted case for you. The hallway needs room for the tilt, so clear it first. Protect the door frame with a blanket or cardboard — a tight fit that scrapes the frame is a fit that damages the house. Two people and a slow, steady lift beat one person forcing it.

If none of that works, there are still options. Some sofas and wardrobes come in flat-pack versions that are assembled inside the room — ask the shop before you buy. A ground-floor window can work if it opens wide enough and the piece fits through it, but measure the opening first and get permission if the building is shared. As a last resort, pick a smaller size: a two-seater sofa instead of a three-seater, or a wardrobe 120 wide instead of 180. A piece that fits is better than a piece that sits in the hallway.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is measuring the furniture without its legs and then being surprised when it arrives taller than the doorway. Or the reverse: measuring with the legs on and giving up on a piece that would have fitted with them off. Measure both ways, and know which measurement each check needs. The second mistake is forgetting the hallway turn. A piece can fit the door and still be stuck because there is no room to swing it into the opening. Measure the hallway, not just the door.

The third mistake is trusting the shop's listed size without checking. Listed sizes are often rounded, and some shops measure the box rather than the piece. Measure the piece itself when it arrives, before the delivery people leave, and keep the tape handy. The fourth mistake is measuring the room but not the route: the front door, the stairs and the landing are all part of the journey, and the narrowest point on the route is the one that decides. And the fifth mistake is forgetting the ceiling: a tall wardrobe tilted to pass a door needs ceiling height above the stairs, and a low ceiling over a stairwell can stop a piece that fits every width.

What this checker cannot do

This checker cannot see your home. It does not know about the hallway turn, the ceiling above the stairs, the radiator next to the door, or the step at the front entrance. It checks three things — room, doorway and stairs — from the numbers you type in, and those numbers are only as good as your measuring. A piece that passes all three checks can still get stuck on a landing that is too small to turn in, so walk the whole route with a tape before delivery day.

It also cannot judge weight or strength. A piece that fits through the door may be too heavy for two people to carry up the stairs, or too heavy for the floor where it will stand. It cannot tell you whether a flat-pack version exists, whether a window opens wide enough, or whether your building allows furniture to be carried through a shared hallway. For very large or very heavy pieces, ask the shop about delivery: many shops offer a delivery team that measures the route first, and that is money well spent when the piece is expensive.

Questions people ask

How do I measure a sofa?

Measure the length from arm to arm, the depth from the front of the seat to the back, and the height from the floor to the top of the back. Measure with the legs on, then again with the legs off — the legs-off size is what travels through the door. If the back comes off, measure that separately too, and measure the diagonal of the depth-by-height face for the tilt check.

What is the standard doorway width?

There is no single standard that every home follows. Many homes have interior doors around 75 to 90 centimetres wide, and main entrance doors are often wider, around 90 to 100 centimetres — but older houses vary a lot, and door frames narrow the clear opening. Never trust a standard figure: measure your own doorway at its narrowest point with the door fully open.

Can a sofa fit through a window?

Sometimes, if the window opens wide enough and the room is on the ground floor. Measure the clear opening of the window, not the frame, and compare it with the sofa's smallest face. Get permission first if the building is shared, and have two people lift — a sofa through a window is awkward and heavy. Often the legs-off and turn route through the door is easier.

Should I measure diagonally?

Yes, for the tilt check. When a piece is tilted through a door, the diagonal of its depth-by-height face must fit under the door height, while its narrowest side must fit the door width. The checker works this out for you. Measure the diagonal with a tape from one corner to the opposite corner, and compare it with the doorway height.

What if it fits the room but not the stairs?

Measure the stairs, the landing and the turn at the top — the landing is often the real problem, not the stair width. Try taking the legs off and tilting the piece. If it still does not fit, ask the shop about a flat-pack version, or about delivery through a ground-floor window. As a last resort, pick a smaller piece that fits the route, not just the room.

Do I measure with the legs on or off?

Both. Measure with the legs on for the room check, because that is how the piece will sit in the room. Measure with the legs off for the door and stairs checks, because that is how the piece will travel. Legs usually add 10 to 15 centimetres of height, and that difference often decides whether a piece fits through a door.
