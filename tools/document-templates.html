How to use a template

Using a template takes four steps. First, pick the template that matches your job from the buttons above, and read it through once so you know what it says. Second, press the copy button and paste the text into your word processor or notes app. Third, replace every bracketed part, such as [Your Name] or [Date], with your own details, and adjust any sentence that does not fit your situation. Fourth, read the finished letter aloud once before you send it. That final read catches leftover brackets, wrong dates and sentences that made sense in the template but not in your case.

A template is a starting point, not a finished letter. The structure, the polite phrases and the order of information are done for you, but the details must be yours. Change the reason, the dates and the names until the letter sounds like you wrote it for this exact situation, because you did. A letter that still reads like a template, with generic phrases and no real detail, tells the reader you did not care enough to finish the job. Ten minutes of careful editing turns a template into a letter that works.

Match the tone to the reader. A letter to a bank or a government office should be formal and complete, with full names and dates. A note to a neighbour or a small local shop can be warmer and shorter, while still keeping the facts clear. When you are unsure, choose the more formal tone: nobody has ever been offended by a polite letter, while a letter that is too casual for its reader can undo its own purpose. The templates lean formal for exactly this reason, and you can soften the wording when you know the reader well.

Letters for work

Six of the templates cover working life. The job application letter introduces you for a role you want, and it works best when you name one or two achievements that match the job rather than listing everything you have ever done. The resignation letter keeps your departure polite and professional, which matters more than people think: the working world is small, and a graceful goodbye is remembered. The salary certificate request asks your employer for proof of your pay, usually for a bank or a visa, and it helps to say exactly what the certificate must include. The leave application states your dates, your reason and who will cover your work. The recommendation request asks a teacher or manager for support, and it should always give them plenty of time before the deadline. The cover note is the short letter that travels with documents you are sending, listing what is enclosed so nothing goes missing.

Work letters share one rule: be clear about what you want in the first paragraph. A busy manager reads the first lines and decides what the letter is for, so do not bury the request in the third paragraph. State the dates, the role or the document plainly, then give the supporting detail. Keep the tone respectful even when the news is difficult, as in a resignation or a complaint, because the letter stays on file long after the feelings fade. And always keep a copy of what you sent, with the date, so you can prove what you asked for and when.

Letters for everyday life

Two templates cover the letters most people write outside work. The complaint letter is for the day a product or service lets you down: state what happened, when it happened, and what you want done about it, and attach proof such as a receipt or an order number. A calm complaint with clear facts gets results far more often than an angry one, because the person reading it can act on facts and can only ignore anger. The permission letter asks an organisation to allow something, such as a school trip or an event, and it should name the person, the activity, the date and the place, so the reader can approve it without writing back for details.

Everyday letters work best when they are short. Say what you need in the first paragraph, give the facts in the middle, and close with a clear next step. Long letters about simple matters try the reader's patience and bury the point. If your letter runs past one page, ask yourself what can be cut: the reader needs the facts, the dates and the request, and very little else. A short letter that is easy to answer gets answered first.

Money documents

The invoice and the receipt handle money on paper. An invoice tells a client what they owe you: describe the work or goods, list the amounts, give the total, and state when payment is due. A receipt proves money changed hands: name the payer, the amount, the date and what the payment was for. Both documents should be numbered and dated, and you should keep a copy of each one. When a payment is ever questioned, a dated receipt ends the argument in seconds, while a missing one can turn a small matter into a long dispute.

The templates show amounts as [Amount] with no currency symbol, because the same template is used in many places. When you fill it in, write the amount in your own currency and spell out the figure in words for large payments, so there is no doubt about what was agreed. Always record the payment method too, whether cash, bank transfer or another method, and keep the matching bank record or signed copy with the receipt. Money documents are only useful if they are complete, so fill in every field before you send or hand one over.

Agreements and records

The rental agreement outline records the main terms between a landlord and a tenant: who is involved, which property, the dates, the rent, the deposit and the notice period. It is an outline, not a finished legal contract, and both sides should read it carefully and check the rules where they live before signing anything. Writing the terms down, even in outline form, prevents most rental arguments, because disagreements about rent day or notice period are settled by the paper rather than by memory.

The meeting minutes template records what a meeting decided, which is the part everyone forgets fastest. Good minutes are short: the people present, the points discussed, the decisions made, and the actions with an owner and a due date for each one. Send the minutes to everyone who attended within a day, while memories are fresh, and ask for corrections at once. Minutes that arrive a week late are rarely read, and decisions nobody wrote down have a habit of being decided again at the next meeting.

What never to put in a formal letter

Some things do not belong in a formal letter, however strongly you feel them. Never write in anger: a letter written in the heat of the moment will be read cold, and the reader will judge the writer by it. Never include jokes, slang or personal remarks about other people. Never make a promise you cannot keep, such as a delivery date you cannot meet, because the letter is proof of the promise. Never share another person's private information, such as their address or their pay, without their permission. And never put down in writing anything you would not want read aloud in front of a stranger, because letters are forwarded, filed and sometimes shown to people you never expected.

Common mistakes

Template mistakes are small, but they are easy to spot once the letter has been sent.

  • Leaving [Your Name] or another bracketed part in the final copy. It is the most common template mistake, and the final read-aloud catches it every time.
  • Using the wrong dates, especially the year, in a letter copied from an old one.
  • Copying a template written for a different country without adjusting titles, date formats and formal phrases.
  • Sending a letter without reading it once, and discovering the error only after the reply arrives.
  • Using a casual letter for a formal matter, or a stiff formal letter where a friendly note would do.

Each of these is a two-minute fix at your own desk and a lasting embarrassment once the letter has been sent. The template gives you the structure; the final read gives you the letter.

What these templates cannot do

These templates are a starting point, not legal advice. They give you sound wording for everyday documents, but they cannot know the laws where you live, the rules of your employer, or the details of your situation. The rental agreement outline is not a substitute for a proper contract, and no template can replace qualified advice when large amounts of money, employment terms or legal duties are involved. Use the templates with judgment: adapt them, check them, and ask a qualified person to review anything that carries real risk. A template used wisely saves time; a template used blindly creates trouble.

Questions people ask

Are these templates really free?

Yes. Every template on this page is free to copy and use, with no signup and no limit. Copy the text, fill in your details, and use it as many times as you like.

What do the square brackets mean?

Square brackets mark the parts you must replace with your own details, such as your name, a date or an amount. Read the whole template once after filling it in, and make sure no bracketed part is left behind.

Can I use the rental agreement as a legal contract?

The rental agreement here is an outline that records the main terms two people agree on. It is not a finished legal contract. Rental rules differ from place to place, so check the rules where you live and get proper advice before you rely on it.

How do I change a template after copying it?

Paste it into any text editor or word processor, then replace the bracketed parts and adjust the wording to fit your situation. Keep the polite structure, but make the details yours: dates, names, amounts and reasons.

Do the templates work for any country?

The templates use plain, neutral wording that fits most situations, but formal letters and agreements follow local customs and rules. Check how formal letters are written where you live, and adjust dates, titles and terms to match.

Can I use these templates for my business?

Yes, for everyday business letters, invoices and receipts. For anything involving large amounts of money, employment terms or legal duties, have a qualified person review the final document before you send or sign it.
