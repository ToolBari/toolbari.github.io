What this presentation maker does

This page turns a topic into a full presentation, slide by slide. You enter your topic and your audience, choose how many slides you need and what the talk is for, and the page builds the whole deck: a title for every slide, three or four bullet points under each title, and a speaker note telling you what to say. Then you copy the deck — one slide at a time, or all at once — and paste it into your slides app.

It is important to know what the page does not do. It writes words and structure. It does not design slides, choose pictures, or make a .pptx file you can open in PowerPoint. That is a deliberate limit, and it is stated on the page: design is a different job, and the guide below shows how to paste the content into Google Slides in about two minutes, where the design takes care of itself with a simple theme.

The page is also different from an outline generator. An outline gives you slide titles only — a skeleton. This page builds the full content under each title, so you can paste it straight into your slides and start presenting. If you only need titles, the outline generator is the lighter tool. If you need the words too, this page is the one to use.

How to use it

Start with the topic. Write it the way you would say it to a friend: "How bees help farmers" is better than "Bees", because a full topic gives the builder something to shape. Then write the audience — who will be sitting in front of you. "My class" and "new customers" need very different talks, and the builder uses the audience to frame the bullets.

Next, pick the number of slides. Three suits a short talk of a few minutes. Five suits a class project or a team meeting. Ten suits a longer talk or a pitch where you need room for the problem, the solution and the plan. More slides do not make a better talk — one clear idea per slide does — so pick the smallest number that covers your topic.

Then pick the purpose. School shapes the deck around learning: what the topic is, why it matters, what you found out. Work shapes it around a decision: the situation, the problem, the plan, the next steps. Pitch shapes it around persuasion: the problem, the solution, why now, and the ask at the end. The same topic gives a different deck for each purpose, so choose the one that matches your real situation.

Tap the button and read the deck. Copy each slide into your slides app, or copy the whole deck and paste it into a document first to edit it. Read every slide before you present — the words are a starting point written around your topic, and your own facts and examples are what make the talk yours.

What a good presentation needs

A good presentation has one idea per slide. The audience reads the slide in a few seconds and then listens to you — or they read a wall of text and stop listening entirely. Three or four short bullets are enough. If a slide needs more than that, it is two slides, not one.

There is an old rule that helps: no more than six words per line, and no more than six lines per slide. It is a rule of thumb, not a law, but it points in the right direction — short lines, plain words, and white space around them. The decks this page builds follow that shape: short bullets, one idea each, with the detail in the speaker note instead of on the slide.

A good presentation also has a shape: a beginning, a middle and an end. The beginning says what the talk is about and why it matters. The middle walks through the points in an order the audience can follow. The end says the main point once more and stops. The decks here follow that shape automatically — a title slide, content slides, and a closing slide — so the structure is handled and you can focus on the words.

Finally, a good presentation is spoken, not read. The speaker notes exist for a reason: they hold what you say, while the slide holds what the audience sees. If you find yourself reading the bullets aloud, the slide has too much on it. Say the idea in your own words, let the slide show the shape of it, and move on.

Two worked examples

A school project: How bees help farmers. Five slides for a class. Slide one is the title: the topic, the class, and why bees matter. Slide two asks what bees actually do — they carry pollen from flower to flower, which lets plants make fruit and seeds. Slide three gives the key facts: how much of our food depends on pollination, and what happens when bee numbers fall. Slide four is an example: one farm, one season, what changed when the farmer kept hives nearby. Slide five is what the student learned — the main point, said once, and a thank-you. Each slide has three or four bullets and a speaker note. The student adds their own facts and one picture, and the talk is ready.

A business pitch: a lunch delivery service for offices. Ten slides for people being asked to back the idea. Slide one is the title: the service, the audience, the promise. Slide two is the problem: office workers skip lunch or eat badly because there is no time. Slide three is the solution: fresh lunch delivered to the desk by noon. Slide four is why now: more offices, more people working through lunch, more demand for healthy food. Slide five is how it works: order by ten, cooked fresh, delivered by twelve. Slide six is who it helps: busy teams, small offices, anyone who works through lunch. Slide seven is what makes it different: local food, no plastic, a menu that changes daily. Slide eight is the plan ahead: start with five offices, grow to fifty. Slide nine is why the audience should join: the market is ready and the team is ready. Slide ten is the ask — the main point, said once, and a thank-you. The founder fills in the real numbers and the real menu, and the pitch has a shape that holds together.

Notice what both decks share: the structure does the heavy work. The speaker fills in the facts. That split — structure from the tool, facts from the person — is what makes a generated deck useful instead of generic.

From words to slides in two minutes

Once your deck is built, getting it into slides is quick. Open Google Slides in your browser and start a blank presentation. Pick a simple theme — one colour, clean text, nothing busy. Then work through your deck one slide at a time: copy a slide title into the title box, copy the bullets into the body box, and move on. Five slides take about two minutes. Ten slides take about four.

Do not paste the speaker notes onto the slides. They belong in the notes area under each slide, where only you see them — or in a separate document you read before the talk. The slide shows the shape; the note holds what you say.

One picture per slide is plenty, and for many talks no picture is needed at all. If you do add a picture, use one that shows the idea — a photo of a bee on a flower, a picture of a lunch box — not a decoration that competes with the words. And check the slides on your phone before the talk: if a bullet wraps to three lines on a small screen, shorten it.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a wall of text. A slide crammed with paragraphs is not a slide — it is a document held up on a screen, and nobody reads it. If a slide has more than six lines, split it or cut it. The words you cut can be spoken instead.

The second mistake is too many slides. Twenty slides for a ten-minute talk means thirty seconds per slide, which means rushing, skipping and a breathless ending. Fewer slides, spoken slowly, always beat more slides spoken fast. If you are not sure how many you need, pick fewer.

The third mistake is reading the slides aloud. The audience can read. They came to hear you say something the slide does not say — your example, your reasoning, your view. The speaker notes are there so you know what to say; the slide is there so the audience knows where you are. Keep those two jobs separate.

The fourth mistake is skipping the practice. Read the deck aloud once, out loud, standing up, before the real talk. You will find the awkward sentence, the slide that needs one more bullet, and the ending that trails off. Ten minutes of practice fixes all three, and it is the cheapest improvement any presentation can get.

Questions people ask

Does it make a PowerPoint file?

No. This page writes your slide words and structure — titles, bullet points and speaker notes. It does not design slides or make a .pptx file. The guide on this page shows how to paste the content into Google Slides in about two minutes.

How is this different from an outline generator?

An outline generator gives you slide titles only. This page builds full slide content — the bullets under each title and a speaker note for every slide — so you can paste it straight into your slides and start presenting.

How many slides should I use?

Use as few as your topic needs. Three slides suit a short talk, five suit a class project, and ten suit a longer meeting or pitch. More slides do not make a better talk — one clear idea per slide does.

Can I change the words?

Yes, and you should. The deck this page builds is a starting point written around your topic. Read every slide, fix anything that does not sound like you, and add your own facts and examples before you present.

Does it work on my phone?

Yes. Enter your topic, pick your purpose and tap the button. Copy each slide or the whole deck, then paste it into your slides app. The buttons are big and the page fits a phone screen.

What if my topic is very specific?

The builder writes around any topic you type, but it does not know your subject — it cannot invent your facts. For a specific topic, treat the deck as a structure, then fill in the real details yourself.
