How the estimator works

Enter the value of the goods, the duty rate as a percentage, the shipping cost and the VAT rate, all in your own currency. The estimator works out the duty on the goods value, then works out VAT on the goods value plus the duty plus the shipping, and adds everything together into an estimated landed cost — the total you can expect the order to cost once it has crossed the border.

One assumption is built into the sums and it is stated on the page: VAT is charged on the value plus duty plus shipping. What duty is and who sets it

Import duty is a tax charged when goods cross a border, and the rate depends on what the goods are and where they come from. The same item can carry a different rate in different countries, and two similar-looking products can sit in different tariff categories with different rates. The rate is set by the government of the importing country and published in its tariff schedule.

Because the rate depends on the product, there is no single rate this page could safely state. A rate that is right for clothing may be wrong for electronics, and a rate that is right for one country may be wrong for its neighbour. That is why the estimator asks you to enter the rate yourself, and why the warn box tells you to check official customs before you order.

The duty is normally charged on the value of the goods as declared on the invoice or customs form. Understating that value to lower the duty is not a saving — it is misdeclaration, and it can lead to fines, seizure of the goods, or both. Declare the real value and let the estimate tell you the real cost.

Rates also change over time. Governments adjust tariffs for trade policy, for revenue, or in response to agreements with other countries, so a rate that was correct last year may not be correct today. That is another reason the estimator asks you to enter the rate yourself rather than carrying a list of rates that would go out of date. Always check the current rate on the official customs website, and check it again if some time has passed since your last order.

Worked example: an order worth 200

Take an order worth 200 in your own currency, with a duty rate of 10 percent, shipping of 30 and a VAT rate of 20 percent. The estimator works through the figures in order. Duty is 10 percent of 200, which is 20. The VAT base is the goods value plus the duty plus the shipping: 200 plus 20 plus 30, which is 250.

VAT is 20 percent of 250, which is 50. The landed cost is the goods plus the duty plus the shipping plus the VAT: 200 plus 20 plus 30 plus 50, which is 300. The order that looked like 200 on the website and 230 with shipping actually costs 300 once duty and VAT are counted.

That gap — 70 on a 200 order — is why so many importers feel caught out. The duty itself is only 20; the rest is VAT charged on a base that already includes the duty and the shipping. Understanding that the VAT base includes the shipping is the single most useful thing this page teaches.

Why the landed cost surprises people

Most people budget for the price on the website plus the shipping, and forget that duty and VAT are charged on top. The surprise is worse than the duty rate suggests, because VAT is charged on a base that includes both the duty and the shipping. A 10 percent duty rate does not add 10 percent to the order — in the worked example above, duty and VAT together added 35 percent.

Handling and brokerage fees add a second surprise. Carriers and customs brokers often charge a fee for clearing the goods through customs, and that fee is separate from the duty and the VAT. The estimator does not include it, because the fee varies by carrier and by country, but you should expect it and ask your carrier what it charges.

Exchange rates add a third surprise for orders priced in another currency. The duty and VAT are worked out on the value converted into your own currency, and the rate used is the one on the day the goods clear customs, not the day you ordered. A small move in the exchange rate can change the final charge.

What this estimator does not include

The estimator covers duty and VAT only. It does not include handling or brokerage fees charged by the carrier, storage fees if the goods wait at customs, or any inspection fees. It does not apply duty-free thresholds — the value below which some countries charge no duty — because thresholds differ by country and change over time.

It also does not cover exemptions. Some goods are duty-free under trade agreements, some are exempt because of their use, and some categories carry reduced rates. Whether your goods qualify depends on the product, its origin and your country's rules, and only the customs office can confirm it.

Finally, it does not cover restricted or prohibited goods. Some products cannot be imported at all, some need a licence, and some need certificates. If you are unsure whether your goods can legally enter the country, check before you order — the estimator cannot tell you, and a seized shipment costs far more than any duty.

Before you order: a checklist

A few minutes of checking before you order can save an expensive surprise at the border. Work through this list for every import, and keep the answers with the order paperwork so you can check the final charge when it arrives.

  • Find the duty rate for your exact product on the official customs website of your country.
  • Check whether a duty-free threshold applies and whether your order falls under it.
  • Check whether the goods are restricted, need a licence, or need a certificate.
  • Ask the carrier what handling or brokerage fee it charges for customs clearance.
  • Keep the invoice, because the declared value is what the duty is worked out on.
  • Run the estimator with the real rate and the real shipping cost, and compare the landed cost with buying locally.

If the landed cost is close to the local price, buying locally is often the better choice: no border delay, no customs paperwork and a simpler return if something is wrong. The estimator exists to make that comparison honest.

Keep your paperwork after the order arrives, too. If the final charge differs from the estimate, the invoice and the customs paperwork show where the difference came from — a different rate, a fee you had not counted, or a threshold that did not apply. That record makes the next estimate better, because you will know exactly what your last order really cost.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is treating the estimate as exact. It is not. Rates change, thresholds change, and the customs office makes the final decision. Treat the estimate as a planning figure and keep a margin for the real charge being higher.

The second mistake is forgetting that VAT is charged on the shipping too. People work out VAT on the goods value alone and then wonder why the bill is higher. The estimator includes shipping in the VAT base, which is the usual way in many countries — but check your own country's rule, because it is not universal.

The third mistake is entering a rate from memory or from another country's tariff. Rates differ by country and by product, and a rate that was right last year may have changed. Always check the current rate on the official customs website before you rely on the estimate.

Questions people ask

Is this the exact duty I will pay?

No. This is an estimate only. Duty rates vary by country and by product, and the real charge is set by the customs office when the goods arrive. Use the estimate to plan, and check official customs for the real numbers before you order.

Why is VAT charged on the shipping too?

In many countries VAT is charged on the goods value plus the duty plus the shipping, so the shipping forms part of the VAT base. That is how this estimator works it out. Your country may differ, so check the official rule if the difference matters to you.

What if my country has a duty-free threshold?

The estimator does not apply thresholds, because they differ by country and change over time. If your order falls under your country's threshold, the real duty may be lower than the estimate — check official customs to find out.

Does the estimator include handling fees?

No. Carriers and customs brokers often charge a separate fee for clearing goods through customs, and the estimator does not include it because it varies by carrier and country. Ask your carrier what it charges and add it to the estimate.

Where do I find the duty rate for my product?

On the official customs website of your country, in its tariff schedule. You will need to know the product category the goods fall into. If you cannot find the rate, contact the customs office directly rather than guessing.

What if I enter the wrong rate?

The estimate will be wrong by the same margin, because every figure flows from the rate you enter. Always verify the current rate on the official customs website before you rely on the estimate, and re-check it if some time has passed since you last looked.
