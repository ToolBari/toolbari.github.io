How to use this comparison

Enter up to three vehicles, each with its price, fuel economy, seats, boot space, safety notes and yearly running cost. Leave a vehicle blank if you only want to compare two. Tap Compare to see the side-by-side table and a plain-words summary: the cheapest to buy, the cheapest to run, the roomiest, the most economical, and the cheapest over five years when price and running cost are added together.

The Load example cars button fills the form with three common car types — a small hatchback, a family sedan and a small SUV — with clearly labelled example figures. Use them to see how the comparison works before you enter your own cars, and replace every figure with the real numbers for the cars you are actually considering. The example figures are made up for teaching, and they are not prices for any real car on sale anywhere.

The yearly running cost is the number most people guess at, so take a minute over it. Add up a year of fuel, insurance, routine servicing and the small repairs you expect, and use the same method for each car so the comparison is fair. A car that is cheap to buy but thirsty and expensive to insure can cost more over five years than a dearer car that sips fuel — the five-year figure in the summary catches exactly that.

What to compare — and what to ignore

Price is the first number, but it is not the whole story. Two cars at the same price can cost very different amounts to own, because fuel, insurance and servicing differ by model. That is why the comparison shows the yearly running cost and the five-year total next to the price: the cheapest car to buy is not always the cheapest car to own, and the summary says so plainly when that happens.

Fuel economy matters more the further you drive. For a car that covers a short distance each week, the difference between 14 and 18 kilometres per litre is small money; for a car that covers a long distance every day, it is the biggest running cost of all. Work out your own weekly distance before you judge the fuel numbers, and remember that the official economy figure is measured in ideal conditions — real driving usually does a little worse.

Space and safety are the numbers that are hardest to put a price on, and the ones buyers regret ignoring. Boot space decides whether the weekly shop, the pram or the holiday luggage fits. Seats decide whether the whole family travels together. Safety notes — airbags, braking help, stability control, a reversing camera — are worth reading carefully, because they are the difference between a fright and a crash. Compare them honestly, and do not let a low price talk you out of the safety your family needs.

Buying checklist

Before you pay for any car, new or used, work through the same checks. Read the documents: the registration, the service history, and any outstanding finance or accident record the seller should declare. Check the car in daylight, not under showroom lights: look for mismatched paint, uneven gaps between panels, worn tyres, and warning lights on the dashboard. Take it for a proper drive — town streets and a faster road — and listen for noises, feel for pulling to one side, and test the brakes, the air conditioning and every electric window.

Ask the questions a careful buyer asks. Why is the car being sold? Has it been in an accident, and what was repaired? When was it last serviced, and where? Are there any known faults, however small? A seller who answers plainly is a good sign; a seller who rushes you or avoids the questions is not. For a used car, an independent inspection by a mechanic you trust is the best money you will spend — it costs little next to the price of the car and it finds the problems a test drive misses.

Finally, settle the money in writing before you hand any over. Agree the full price including any fees, get a receipt, and make sure the registration transfer is done properly. Never pay in full for a car you have not seen in person, never send money to a seller who will not meet you, and never let urgency — a claimed second buyer, a price that ends today — push you into skipping the checks. The car will still be there tomorrow if it is a genuine sale.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is comparing cars on price alone. A cheap car with high running costs, poor fuel economy and expensive parts can cost more over five years than a dearer car that is cheap to run — which is why this tool shows the five-year total next to the price. Always compare the whole cost of owning the car, not just the number on the windscreen.

The second mistake is comparing a new car with a used car as if they were the same thing. A new car comes with a warranty, no history to worry about, and the steepest depreciation ahead of it. A used car is cheaper, has already lost most of its value, and may need repairs sooner. Both can be good buys, but they should be compared on what each will cost over the years you plan to keep it, not on the price alone.

The third mistake is trusting the official fuel economy figure. It is measured in ideal conditions, and real driving — short trips, traffic, air conditioning, a loaded car — usually does worse. Use the official figure to compare cars with each other, because it is measured the same way for all of them, but budget for about 10 to 20 percent worse in real life, and ask owners of the same model what they actually get.

What this page cannot do

This page does not pull live prices from anywhere, and it cannot tell you what a car is worth today. The figures are entered by you or labelled examples, and real prices depend on the seller, the condition, the mileage and the local market. It cannot inspect a car, check its documents, or tell you whether a seller is honest. It cannot tell you which car to buy — it can only lay the numbers out side by side so the decision is yours, made with the full picture in front of you.

It also cannot see the future. Fuel prices, insurance costs and repair bills all change, and a car that is cheap to run today may not be cheap to run in five years. Use the comparison to understand the trade-offs between the cars in front of you, then check the real prices, drive the cars, and buy the one that fits your life and your money.

Questions people ask

What matters most when comparing cars?

The whole cost of owning the car, not just the price: purchase price plus fuel, insurance and servicing over the years you will keep it. After cost, the things that matter most are space for your family and luggage, and safety features — the things you will notice every day you drive it.

How do I compare running costs fairly?

Use the same method for each car: a year of fuel for your own weekly distance, plus insurance quotes for each model, plus routine servicing. A car that is cheap to buy but thirsty and expensive to insure can cost more over five years than a dearer car that sips fuel.

Should I compare new vs used the same way?

Compare them on what each will cost over the years you plan to keep it. A new car has a warranty and steep depreciation ahead; a used car is cheaper, has already lost most of its value, and may need repairs sooner. Price alone does not capture either side of that.

What is a fair fuel economy figure?

It depends on the size of the car: a small hatchback may do 16 to 20 kilometres per litre, a family sedan 12 to 16, and a large SUV 9 to 12. Those are rough guides — real driving usually does 10 to 20 percent worse than the official figure, so budget for that.

How many cars should I compare at once?

Three is a good number. Fewer and you may miss the better option; more and the differences blur together. Shortlist three cars that genuinely fit your needs and budget, compare them properly, and then drive the best two before you decide.

What checks should I do before paying?

Read the documents, check the car in daylight, take a proper test drive on town streets and a faster road, and ask about accidents, servicing and known faults. For a used car, pay a mechanic you trust for an independent inspection — it costs little next to the price of the car.
