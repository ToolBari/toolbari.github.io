How the risk assessment works

This tool asks you to rate 16 common business risks, four in each of four areas: finance, operations, legal and market. For every risk you choose one of three ratings: not a concern, some concern, or a major concern. Each rating carries points — zero, one or two — and the points are added up into a score for each area and one overall score out of 32.

The overall score is shown as a percentage and a plain level: low, moderate, high or very high. The level is a summary of your own ratings, nothing more. It does not compare you with other businesses, and it does not predict what will happen. Two businesses with the same score can be in very different positions, because the score only reflects what the person filling it in feels worried about.

The most useful part of the result is the list of your top three mitigations. The tool picks the risks you rated highest and pairs each one with a practical first step. Those three steps are your starting point. You do not need to fix everything at once — working through the top three, then running the assessment again, is a steadier way to reduce risk than trying to change sixteen things in one week.

The four risk areas

Finance risks are about money coming in, money going out, and what you hold in reserve. A business can be busy and still be at risk if one customer provides most of its income, if cash reserves are thin, or if short-term debts are larger than the cash available to pay them. The finance questions ask about these pressures directly, because they are the risks that most often force a business to close at short notice.

Operations risks are about how the work gets done day to day. They cover dependence on one supplier, knowledge held by only one person, missing backups of important files, and single points of failure such as one machine or one system that everything depends on. These risks are easy to ignore while things are running well, and expensive to fix in the middle of a failure.

Legal risks are about the rules and agreements around the business: contracts, licences, customer data and written terms. Small businesses often run for years without reading their contracts or writing down their terms, and nothing goes wrong — until a dispute arrives and there is nothing to point to. The legal questions are a prompt to check the paperwork before it matters.

Market risks are about customers and demand. They cover new competitors, falling demand for your main product, rarely asking customers what they think, and depending on a single sales channel. Market risks change slowly and then suddenly, which is why a regular check matters more than a single careful review.

Who should use this tool

This tool is made for owners and managers of small businesses who want a quick, honest look at where their business is exposed. It works for a one-person business and for a team of twenty, because the questions are about the business itself rather than its size. A freelancer can answer them in a few minutes; a shop owner with staff can answer them with the team and compare notes.

It is also useful before a big decision, such as taking on a loan, signing a lease, hiring your first employee, or launching a new product. A decision like that changes your risk profile, and running the assessment before and after shows you what changed. If the overall score jumps, that is worth understanding before you commit.

It is not a replacement for professional advice. It does not look at your accounts, your contracts or your insurance, and it cannot tell you whether a particular risk is acceptable. What it can do is show you, in one page, where your own worries cluster — and that is often enough to decide what to work on next.

How to use it step by step

Start at the top and work down the list. Read each risk statement and ask yourself whether it describes your business today, not last year and not in an ideal future. Choose not a concern if the risk does not apply or is already handled, some concern if it applies but is under control, and major concern if it applies and worries you.

Answer honestly rather than hopefully. The tool only works if the ratings reflect reality. There is no benefit in scoring everything low to get a comforting result — the point is to find the risks worth acting on. If you are unsure about a statement, choose some concern and make a note to check the facts later.

When all sixteen are rated, press the button to see your profile. Read the overall score first, then the four area scores, then the top three mitigations. Copy the profile if you want to keep it or share it with a partner or adviser. Nothing you enter is stored or sent anywhere — the page works entirely in your browser, and closing the page clears your answers.

Worked example

Imagine a small bakery with two staff. The owner rates the finance risks as major, some, major and some concern — 2, 1, 2 and 1 — which gives 6 out of 8 for finance. Operations gets 2, 2, 1 and 0, which is 5 out of 8. Legal gets 1, 2, 1 and 1, which is 5 out of 8. Market gets 2, 1, 1 and 1, which is 5 out of 8. The total is 21 out of 32, which is 66 percent, a high level.

The top three mitigations come from the risks rated major concern: most income from one customer, owing more in the short term than the cash held, and one supplier providing most of the stock. The owner decides to act on the supplier first, because a backup supplier can be found in a week, then to chase the late payers who are causing the cash problem, and then to start looking for a second regular customer.

Three months later the owner runs the assessment again. The supplier risk is now some concern instead of major, and the short-term debt risk has dropped too. The overall score has fallen from 66 percent to 50 percent. The business is not risk-free — no business is — but the owner can see the improvement and knows what to work on next.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is rating everything as a major concern. That usually means the ratings are driven by general worry rather than by the specific statements. Read each statement on its own and ask whether it actually describes your business. A risk you have already handled — for example, backups you already keep — is not a concern, even if the topic worries you in general.

The opposite mistake is rating everything as not a concern because nothing has gone wrong yet. A risk is still a risk if it has not happened. One supplier, one customer and one person who knows how a key job is done are all real exposures even on a good day. The tool is a check on exposure, not a record of past problems.

Another mistake is doing the assessment once and forgetting it. Risks change as the business changes: a new customer, a new supplier, a new rule or a new competitor can move a score in either direction. Running the assessment every few months takes ten minutes and keeps the picture current. It also shows whether the steps you took actually reduced the risks you aimed at.

Finally, do not treat the score as a grade. A high score is not a failure and a low score is not a pass. The score is a starting point for a conversation — with yourself, with a partner, or with an adviser — about what to do next.

What to do with your results

Start with the top three mitigations. Each one has a practical first step, and the steps are deliberately small: find a backup supplier, write down the steps for a key job, set a monthly date to review cash flow. Small steps get done; grand plans get postponed. Pick the one that feels easiest and do it this week.

Then look at the area scores. If one area is much higher than the others, that area deserves a deeper look. A high finance score might mean talking to an accountant about cash flow. A high legal score might mean getting your contracts and terms reviewed. A high market score might mean spending time with customers before spending money on advertising.

Keep a copy of your profile. The copy button saves the scores and mitigations as plain text, which you can paste into a note or a document. When you run the assessment again in a few months, compare the two profiles side by side. The comparison shows what improved, what stayed the same, and what got worse — and that is the real value of the exercise.

If any single risk feels urgent — for example, you cannot pay a bill that is due, or you have discovered you are trading without a licence you need — do not wait for a plan. Deal with that risk first, and get professional help if the amounts or the rules are unclear.

Questions people ask

What is a business risk assessment?

A business risk assessment is a structured check of what could go wrong in a business, so the owner can act before it does. This tool covers 16 common risks across finance, operations, legal and market areas, and turns your ratings into a scored profile with practical mitigations.

How often should I assess my business risks?

Every few months is a good rhythm for a small business, and always before a big decision such as a loan, a lease or a new hire. Risks change as the business changes, so a single assessment goes out of date. Repeating it shows whether the steps you took actually worked.

What does the risk score mean?

The score is the total of your own ratings, out of 32, shown as a percentage and a level from low to very high. It shows where you feel exposed, based on what you rated. It is not a prediction, and it does not compare you with other businesses.

Can this tool predict whether my business will fail?

No. No checklist can predict that. The tool shows where your worries cluster so you can act on the biggest exposures first. A high score is a prompt to take action, not a forecast, and a low score is not a guarantee of safety.

Do I need to share any business data to use it?

No. Everything you enter stays in your browser and is cleared when you close the page. Nothing is stored, sent or shared, and there is no account or sign-up.

What should I do after getting my top three mitigations?

Start with the easiest one and do it this week, then work through the others. Copy the profile so you can compare it with a later run. If a risk looks urgent or involves legal or financial rules you are unsure about, get professional advice before acting.
