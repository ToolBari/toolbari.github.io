How the board works

Add a task with a title, one owner, a priority and a due date, and it appears on the board under To Do. When work starts, move it to Doing. When it is finished, move it to Done. The board shows the count in each column, flags tasks that are past their due date, and lets you filter by owner so one person can see just their own work.

The board is saved in this browser, so it is still there when you come back. Copy the board summary to share it as text — the summary lists every task under its column with its owner, priority and due date. Clear done tasks when the week is over to keep the board focused on live work.

Keep the board small. It is designed for a working week or two, not for a year's worth of tasks. A board with a dozen live tasks is a tool; a board with a hundred is a graveyard. If the To Do column keeps growing, the problem is not the board — it is that more work is arriving than the team can finish.

The board also makes handover easier. When someone goes on leave, their column can be reviewed task by task and each one reassigned to a named person with a fresh due date. Without a board, handover depends on memory, and memory drops tasks. With a board, the handover is a list, and a list can be checked off one item at a time.

Why every task needs one owner

A task with no owner is a task nobody does. A task with two owners is often the same, because each owner assumes the other is handling it. The board asks for one owner per task for exactly that reason: one name, one person responsible, no ambiguity about who should act.

One owner does not mean one worker. The owner is the person accountable for the task being finished, even if others help. If a task genuinely needs two people, split it into two tasks with one owner each, so each part has a clear owner and a clear finish.

Ownership also makes the weekly review possible. When every task has a name on it, the review is a conversation with that person, not a hunt through the team to find out who was supposed to do what. The board's owner filter exists for this: pick a name and see exactly what that person carries.

Priorities that mean something

Three priorities are enough: High, Medium and Low. High means this task comes before other work — it is urgent, or it blocks someone else, or it has a hard deadline. Medium means it matters and should be done this week. Low means it matters but can wait for a quieter day.

The rule that makes priorities work is scarcity. If everything is High, nothing is High, and the team will choose by habit instead of by importance. Keep the number of High tasks small — two or three per person at most — and be honest when a new High task arrives: something else must drop to Medium to make room.

Review priorities weekly, because they change. A task that was Low last week may be High this week as its due date approaches, and a task that was High may have lost its urgency when the deadline moved. A priority that is never reviewed is a priority that slowly becomes meaningless.

Due dates people can keep

A due date is a promise, and promises the team cannot keep are worse than no dates at all. Set due dates with the owner, not for the owner: the person doing the work knows how long it takes and what else is on their plate. A date agreed together is a date the owner will defend.

The board flags tasks that are past their due date and not yet done, so overdue work is visible at a glance. An overdue flag is not a punishment — it is information. It tells you the plan has slipped, and the right response is to ask what is blocking the work, not to blame the owner.

When a date slips, move the date rather than leaving it in the past. A board full of stale due dates teaches the team that dates do not matter. A board where dates are updated when plans change teaches the opposite: dates matter, and they are kept honest.

It also helps to distinguish the due date from the start date. A task due on Friday may need to start on Wednesday, and a team that only looks at due dates will start it too late. If a task needs several days, say so in the title — 'draft the report by Wednesday, final version Friday' — so the owner can see the real shape of the work, not just its deadline.

Running a weekly delegation review

Once a week, open the board and walk through it together. Start with the Done column: acknowledge what was finished, then clear it. Move to Doing: for each task, ask whether it is on track and what it needs. Then look at To Do: check the priorities still make sense and the owners are still right.

The review is also the moment to rebalance. If one person carries most of the Doing column while another has an empty board, move a task. The board makes the imbalance visible; the review is where you fix it. A team that reviews weekly rarely discovers a problem at the last minute.

Keep the review short. Fifteen minutes is enough for a small team if the board is up to date, because the board does the remembering and the meeting only needs the decisions. If the review takes an hour, the board is probably stale — update it daily and the meeting shrinks by itself.

End the review by looking at next week, not just this one. Tasks with due dates in the coming days should already have owners and priorities, and anything without an owner should get one before the meeting ends. A review that only looks backwards leaves next week's problems for next week's meeting, when they will be harder to fix.

What this board does not do

This board does not send tasks to anyone, does not notify owners, and does not sync with your team's devices. It lives in one browser on one device. That is a deliberate limit: the board is a planning aid, not a messaging system, and it never pretends to reach people it cannot reach.

So tell each owner about their tasks yourself, in whatever way your team already communicates. The copy button gives you a plain-text summary of the whole board that you can paste into a message or an email. The summary lists every task under its column with its owner, priority and due date, so nothing is lost in translation.

If your team needs shared live access, a shared board tool is the right answer, and this page does not try to be one. Use this board for your own planning, or for a small team that works from one shared screen, and use the copied summary to keep everyone else informed.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is a vague title. A task called 'sort out the report' means something different to everyone who reads it, while 'finish the sales report and send it to Ana' is clear to all. Write titles so that a stranger could tell what done looks like.

The second mistake is delegating without a due date. A task with no date is a task that will always lose to a task with one. If the date is genuinely flexible, set a date anyway and move it if needed — a date that moves is still better than no date at all.

The third mistake is a stale board. A board that is not updated daily becomes a fiction, and a team that stops trusting the board stops using it. Move tasks on the day their status changes, not at the end of the week, and the board will stay honest.

Questions people ask

Can my team see this board?

No. The board lives only in your browser on this device. To share it, use the copy button to get a plain-text summary of every task, then paste that summary into a message for your team.

What happens if I close the page?

Your tasks are saved in the browser's local storage, so they are still there when you reopen the page. Clearing the browser's stored data will remove them, so copy the summary if you want a lasting record.

How do I mark a task overdue?

You do not need to. The board compares each task's due date with today's date and flags tasks that are past due and not yet done, with the word OVERDUE shown on the task and a count in the summary.

Can a task have two owners?

No — one owner per task. If two people must work on something, split it into two tasks with one owner each, so each part has a clear owner and a clear finish.

How many tasks can I add?

There is no fixed limit, but the board works best with a working week's worth of tasks — roughly a dozen live tasks. If the To Do column keeps growing, more work is arriving than the team can finish.

How do I remove finished tasks?

Move them to Done, then use the Clear done tasks button to remove them all at once. You can also delete any single task with its Delete button, whatever column it is in.
