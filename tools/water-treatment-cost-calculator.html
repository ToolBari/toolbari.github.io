The treatment types explained

A filter is the simplest form of treatment. A jug filter or a tap filter passes water through carbon or a similar material, which improves taste, removes chlorine, and catches some particles. Filters are cheap to buy and easy to fit, but they only remove what they are rated for — a basic carbon filter does not remove lead, nitrates or bacteria, and a filter left unchanged past its life can make water worse, not better.

A water softener deals with hardness. It swaps the calcium and magnesium in the water for sodium, using salt and a resin bed, so the water that comes out no longer leaves scale on kettles and heaters. A softener does not purify water: it does not remove bacteria, lead or nitrates. It is a solution to scale, not to safety.

UV treatment uses ultraviolet light to kill bacteria and viruses in the water as it flows past a lamp. It adds nothing to the water and changes nothing about its taste, but it needs electricity, the lamp needs replacing on schedule, and it does nothing about hardness, lead, nitrates or particles — so it is often paired with a filter that catches those first.

A full treatment system combines several stages — often a particle filter, a carbon filter, and UV or another disinfection stage — to handle a water supply with more than one problem. It costs the most upfront and the most to run, but for a home with a private well or poor-quality supply, it is sometimes the only honest answer. The calculator treats all four types the same way: it works out what the numbers you enter add up to.

What drives the cost

The size of the system is the first driver. A jug filter for one person costs far less than a whole-house system that treats every tap, because the second one must handle far more water, faster. Before you compare prices, decide what you are treating: drinking water only, or the whole house.

Your water itself is the second driver. Water with one problem — hardness, say — needs one stage of treatment. Water with several problems needs several stages, and each stage adds cost to buy and to run. That is why testing your water first saves money: it tells you which stages you actually need, instead of paying for stages that treat problems you do not have.

Installation is the third driver, and it is the one people forget. A jug filter needs no installation. A whole-house softener or UV unit needs plumbing work, a power point, and sometimes a drain. Get the installation quoted separately, and add it to the upfront cost before you compare systems — otherwise you are comparing a system with its fitting against a system without.

The brand and the warranty matter too, but they are harder to price. A system with a long warranty and parts you can buy locally may be worth more than a cheaper one whose filters you cannot find in a year. Ask the seller how long the model has been sold, and whether replacement parts are available where you live.

Filter vs softener vs UV

These three do different jobs, and comparing them by price alone misses the point. A filter improves taste and removes chlorine and particles. A softener removes hardness. UV kills bacteria and viruses. None of them does the others' jobs, so the right question is not "which is cheapest?" but "what is wrong with my water?"

If your water is safe but tastes of chlorine, a filter is enough, and a softener would be money wasted. If your water is safe but leaves heavy scale, a softener is the answer, and a filter will not help. If your water comes from a well and may carry bacteria, UV or another disinfection stage is the one that matters, and neither a filter nor a softener replaces it.

Many homes end up with two stages: a filter to catch particles and improve taste, plus whichever stage solves their main problem. That is normal, and the calculator handles it — enter the combined upfront cost and the combined running cost, and it will work out the totals. The honest comparison is between complete solutions, not between single stages.

The running cost people forget

The upfront cost is the number on the box, but the running cost is the number that decides whether a system is affordable over years. Replacement filters are the biggest part: a cheap system with expensive cartridges can cost more over five years than a dearer system with cheap ones. Always ask the price of the replacement and how often it is needed, and put both numbers into this calculator.

Salt is the running cost of a softener, and it adds up: a softener uses salt every time it regenerates, and the amount depends on how hard your water is and how much you use. Electricity is the running cost of UV — the lamp runs whenever water flows, and the lamp itself needs replacing on a schedule the maker sets.

Servicing is the cost nobody budgets for. A whole-house system should be checked at least once a year: seals wear, lamps age, resin beds lose their edge. A service visit costs money, but a system that fails quietly can cost far more — in bad water, in damaged appliances, or in a repair bill that dwarfs the service it would have prevented.

A worked example

Here is a worked example using numbers chosen only to show the arithmetic — they are not prices from any seller, and your own numbers will differ. Suppose a household uses 200 litres a day, and is quoted a system with an upfront cost of 5,000, a monthly running cost of 100, and replacement filters costing 600 every 6 months.

The replacement share is 600 divided by 6 months, which is 100 per month. Add the monthly running cost of 100, and the total running cost is 200 per month. The cost per litre is 200 divided by 200 litres a day times 30 days — 200 divided by 6,000 — which is about 0.03 per litre. The cost per year is 200 times 12, which is 2,400.

The example shows why the running cost matters more than the upfront cost over time. The upfront cost of 5,000 is paid once, but the running cost of 2,400 a year overtakes it in just over two years. A system that looks cheap on the shelf can be the expensive one by the third year, and this calculator exists to make that visible before you buy.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is forgetting replacement filters. People price the system and forget the cartridges, then discover that the cartridges cost nearly as much as the system every year. Always get the replacement price and interval in writing before you buy, and put them into this calculator.

The second is pricing the system but not the running cost. Two systems with the same upfront cost can have very different running costs — one needs a cheap cartridge every year, the other needs an expensive one every three months. The cost per litre is the fair comparison, because it includes everything the system consumes.

The third is buying treatment before testing the water. A filter bought blind may treat a problem you do not have while missing the one you do. Test first, then treat what the test shows. The test costs money, but it is a fraction of the cost of the wrong system.

The fourth is ignoring installation. A whole-house system that needs plumbing work, a power point and a drain is not the price on the box — it is the price on the box plus the fitting. Get the installation quoted, and add it to the upfront cost.

The fifth is treating the estimate as a quote. This calculator works from the numbers you type in, and those numbers come from sellers whose prices change. Use the estimate to compare options and to plan a budget, then get fresh quotes before you commit to anything.

Every number this calculator shows is an ESTIMATE from the prices you type in. It does not know what systems cost in your area, and it is not a quote from any seller. Get real prices from local suppliers before you buy, and remember that the cheapest system to buy is not always the cheapest to run.

Questions people ask

How much does a water filter cost?

It depends on the type: a jug filter is the cheapest option, a tap filter costs more, and a whole-house system costs far more again. Prices vary widely by place, so get real quotes locally — and always include the replacement cartridges, which are the running cost that matters most.

Do I need a softener or a filter?

It depends on your water. A filter improves taste and removes chlorine and particles; a softener removes hardness and stops scale. Test your water first — if hardness is your only problem, a softener is the answer, and a filter will not help.

What is the cheapest way to treat water?

The cheapest honest answer is to treat only the problem you have. For taste and chlorine, a simple carbon filter is usually enough. For anything else, test first, then buy the smallest system that solves the problem the test shows.

How often do filters need changing?

It depends on the filter and how much water passes through it — the maker's schedule is the one to follow, and it is usually given in months or in litres. A filter used past its life can make water worse, so change it on time.

Is treated water safe to drink?

Treated water is safe to drink if the treatment matches the problem: a filter rated for your water, changed on schedule, does its job. But no single stage removes everything, so match the treatment to your test results, and keep testing.

Why do treatment costs vary so much?

Because the systems vary: a jug filter and a whole-house system are different products, installation costs differ, and prices differ by place. The running cost also varies — replacement filters, salt and electricity all add up differently for each system.
