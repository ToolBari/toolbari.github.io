What this library does and who it is for
A small business usually starts without written HR policies. The team is small, everyone knows each other, and decisions are made by talking. That works until the first disagreement about leave, the first expense claim nobody expected, or the first time someone asks what the rules actually are. Written policies turn those conversations into something everyone can read, agree to, and follow — and they protect both the business and the people who work in it.
This library gives a small team a head start: eight ready-made templates covering the policies a small business needs first — leave, remote work, conduct, expenses, health and safety, data protection, equal opportunities, and probation. Pick a template, add your company name, and the text is ready to copy. Each template is written in plain language, with short sections that say what the policy covers, who it applies to, and what happens in practice.
The library is for owners and managers of small teams who need something solid to start from, not a blank page. It is also useful for anyone setting up a new business who wants the basics in place before the first hire. What it is not is a lawyer: every template is a starting point, and local labour law always wins. The templates say so on the page, and the page says so again here, because a template used as if it were finished law is worse than no template at all.
How to use the library, step by step
Start by typing your company name in the field at the top of the tool. It appears at the top of the generated template, so the text reads as your own document from the first line. If you leave the field blank, the template uses the words Your Company instead, and you can replace them after copying.
Next, choose a template from the list. The eight templates cover the everyday rules of a small workplace. Tap Show template and the full text appears below, ready to read. Read it through before you copy it: check that each section fits how your business actually works, and note the parts you will want to change — the notice you expect for leave, the expenses you will and will not pay, the way remote work is arranged.
Tap Copy template to copy the text, then paste it into your own document. That is where the real work happens: adapt the words to your business, fill in the details the template leaves open, and check the result against local labour law. When the policy is final, share it with the team, keep a copy where everyone can reach it, and note the date it starts. A policy nobody has seen is not a policy.
What the output means
Each template is a complete short policy in plain language. It opens with a purpose — what the policy is for — then covers who it applies to, the main rules, and what happens when the rules are not followed. The sections are short on purpose: a policy that takes twenty minutes to read is a policy nobody reads. The language is deliberately plain, because a policy written in legal jargon is a policy the team cannot use.
Every template ends with the same note: it is a starting point, not a finished legal document, and it must be checked against local labour law and reviewed by a professional before use. That note is part of the output, not an afterthought. It stays in the copied text so that anyone reading the document later can see exactly what it is and what still needs doing.
The output is text, not a signed document and not a system. Nothing is stored anywhere: the template is generated in your browser and the copy button is the only way to take it away. What you do with the text — adapt it, review it, share it, file it — is up to you, and the tips section below explains how to do that well.
One more thing the output does: it gives the team something to react to. A draft policy is easier to discuss than an empty page — people can point at a sentence and say whether it matches how things work. Use the draft to start the conversation, and let the conversation shape the final version. The best policies are the ones the team helped write, because those are the policies the team actually follows.
A worked example
Imagine a small design studio with six people. The owner has never written a leave policy, and the team asks about leave in different ways — some by message, some by asking in person, some by assuming. One summer, two people book the same week off and a project slips. The owner decides it is time to write the rules down.
She opens the library, types the studio name, and picks the Leave Policy template. The text covers the essentials: who it applies to, how to request leave, how much notice to give, how approval works, and what happens to unused leave. She copies it and adapts it: she sets the notice she expects, names the person who approves leave, and writes in the carry-over rule she has agreed with the team. She checks the result against local law and asks a professional to look it over.
Then she shares it with the team, talks through the main points in a short meeting, and files it where everyone can read it. The next summer, leave requests come in early, the calendar shows who is off when, and nobody books the same week by accident. The template did not create the policy — the owner did — but it gave her a solid draft in minutes instead of a blank page that would have stayed blank.
Tips for a good result
Start with the policies that prevent the most arguments: leave, conduct, and expenses. Those three cover the questions a small team asks most often, and they are the ones where a missing rule causes the most friction. Add the others as the team grows or as the need appears — a remote work policy when the first person asks to work from home, a probation policy before the next hire.
Adapt every template before you use it. Change the words to match how your business actually works, fill in the details the template leaves open, and remove anything that does not apply. A policy that describes a different business is worse than no policy, because it creates expectations nobody can meet. Keep the language plain: if a sentence needs a lawyer to understand it, rewrite it.
Share each policy with the team and keep a copy where everyone can reach it. Tell people when a policy changes, and note the date each version starts. Review the policies once a year, or whenever the law changes or the business changes in a way that affects them. A policy that is a year out of date is a policy that may already be wrong.
Limits of the library — what it cannot do
These templates are starting points, not legally complete documents. They do not know the law where your team works, they do not know your contracts, and they cannot replace a professional review. Labour law differs by country and sometimes by region, and a template that is fine in one place may be wrong in another. Check every policy against local law, and get a professional to review anything that affects pay, dismissal, safety, or personal data.
The library does not store, sign, or enforce anything. It generates text in your browser and nothing more: no accounts, no signatures, no records. Keeping the signed copies, tracking who has read each policy, and following up when a policy is broken are all your job, and they matter as much as the words themselves.
A template also cannot fix a workplace problem on its own. A conduct policy does not stop bad behaviour by existing; a leave policy does not resolve a clash by itself. Policies work when people know them, believe in them, and see them applied fairly. Write them well, share them openly, apply them consistently — and the templates have done their job.


Questions people ask


What HR policies does a small business need first?
Start with leave, conduct and expenses — the three that prevent the most arguments. Add health and safety, data protection, equal opportunities, remote work and probation as the team grows or the need appears.
Can I use these templates as they are?
No. Every template is a starting point. Adapt it to your business, fill in the details it leaves open, and check it against local labour law before you use it.
Do I need a lawyer to check an HR policy?
For anything that affects pay, dismissal, safety or personal data, yes — get a professional review. Templates cannot know the law where your team works, and the law differs by place.
What should a leave policy cover?
Who it applies to, how to request leave, how much notice to give, how approval works, what happens to unused leave, and how sick leave is reported. The leave template covers all of these.
How often should HR policies be updated?
Review them once a year, and whenever the law changes or the business changes in a way that affects them. Note the date each version starts so everyone knows which version is current.
Can I copy a template for my own business?
Yes. Tap Copy template and paste the text into your own document, then adapt it. The templates are free to use as starting points for your own policies.
