How the calculator works

Enter the number of pallets you need to store, the length and width of one pallet, how many pallets fit in a row, and the width of the aisles between the rows. The calculator works out how many rows you need, the floor area the pallets themselves cover, the floor area the aisles take, and the gross floor area — the total you must actually rent or build.

The model is deliberately simple and it is stated on the page: pallets stand in rows, and each row has its own aisle beside it. That is a planning model, not a warehouse design. Real warehouses often place rows back to back, use racking that stacks pallets several high, and share aisles between row pairs, all of which change the numbers. The calculator gives you a clear starting figure to discuss with a warehouse provider, not a finished layout.

The output also gives you the usable share — the percentage of the gross floor that actually holds pallets. That single number is often the most useful result, because it shows how much of the floor you pay for is working for you and how much is access. A low usable share usually means the aisles are wide, the rows are short, or both.

Pallet sizes: EUR and GMA

Two pallet sizes dominate world trade, and the calculator offers both as presets. The EUR pallet measures 1200 by 800 millimetres and is the standard EUR size, widely used across Europe. The GMA pallet measures 1219 by 1016 millimetres — 48 by 40 inches — and is the standard North American size, widely used in the United States and Canada.

The difference looks small on paper — a few centimetres each way — but across hundreds of pallets it changes the floor area by a noticeable amount. A GMA pallet covers about 1.24 square metres while a EUR pallet covers 0.96 square metres, so the same number of GMA pallets needs roughly 29 percent more storage floor before aisles are even counted.

Always measure your own pallets rather than trusting a preset. Many businesses use half pallets, quarter pallets or custom sizes, and goods that overhang the pallet edge change the real footprint. The presets are there to save typing when you know your pallets are standard; they are not a substitute for a tape measure.

Worked example: 200 pallets

Take 200 EUR pallets, each 1.2 metres long and 0.8 metres wide, stored 20 to a row with aisles 3 metres wide. The calculator works through the figures in order. Each pallet covers 1.2 times 0.8, which is 0.96 square metres, so 200 pallets cover 192 square metres of storage floor.

With 20 pallets per row, 200 pallets need 10 rows. Each row is 20 times 1.2 metres long, which is 24 metres. Each aisle is 3 metres wide and runs the full 24 metres of its row, so one aisle takes 72 square metres, and 10 aisles take 720 square metres.

Adding storage and aisles gives a gross floor of 912 square metres. The usable share is 192 divided by 912, which is 21.1 percent. That low figure surprises people, but it is honest arithmetic for wide aisles and single-deep rows: the access space is nearly four times the storage space. It is exactly why aisle width is the biggest lever in warehouse planning.

Usable space versus gross space

Usable space is the floor that holds pallets. Gross space is everything you pay for, including aisles, and the gap between the two is where warehouse budgets quietly disappear. A quote for floor space almost always means gross space, so comparing two warehouses by their headline area without knowing the usable share is comparing two different things.

The gap is driven by three things: aisle width, row length and pallet size. Wide aisles and short rows both push the usable share down, because each aisle serves fewer pallets. Longer rows push it up, because one aisle serves more pallets. Larger pallets push it up too, because each pallet position holds more goods per square metre of aisle.

There is no single good number for the usable share. A warehouse with very wide aisles for large forklifts will always score lower than one with narrow aisles and specialised trucks, and both can be the right choice for their own work. What matters is knowing the number before you sign, so the rent you agree reflects the storage you actually get.

Shape matters as well as size. A long narrow building forces short rows and more aisles, which pushes the usable share down, while a squarer building allows longer rows and fewer aisles. Two buildings with the same gross area can therefore hold quite different amounts of stock. When you compare buildings, sketch the rows for each one in the calculator rather than comparing headline areas alone — the sketch tells you more than the brochure.

Aisle width: the biggest lever

Aisle width is usually set by the equipment that must travel down it. A standard counterbalance forklift needs a wide turning aisle, while a narrow-aisle truck or an order picker needs much less. The trade-off is direct: narrower aisles mean more storage per square metre of building, but they also mean specialised equipment and slower turns.

Try the calculator with two different aisle widths for the same pallet count and watch the gross floor change. In the worked example above, cutting the aisle from 3 metres to 2 metres would cut the aisle area from 720 to 480 square metres and lift the usable share from 21.1 percent to about 28.6 percent. That is the same stock on a much smaller floor — or more stock on the same floor.

Never set an aisle width below what your equipment and your safety rules require. An aisle that is too narrow for the truck is not a saving, it is an accident waiting for a busy day. The calculator takes the width you enter, so enter the width your operation genuinely needs.

Planning for growth and peak season

The pallet count you enter should be the count you need at your busiest, not your quietest. Most businesses have a peak — a holiday season, a harvest, a product launch — when stock levels run well above normal. A warehouse sized for the average month will overflow in the peak month, and overflow stock ends up in aisles, which blocks the very access the aisles exist to provide.

A sensible approach is to run the calculator twice: once for the normal pallet count and once for the peak count. The difference between the two gross floors is the space you need to find for a few weeks a year. Sometimes the answer is a short-term overflow area; sometimes it is worth renting the larger space all year. Either way, the decision is now based on numbers rather than hope.

Growth belongs in the same calculation. If your pallet count rises by roughly the same amount each year, project it forward two or three years and check the floor that count needs. Moving warehouse is expensive and disruptive, so a space that fits today but not next year is often the dearer choice.

Also consider how stock arrives. If deliveries come in full pallets that must be put away the same day, you need free positions waiting for them, not a warehouse already full to the last pallet. A useful habit is to keep a small share of positions empty at all times for receiving and for stock that arrives early. The calculator shows the floor for the pallets you enter, so add your receiving buffer to the pallet count before you run it.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is forgetting the aisles entirely and quoting the storage floor as the floor needed. The worked example shows how wrong that is: 192 square metres of pallets need 912 square metres of floor once aisles are counted. Anyone who rents 200 square metres for that stock will be turning pallets away on the first day.

The second mistake is treating gross space as usable space when comparing quotes. Two buildings with the same gross area can hold very different amounts of stock, depending on their shape and aisle layout. Always compare the pallet positions each building can actually hold, not just the floor area on the brochure.

The third mistake is forgetting everything that is not storage. This calculator covers the storage floor only — it does not include receiving docks, packing benches, offices, walkways or the space a forklift needs to turn at the end of a row. Add those separately, with your warehouse provider, before you commit to a building.

Questions people ask

What is a pallet position?

A pallet position is one place for one pallet. In this calculator the number of positions equals the number of pallets you enter, because each pallet needs its own place on the floor. Racking that stacks pallets several high still counts one position per pallet, even though the positions share the same floor area.

How wide should warehouse aisles be?

It depends on the equipment that uses them and on your safety rules, so this page does not state a standard width. Enter the aisle width your forklifts and trucks genuinely need. Try the calculator with two widths to see how much floor the difference is worth.

What is the difference between usable and gross space?

Usable space is the floor the pallets themselves cover. Gross space is the total floor including the aisles between the rows. The calculator shows both, plus the usable share as a percentage, so you can see how much of the floor you pay for actually holds stock.

Does the calculator include offices or loading docks?

No. It covers the storage floor only — pallets plus their aisles. Receiving docks, packing areas, offices, walkways and turning space at the ends of rows all need to be added separately with your warehouse provider.

What pallet size should I enter?

Measure your own pallets if you can. The presets cover the two most common standards: the EUR pallet at 1200 by 800 millimetres and the GMA pallet at 1219 by 1016 millimetres. Goods that overhang the pallet edge make the real footprint bigger than the pallet itself.

How much extra space should I plan for?

Plan for your busiest pallet count, not your average one, and allow for growth over the next few years. Run the calculator once for normal stock and once for peak stock, and treat the difference as space you must find for part of the year.
