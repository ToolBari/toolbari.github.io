One page, one story, numbers
A cover letter is one page. Not two, not a page and a half. One page, three or four short paragraphs, and then you stop. The hiring manager reads it in under a minute, and a second page never gets read at all.
One story is enough. Pick the achievement that fits this job best and tell it properly: what the problem was, what you did, and what happened. One real story beats five vague claims. If you are a junior designer, the story might be the poster series that 2,000 students saw. If you are an accountant, it might be the month-end you closed two days early, eight months running. The story should be yours, specific, and checkable.
Numbers do the heavy lifting. Trained new staff is a claim. Trained 12 new starters, all still with the team after six months is proof. The builder asks for three achievements precisely because three proved claims are what a reader remembers. If you cannot put a number on an achievement, put a scale on it: how many people, how often, how big. And never invent a number — an interviewer will ask how you measured it.
The four parts: hook, story, proof, close
The hook. The first line names the job and the company. That sounds obvious, but many cover letters open with a sentence that could be sent to any employer. Dear Hiring Manager, I am writing to apply for the position of Junior Graphic Designer at Bright Studio tells the reader in one line that this letter is for them. It also helps the ATS, which looks for the job title.
The story. Two or three sentences about how you work — not a list of adjectives, but a way of working. I do my best work where results matter, and I bring a record of delivering them says something real. I am a hard-working team player says nothing. The builder's story line is a starting point; make it yours by adding one detail from your own work.
The proof. Your three achievements, as short lines. This is the part the reader remembers, so put your strongest achievement first. Each line should stand alone: what you did, and the result. If you only have one strong achievement, one line is fine — a short proof section beats a padded one.
The close. One line that asks for the conversation and thanks the reader. Keep it simple and confident. I would welcome the chance to discuss how I can contribute is enough. Never beg, never apologise for applying, and never promise what you cannot deliver.
Tone: formal, friendly or confident
The builder offers three tones, and the right one depends on the employer. A bank, a law firm or a government office usually expects formal: polite, measured, no slang, no exclamation marks. A design studio, a startup or a small creative company usually welcomes friendly: warm, direct, a little personality showing. Confident suits senior roles and sales jobs, where the employer wants someone who will take charge.
When you are unsure, friendly is the safe middle. It reads as human without being casual, and it fits most workplaces. Formal is safe for traditional employers but can read as stiff elsewhere. Confident is powerful when it is backed by real results, and empty when it is not — so only pick it if your achievements support it.
Whatever tone you pick, stay consistent. A letter that opens formally and closes with a joke confuses the reader. Read the finished letter once and check that every sentence sounds like the same person — you, on a good day, writing carefully.
Worked example 1: a junior applicant
Tom is applying for his first design job. He has no full-time experience, but he has real work: a poster series, a competition win, and a magazine cover. The builder turns those three achievements into a friendly letter that sounds like a keen junior, not a template.
Inputs: name Tom Baker, job title Junior Graphic Designer, company Bright Studio, tone friendly, achievements — the poster series seen by 2,000 students, first place in the college design competition, and the magazine cover that lifted sales the following term.
Dear Hiring Manager,

I would love to apply for the role of Junior Graphic Designer at Bright Studio.

I enjoy work where I can make a real difference day to day, and this role looks like exactly that kind of job.

A few things I am proud of:
- Designed the poster series for the university film festival, seen by 2,000 students
- Won first place in the college design competition with a three-poster set
- Redesigned the student magazine cover, and sales rose the following term

I would love to talk about how I could help. Thank you for your time — I hope to hear from you soon.

Best wishes,
Tom Baker
Tom's next step is to edit: add one line about why Bright Studio in particular, and change any sentence that does not sound like him. The draft is the scaffolding; his voice is the letter.
Worked example 2: a career change
Priya taught for years and now wants to coordinate training at LearnWell. Her achievements are all training achievements — induction for 40 new teachers, a handbook the department still uses, a welcome guide that halved new-starter questions. The letter does not apologise for the change of field. It shows that the work is the same work, in a new place.
Inputs: name Priya Nair, job title Training Coordinator, company LearnWell, tone formal, achievements — induction training for 40 new teachers, the training handbook, and the one-page welcome guide.
Dear Hiring Manager,

I am writing to apply for the position of Training Coordinator at LearnWell.

In my current work, I have learned to deliver steady, reliable results, and I would bring the same care to this role.

What I would bring to the role:
- Ran induction training for 40 new teachers over three years
- Wrote the training handbook the whole department still uses
- Cut new-starter questions by half with a one-page welcome guide

I would welcome the chance to discuss how I can contribute. Thank you for considering my application.

Yours sincerely,
Priya Nair
Priya chose the formal tone because LearnWell is a training provider with a professional style. If she were applying to a small startup, friendly would fit better. Tone is a choice, not a habit — pick it for the reader.
Worked example 3: an experienced applicant
James has years in accounting and wants a senior role. His letter is confident because his record supports it: month-end closed two days early for eight months running, a billing error caught before it became an audit, two juniors trained to exam success. Confidence without proof is boasting. Confidence with proof is a good letter.
Inputs: name James Wilson, job title Senior Accountant, company Harbor Finance, tone confident, achievements — month-end closed early eight months running, the billing error that saved a full audit, and two junior accountants trained to first-time exam passes.
Dear Hiring Manager,

I am applying for the role of Senior Accountant at Harbor Finance.

I do my best work where results matter, and I bring a record of delivering them.

Results I can point to:
- Closed month-end two days early for eight months in a row
- Found the billing error that saved the company a full audit
- Trained two junior accountants who both passed their exams first time

I am ready to start delivering from day one, and I would welcome the chance to show you how. Thank you for your time.

Kind regards,
James Wilson
James's letter works because every claim is checkable. An interviewer can ask about any of the three lines and get a real story. That is the test for every achievement you put in a letter: could you talk about it for two minutes? If not, leave it out.
Mistakes to avoid
Sending the draft raw. The builder writes a starting point, not a finished letter. Edit it in your own voice, add one detail only you could write, and read it out loud before sending.
Writing about yourself, not the job. Every line should answer the reader's question: what will you do for us? Your life story is not the subject. The job is.
Repeating your resume. The cover letter is not a second copy of your resume. It is the story behind one or two of its lines. Pick the achievement that fits best and tell it properly.
Two pages. One page, three or four paragraphs. If your letter runs longer, cut the weakest paragraph.
Claiming what you cannot prove. Only claim what you can back up in an interview. A letter that overstates the truth will cost you the job when it is found out.
Forgetting to tailor it. Change the job title, the company and at least one achievement for every application. Tailored applications reached interviews at 5.71 percent in the second quarter of 2026, against 3.09 percent for generic ones — the extra minutes are worth it.


Questions people ask


Do I still need a cover letter in 2026?
Yes, when the employer accepts one. A short cover letter is often the difference between two similar resumes, and it is your best chance to say why this job, not just any job.
How long should a cover letter be?
One page, three or four short paragraphs. A hiring manager reads it in under a minute, and a second page never gets read.
Should I send the letter exactly as the builder writes it?
No — edit it first. Change any sentence that does not sound like you, add one detail only you could write, and read it out loud before sending. Never send raw output.
What if I have no achievements to list?
You have more than you think. A project you finished, a customer you helped, a problem you fixed, a team you supported — any of these counts if you can say what happened because of you. Use numbers or scale where you can.
Which tone should I pick?
Formal for traditional employers like banks and government offices, friendly for most workplaces, confident for senior or sales roles where your record supports it. When unsure, pick friendly.
Can I use the same cover letter for every job?
No. Change the job title, the company and at least one achievement for every application. Tailored applications do far better than generic ones.
