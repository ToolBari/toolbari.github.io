How the queue works

Log each service request with its date, the requester's name, what is needed, a priority and a status. The queue shows every request with its priority and status chips, counts requests by status and by priority, and lets you filter by either so you can see exactly the slice of work you need.

Work a request by moving its status: Received to In progress when work starts, In progress to Completed when it is done, and In progress to Waiting when it is blocked on something outside your control — a part, an approval, or a reply from the requester. Waiting requests can be started again when the block clears.

The queue is saved in this browser, so it is still there when you come back. Copy the queue to share it as text, grouped by status with each request's date, requester, service and priority. Clear completed requests when they are no longer needed.

The queue is designed for a small team handling a handful of requests a day, not for a large support desk. It keeps no history, assigns no one, and sends nothing — its job is to make the current workload visible, so the team can see what is waiting, what is moving, and what is stuck.

Priorities: Urgent means now

Four priorities are enough: Low, Medium, High and Urgent. Urgent means the work starts now and everything else waits — something is broken, someone is blocked, or a deadline is about to be missed. High means it comes first among ordinary work. Medium means it should be done this week. Low means it can wait for a quieter day.

Urgent only works if it is rare. If half the queue is Urgent, the label carries no information, and the team will choose by habit instead of by importance. Keep Urgent for the genuinely urgent, and be honest when a new Urgent request arrives: something else must wait to make room.

Priorities also change as time passes. A Medium request with a deadline this week becomes High as the deadline approaches, and a High request whose deadline moved may drop back to Medium. Review the priorities when you review the queue, because a priority that is never updated slowly becomes wrong.

One useful habit is to agree the priority with the requester when the request arrives. A requester who marks their own request Urgent may be overstating it, and a short conversation — 'is this blocking your work today, or can it wait until Thursday?' — usually settles the question honestly and saves the queue from priority inflation.

Statuses that tell the truth

Four statuses cover the life of a request. Received means it has been logged but nobody has started it. In progress means someone is working on it now. Waiting means work has stopped because something is needed from elsewhere — a part, a decision, or information from the requester. Completed means the work is done.

Waiting is the status most teams get wrong, because it is tempting to leave a blocked request as In progress. But a request marked In progress that nobody is working on lies to the queue: it looks like progress and it is not. Marking it Waiting is honest, and it shows the team exactly what is blocked and why.

A request should never sit in a status it has outgrown. A completed request left as In progress makes the team look busier than it is; a waiting request left as Received hides the fact that work has already begun. Move the status on the day it changes, and the queue will stay honest.

The statuses also give the requester a fair picture when you share the copied queue. A requester who sees their request marked Waiting understands that work has paused for a reason, and a requester who sees it marked In progress knows it is being handled. Statuses that tell the truth build trust; statuses that do not erode it.

Working the queue daily

Start each day by looking at the Urgent and High requests first. Those are the ones that matter most, and they are the ones most likely to become a problem if they wait. Then look at the oldest Received request — the one that has waited longest without being started.

Work the Waiting list too, even though nothing can be done directly. Each Waiting request is blocked on something, and the block often needs chasing: a supplier to call, an approval to ask for, a requester to remind. A short daily check of what is blocked keeps the queue moving.

End the day by updating statuses. A request finished today should be marked Completed today, not next week. The queue is only as honest as its last update, and a team that updates daily will trust the queue — and a team that trusts the queue will use it.

It also helps to tell requesters where their request stands. The queue does not notify anyone, so a short message — 'your request is in progress and should be done by Thursday' — costs a minute and saves the requester from asking. People are patient when they know what is happening; silence is what makes them chase.

What to write in a request

A good request record says what is needed, who needs it, and by when. 'Fix the printer' is a start, but 'the office printer on the second floor jams every third page, needed for the Friday report' tells the person doing the work what to expect and why it matters.

Include the deadline if there is one. A request without a date will always lose to a request with one, because the dated request has a reason to be done first. If the date is flexible, say so in the description — a flexible date is still more useful than no date at all.

Write for the person who will do the work, not for yourself. The requester knows the context; the person picking up the request may not. A few extra words of detail save a round of questions, and the questions saved are time the team can spend doing the work instead.

When the queue keeps growing

If the queue grows week after week, the problem is not the queue — it is that more requests are arriving than the team can complete. The counts make this visible: if Received grows while Completed stays flat, the team is falling behind, and the gap will not close by itself.

The honest answers are the same as for any overloaded team: do less, do it faster, or get more help. Doing less means saying no to Low requests, or asking requesters to wait. Doing it faster means fixing the common causes — if the same request keeps arriving, the fix is in the process, not in the queue.

Use the queue's numbers to make the case. A queue that grows by ten requests a week is evidence, not opinion, and it is far more persuasive than a feeling of being busy. Show the counts, show the oldest unstarted request, and the conversation about help becomes much easier.

Sometimes the answer is to stop the requests at the source. If the same simple request arrives every week — a password reset, a form to fill in, a document to find — a short guide or a checklist can remove it from the queue entirely. The best request is the one that never needs to be logged.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is a vague service description. 'Help with the computer' tells the next person nothing. Write what is needed, where, and by when, and the request will be picked up without a round of questions.

The second mistake is marking everything Urgent. When every request is Urgent, the queue cannot tell the team what to do first, and the team will choose by habit. Keep Urgent rare, and the queue will keep its power to prioritise.

The third mistake is leaving blocked requests as In progress. A request that is waiting on a part or an approval is not in progress, and calling it In progress hides the block from the team. Use the Waiting status honestly, and the blocked work becomes visible — which is the first step to unblocking it.

Questions people ask

Does this tool assign requests to anyone?

No. The queue lives only in your browser on this device. It does not send requests anywhere, assign work to a team, or notify anyone — you decide who does the work and tell them yourself.

What does Waiting mean?

Waiting means work has stopped because something is needed from elsewhere: a part, an approval, or information from the requester. It is different from In progress, where someone is actively working on the request.

How do I find a request?

Use the two filters above the list: one for status (Received, In progress, Waiting, Completed) and one for priority (Low, Medium, High, Urgent). The filters work together, so you can see, for example, only the Urgent requests that are still Received.

Can I change a request's priority?

Not directly. If a priority changes, delete the request and add it again with the new priority, keeping the same date and description. The queue keeps no history, so the re-added request is the current record.

Is the queue saved?

Yes, in your browser's local storage, so it is still there when you reopen the page. Clearing the browser's stored data will remove it, so copy the queue if you need a lasting record.

How do I clear completed requests?

Use the Clear completed button to remove all completed requests at once, or delete a single request with its Delete button. Clearing cannot be undone, so copy the queue first if you need a record.
