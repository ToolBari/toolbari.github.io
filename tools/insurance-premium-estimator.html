What this calculator does and who it is for

An insurance premium estimator gives you a rough range for what insurance cover might cost, before you talk to an insurer. You enter the type of cover, the sum insured, your age band and your location type. The page applies simple illustrative rates, shows the factors it used, and gives you an estimated monthly and yearly range. The range is the point: a single number would pretend to a precision this page does not have.

This page is made for anyone who is shopping for insurance for the first time, comparing cover types, or trying to fit insurance into a budget. It is also useful for someone who has received a quote and wants to sense-check it: if your quote sits far outside the estimated range, it is worth asking the insurer why.

The estimator covers five types of cover: health, home, vehicle, life and travel. Each type has a different illustrative base rate, because the risks are different: vehicle cover costs more per unit of sum insured than home cover, because claims are more common and more expensive. The age band and location type adjust the rate up or down, and the page shows each adjustment so you can see what drives the result.

Everything on this page is an estimate. The rates are simple and illustrative, chosen to show how premiums are built up, not to match any real insurer's prices. A real premium depends on far more: your health, your driving record, your home's construction, your claims history, and the insurer's own costs. This page teaches the shape of a premium; a licensed insurer gives you the number.

The inputs, explained one by one

Cover type is the kind of insurance you want. Health cover pays for medical treatment. Home cover protects a house and its contents. Vehicle cover protects a car or other vehicle. Life cover pays a sum to your family if you die. Travel cover protects a trip. Each type has its own illustrative base rate, because each type carries a different risk.

Sum insured is the maximum amount the insurer would pay out. It is the number the premium is built on: a higher sum insured means a higher premium, because the insurer is risking more. Enter the sum you actually need, not the biggest number you can think of, because over-insuring costs money and under-insuring leaves you short when you claim.

Age band is your age group: under 25, 25 to 40, 41 to 60, or over 60. Age affects risk differently for different cover types, but the estimator applies a simple factor per band so you can see the direction of the effect. Young drivers and older travellers usually pay more, because the claims data says they claim more.

Location type is where the cover applies: urban, suburban or rural. Urban areas usually cost more for vehicle and home cover, because theft and accident rates are higher and repair costs are higher. The estimator applies a simple factor per location type, and shows it, so the adjustment is visible rather than hidden.

The formula and how the result is worked out

The formula is deliberately simple, so you can check it by hand. First, the sum insured is divided by 1000, giving the number of units. Then the units are multiplied by the illustrative base rate for the cover type: 0.9 for health, 0.35 for home, 2.4 for vehicle, 0.55 for life, and 1.8 for travel, per 1000 of sum insured per year. Those rates are illustrative only, chosen to show the shape of a premium, not to match any insurer.

Next, the age factor is applied: 1.25 for under 25, 1.0 for 25 to 40, 1.15 for 41 to 60, and 1.4 for over 60. Then the location factor: 1.15 for urban, 1.0 for suburban, and 0.9 for rural. The result is the estimated yearly premium at the middle of the range.

Finally, the page shows a range, not a single number. The low end is 80 percent of the middle estimate, and the high end is 120 percent. The monthly figures are the yearly figures divided by 12. The range exists because real premiums vary widely between insurers, and a single number would be misleading.

Every step is shown in the output, so you can see exactly which factor moved the price. If the age factor is the biggest driver, you know it. If the location factor is, you know that too. That transparency is the point of the tool: not the number, but the understanding of where the number comes from.

A worked example with real numbers

Take a home insurance estimate. The cover type is home, the sum insured is 200000, the age band is 25 to 40, and the location is suburban. The page works through the formula step by step.

First, the sum insured divided by 1000 is 200 units. The base rate for home cover is 0.35 per unit, so 200 times 0.35 is 70. The age factor for 25 to 40 is 1.0, so 70 times 1.0 is still 70. The location factor for suburban is 1.0, so the middle estimate stays 70 per year.

The range is 80 percent to 120 percent of 70, which is 56 to 84 per year. The monthly range is those figures divided by 12: 4.67 to 7.00 per month. The page shows all of this, with each factor listed, so the reader can check the arithmetic.

Now change one input and watch the result move. Switch the location to urban, and the location factor becomes 1.15: 70 times 1.15 is 80.50 per year, with a range of 64.40 to 96.60. Switch the age band to over 60 as well, and the age factor becomes 1.4: 70 times 1.4 times 1.15 is 112.70 per year. The same cover, the same sum insured, and the estimate nearly doubles, because the factors compound. That is how real premiums behave too.

Common mistakes that give a wrong answer

The most common mistake is entering a sum insured that does not match reality. If you insure a home for twice its value, the estimate doubles, and so would a real premium, while the payout would still be capped at the real value. If you insure for half the value, the estimate halves, but a claim would pay only half of the loss. Enter the sum you actually need.

Another mistake is treating the estimate as a quote. The rates here are illustrative, and a real insurer will ask for far more detail: your health, your driving record, your claims history, your home's construction, and more. Two people with the same inputs on this page can get very different real quotes. Use the estimate to budget and to compare, never to decide.

A third mistake is picking the cheapest cover type without reading what it covers. A low premium that excludes the risk you actually face is not a bargain. The estimator shows a price range; it cannot show what is covered, what is excluded, or what the deductible is. Read the policy wording before you buy.

  • Enter the sum insured you actually need, not the biggest or smallest number
  • Remember the range is illustrative: a real quote can sit outside it
  • Compare the same cover type and the same sum insured when you compare estimates
  • Never buy on price alone: read what the policy covers and what it excludes

How to use the result in a real decision

Use the estimate to set a budget. If the yearly range is 56 to 84, plan for the top of the range, not the bottom, because real quotes can come in above an illustrative estimate. A budget built on the high end leaves room for the real number; a budget built on the low end does not.

Use it to compare cover types. Run the estimator for each type you are considering, with the same age band and location, and compare the ranges. The comparison shows which types of cover are expensive per unit of sum insured and which are cheap, which helps you decide where to spend your insurance budget.

Use it to prepare for the insurer. When you ask for a real quote, you will know roughly what to expect and which factors matter. If the quote comes in far above the estimated range, ask the insurer which factor drove it: your age, your location, your history, or the cover itself. An informed question gets a useful answer.

Finally, get a real quote from a licensed insurer before you decide anything. This page teaches the shape of a premium. Only an insurer can give you a price, and only a policy document can tell you what is covered. The estimate is a starting point, not a finish line.

Questions people ask

Is this a real insurance quote?

No. Every number on this page is an estimate from simple illustrative rates, never a real premium and never a promise of cover. A real quote comes from a licensed insurer, based on far more detail than this page asks for. Use the estimate to budget and compare, then get a real quote before deciding.

What affects an insurance premium most?

The sum insured matters most, because the premium is built on it: a higher sum means a higher premium. After that, the cover type, your age band and your location all move the price, and a real insurer will also weigh your health, your driving record, your claims history and the details of what is being insured.

Why do premiums differ between insurers?

Insurers judge risk differently, carry different costs, and price for different customers. One insurer may be cheap for young drivers and expensive for older ones, while another is the opposite. That is why the estimator shows a range, and why comparing real quotes from several insurers is always worth doing.

What is a deductible and how does it change the price?

A deductible, also called an excess, is the part of a claim you pay yourself before the insurer pays the rest. A higher deductible usually means a lower premium, because you are taking more of the risk. A lower deductible means a higher premium. Choose a deductible you could actually afford to pay if you claimed.

Should I pick the cheapest premium?

Not on price alone. A cheap premium that excludes the risk you actually face, or carries a deductible you cannot afford, is not a bargain. Compare what each policy covers, what it excludes, and what the deductible is, as well as the price. The cheapest policy that covers what you need is the right choice.

What details do insurers ask for before a quote?

An insurer usually asks for your age, your location, what is being insured and its value, your claims history, and details that affect risk, such as your health for health cover or your driving record for vehicle cover. This page asks for only four inputs, which is why its numbers are estimates, not quotes.
