How to use the bill split calculator

Enter the total bill, the number of people, and the tip percentage. Press Calculate and the tool shows the tip, the grand total, and what each person pays — the share of the bill and the share of the tip shown separately, so nobody has to wonder what the tip was. The Copy button copies the whole split as plain text, ready to paste into a group chat.

If people ordered different things, choose Unequal shares. A box appears — enter one amount per line, what each person ordered before tip — and the tool splits the tip in proportion to what each person ordered. The amounts you enter must add up to the total bill exactly; if they do not, the tool says so and asks you to fix them, rather than guessing. The number of people box is ignored for an unequal split, because the lines themselves say how many people there are.

The tip is optional: leave it at zero and the tool splits the bill alone. The tip percentage applies to the whole bill, and it is split in the same proportions as the bill — equal shares mean equal tip shares, and unequal shares mean the tip follows what each person ordered.

The fair ways to split

There are three fair ways to split a bill, and the right one depends on what happened at the table. The simplest is equal shares: everyone pays the same. It is fair when everyone ate and drank roughly the same, and it is fast — one number, no arguments. Most group dinners end this way, and most of the time it is the right choice.

The second way is by what each person ordered. It is fair when one person had a starter, a main and a dessert while another had only a main, or when one person drank and the others did not. The unequal shares box on this page does exactly this: enter what each person ordered and the tool works out each share, tip included. It takes a minute longer, but it removes the quiet resentment that an equal split can leave behind.

The third way is a hybrid: split the shared food equally and the individual items by who ordered them. The shared starters and the shared desserts go in the equal pot; the mains and the drinks go on each person's own line. This is the fairest split of all for a mixed table, and it is easy to do with this tool — work out the shared part as one equal split, then add each person's own items to their share.

What is not fair is the split where one person quietly pays more than their share every time. If the same person always covers the gap, the group has a problem that no calculator can fix — but a calculator that shows every share in black and white makes the problem harder to ignore.

Rounding so the total still matches

Dividing a bill rarely comes out even. A bill of 1,000 split three ways is 333.33 each, which adds up to 999.99 — one paisa short. Someone has to pay the missing paisa, or the total does not match the bill. This calculator handles it by giving the last person the leftover amount, so the shares always add up to the grand total exactly:

Total bill: ₹1,000.00
Tip (0%): ₹0.00
Grand total: ₹1,000.00
Person 1: ₹333.33 + ₹0.00 tip = ₹333.33
Person 2: ₹333.33 + ₹0.00 tip = ₹333.33
Person 3: ₹333.34 + ₹0.00 tip = ₹333.34

The difference is one paisa, and it falls on the last person. The same rule applies to the tip: it is split in proportion and the last person takes any leftover paisa, so the tip shares add up to the tip exactly too. No rounding is hidden anywhere — the shares, the tip shares and the grand total all add up, and you can check the arithmetic from the output.

In practice, most groups round to the nearest rupee and let the last paisa go. That is fine for a meal among friends. But when the split is written down — for flatmates, for a trip fund, for a shared expense sheet — the exact figures matter, and a total that does not add up is the first thing anyone notices.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is forgetting the tip. A group splits the bill, everyone pays their share, and then someone notices the tip was never added — and the last person at the table covers it. Enter the tip before you calculate, and the shares include it from the start.

The second mistake is splitting equally when the spending was not equal. One person had a salad and water; another had a steak and two drinks. An equal split is not fair here, and everyone knows it — including the person who benefits. Use the unequal shares box and the problem disappears.

The third mistake is entering unequal shares that do not add up to the total. It happens when someone's order is forgotten, or a shared item is left out. The tool catches this and refuses to calculate until the shares match the total — which is the point: a split that does not add up to the bill is a split that leaves someone short.

The fourth mistake is rounding each share and then wondering why the total is off. Rounding is fine for a quick split among friends, but if the money is being tracked — a trip fund, a flat expense sheet — use the exact figures the calculator gives, where the last person takes the leftover paisa and everything adds up.

Questions people ask

How do I split a bill equally?

Enter the total bill, the number of people and the tip percentage, then press Calculate. Each person pays the same share of the bill and the same share of the tip, and the shares always add up to the grand total.

How do I split a bill unequally?

Enter one amount per line in the unequal shares box — what each person ordered, before tip. The tool splits the tip in proportion to what each person ordered. The amounts must add up to the total bill exactly.

Why does the last person pay one paisa more sometimes?

Because a bill rarely divides evenly. A 1,000 bill split three ways is 333.33 each, which adds up to 999.99 — one paisa short. The last person takes the leftover paisa so the shares always add up to the grand total exactly.

Is the tip split equally or by what each person ordered?

It follows the split you chose. With equal shares, everyone pays the same tip. With unequal shares, the tip is split in proportion to what each person ordered — the person who spent more pays more tip.

What if my unequal shares do not add up to the total?

The calculator shows an error and asks you to fix them. Check for a forgotten item or a typing mistake — a shared starter or a drink is the usual culprit — and make the lines add up to the total bill exactly.

Can I use this for flat bills or trip expenses?

Yes. The same tool works for any shared expense — rent, electricity, internet, groceries, or a trip fund. Use unequal shares when the split is not equal, such as rooms of different sizes, and copy the result for your records.
