What this trainer does

Learning a new language starts with words. Grammar rules help you build sentences, and listening practice helps you understand people, but without words you have nothing to build with. A learner who knows a few hundred common words can already ask for directions, read a simple notice, follow a short conversation, and write a short message. This trainer is built around that simple idea: learn real words, one at a time, with a clear meaning and a real example sentence, and then test yourself.

This page is a small vocabulary trainer that works entirely in your browser. It holds three sets of words: everyday words for home, travel and daily life, school words for lessons and study, and work words for the office and the job. Every word in the trainer comes with a short meaning in plain English and one example sentence that shows the word in use. There are no word lists to download and no account to create. You open the page, pick a set, and start learning.

The trainer shows you one word at a time. That is a deliberate choice. A long list of fifty words looks impressive, but most learners remember very little of it an hour later. One word at a time forces you to slow down and pay attention to the spelling, the meaning and the example. When you feel ready, you move on to the next word. When you have worked through a few words, you can press the quiz button and test yourself with a short round of questions about meanings and spelling.

Your progress is saved on your own device, in your browser. The trainer remembers which words you have seen in each set and your best quiz score, so you can see how far you have come when you return. Nothing is sent anywhere and nothing is stored on a server, because there is no server involved at all. The whole trainer is this one page.

How to use the trainer

Using the trainer is simple, and it works the same way on a phone and on a desktop computer. On a phone, you tap the buttons with your finger. On a desktop, you click them with the mouse. The buttons are large on purpose, so they are easy to tap on a small screen, and the word card is wide enough to read comfortably at any size.

Start by choosing a word set from the list at the top of the trainer. If you are not sure where to begin, start with the everyday set. Those are the words you will meet most often in daily life, and they make the other sets easier later. The school set is useful if you are a student or you help a child with schoolwork. The work set is useful if you use English in an office or you are preparing for a job.

Once you pick a set, the trainer shows you the first word. Read the word, then read its meaning, then read the example sentence slowly. Try to picture the meaning in your mind. If the word is kitchen, picture your own kitchen. If the word is journey, think of a trip you have taken. A picture in your mind makes the word much easier to remember than a plain definition alone.

When you are ready, press the Next word button. The trainer shows the words in a mixed order, so you never know which word is coming next. That is good for learning, because it stops you from memorising the order of a list instead of the words themselves. You can go through the whole set, or stop after a few words and come back later. Your progress is saved either way.

After you have seen some words, press the Start quiz button to test yourself. The quiz asks five questions from the set you chose. Some questions ask what a word means, and some ask how to spell a word. Each question gives you four choices. Pick the one you think is right, and the trainer tells you at once whether you were correct. At the end of the quiz you see your score, and your best score for that set is saved.

If you want to study away from the screen, press the Copy this word list button. It copies the whole word list for the set you chose, with meanings and examples, onto your clipboard. You can paste it into a note, send it to yourself, or print it and study it on paper. Some learners like to keep a paper list beside their desk and glance at it during the day.

How to remember a word

Seeing a word once is not the same as knowing it. Most learners need to meet a word several times before it stays in memory, and the way you meet it matters more than the number of times. Three simple steps make a word stick: see it, say it, and use it in your own sentence.

See it means looking at the word carefully. Notice how it is spelled, especially the parts that surprise you. English spelling is full of silent letters and unexpected patterns, and the only way to learn them is to look. Read the meaning and the example sentence too, and think about how the word fits into the sentence. A word in a sentence is much easier to remember than a word on its own, because the sentence gives it a place to live.

Say it means reading the word out loud. Whisper it if you are in a quiet place, or say it in a normal voice if you can. Hearing your own voice say the word gives your memory a second way in. Many learners only ever read words silently, and then find they cannot say them when they need to speak. Saying each word out loud as you learn it is a small habit that pays off every time you open your mouth in English.

Use it means making your own sentence with the word. This is the step most learners skip, and it is the most important one. A sentence you made yourself is yours in a way that a sentence from a book never is. If you learn the word neighbour, make a sentence about your own neighbour. If you learn the word deadline, make a sentence about a real deadline you have. It does not need to be a long or clever sentence. One honest sentence about your own life is worth more than ten sentences copied from somewhere else.

Come back to the words later. Memory fades fast, and the best cure is a short review the next day, then again a few days after that. The trainer helps here because the words appear in a mixed order each time, so a review never feels like reading the same list twice. Five minutes of review a day will do more for your memory than one long session a week.

The three word sets

The everyday set holds words for home, travel, shopping and daily life. These are words like kitchen, neighbour, journey, umbrella and wallet. They are the words you need to talk about your own day: where you live, what you eat, how you travel, what you buy. If you can use these words comfortably, you can handle a large part of everyday conversation in English, from asking for help in a shop to describing your home to a new friend.

The school set holds words for lessons and study. These are words like lesson, library, exam, homework, teacher and notebook. They are useful for students, for parents who talk to teachers, and for anyone who is taking a course in English. School words also show up in everyday life more than you might expect, in notices, in forms, and in conversations about children and their studies.

The work set holds words for the office and the job. These are words like meeting, deadline, colleague, manager, salary and interview. They are useful if you work in English, if you are looking for a job, or if you want to understand work emails and notices. Many of these words are also used outside work, so learning them pays off in more than one place. An appointment, for example, can be with a manager or with a dentist.

Each set has twenty one words, so the whole trainer holds sixty three words. That may sound like a small number, and it is small on purpose. A trainer with thousands of words is a dictionary, not a trainer, and a dictionary does not teach you anything by itself. Sixty three well chosen words, learned properly and used in your own sentences, will do more for your English than a thousand words you glanced at once and forgot.

You can work through the sets in any order, but starting with the everyday set is the best choice for most learners. The words there are the most common, and they give you the most chances to practise in real life. When you know most of them, move on to the school set or the work set, whichever fits your life better.

How the quiz mode works

The quiz mode turns learning into a short test. When you press the Start quiz button, the trainer picks five words from the set you chose and asks you five questions. The questions come in two kinds. A meaning question shows you a word and asks what it means, with four meanings to choose from. A spelling question shows you a meaning and asks you to pick the correct spelling of the word, again with four choices.

Four choices is enough to make you think without making the quiz feel like a guessing game. The wrong answers are always real meanings or real looking spellings from the same set, so you have to know the word to pick the right one. If you guess, you will sometimes be right by luck, but over five questions luck does not carry you far. That is the point: the quiz shows you honestly which words you know and which ones you still need to practise.

After each answer, the trainer tells you at once whether you were right. If you were wrong, it shows you the correct answer so you can learn from the mistake straight away. There is no reason to wait until the end to find out. Learning from a mistake is easiest in the moment when the question is still fresh in your mind.

At the end of the quiz you see your score out of five. The trainer saves your best score for each set, so you can try to beat it next time. A perfect score does not mean you know the words forever. It means you knew them today. Come back tomorrow and try again. If the score holds up, the words are really starting to stick.

The quiz is practice, not an exam. Nobody sees your score but you, and there is no pass mark. A low score is useful information, not a failure. It tells you which words need another look. Go back to the word card, read those words again, make a sentence with each one, and take the quiz again when you feel ready.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is trying to learn too many words in one sitting. Fifty new words in an evening feels productive, but most of them will be gone by the morning. Memory works better with small amounts repeated often. Five to ten new words a day is a good target for most learners. That is enough to make real progress over a month, and small enough that each word gets the attention it deserves.

The second mistake is learning words and never using them. A word you have only read is a word you only half know. You need to say it out loud and write it in your own sentence before it becomes truly yours. If you cannot think of a sentence for a word, that is a sign you do not understand it well enough yet. Read the example again, look at the meaning once more, and think of a person or a place in your own life that the word fits.

The third mistake is studying only from a list in the same order every time. When the words always appear in the same order, you start to remember the order instead of the words. You know the third word because it comes after the second one, not because you know the word itself. The trainer avoids this by mixing the order every time you choose a set, and the quiz picks its words at random for the same reason.

The fourth mistake is skipping the review. Learners often move on to new words and never look back, and then wonder why the old words have faded. Review is not wasted time. It is the part of learning that turns a new word into a known word. A quick run through a set you finished last week takes only a few minutes, and it will save you from having to learn the same words twice.

The fifth mistake is treating spelling as unimportant. In English, spelling matters, because many words sound alike but are spelled differently, and a misspelled word can change the meaning of a sentence. The quiz asks about spelling for a reason. When you learn a word, look at its spelling as carefully as you look at its meaning, and write the word down at least once by hand if you can.

What this trainer cannot do

This trainer teaches common words with short meanings, and that is all it tries to do. It does not teach grammar, so it will not show you how to build sentences or when to use one tense rather than another. It does not teach pronunciation, because there is no sound on this page, so you will need to hear the words spoken somewhere else if you are unsure how they sound. And it does not cover every meaning of a word. Many English words have several meanings, and this trainer gives you the most common one to start with.

That is why the trainer is a starting point, not a dictionary. When you meet a word in a book or a conversation and the short meaning here does not fit, look it up in a dictionary and see its other meanings. That is normal, and it is part of learning. No trainer can replace reading, listening and talking to real people, but a solid set of words makes all of those things easier.
