How the quizzes work

This platform offers three timed multiple-choice quizzes on the basics of programming: variables, loops and data structures. Each quiz has eight questions, and you have thirty seconds for each one. When the time runs out on a question, the quiz moves on and counts that question as unanswered.

To start, pick a topic and press the start button. Read each question, tap the answer you think is right, and press next. You can change your answer before pressing next by tapping a different option. At the end you see your score out of eight, plus a review of every question with the correct answer and a short explanation.

The timer is part of the challenge. Thirty seconds is enough to read a short question and think, but not enough to look things up. That is deliberate: the quizzes test what you know, not what you can search for. If you run out of time on a question, treat it as a topic to study rather than a failure.

What the three topics cover

Variables is the starting point. The questions cover what a variable is, how assignment works, what makes a valid name, and the basic types of value such as numbers and text. If you are new to programming, start here — everything else builds on variables.

Loops covers repetition: for loops, while loops, how many times a loop runs, and the break and continue commands. Loop questions often ask you to trace a small piece of code in your head, which is a skill worth practising on its own. Counting loop runs carefully is the habit that prevents the classic off-by-one error.

Data structures covers the basic ways programs organise values: arrays, stacks, queues, dictionaries, sets and linked lists. The questions test what each structure is for and how it behaves, not how to build one from scratch. Knowing which structure fits a job is one of the most useful skills in programming.

Who the quizzes are for

The quizzes are for people learning to code, whether on their own, in a class, or as part of a career change. They suit complete beginners who have read an introduction to programming and want to check what stuck, and they suit people returning to code after a break who want a quick refresher.

Teachers can use the quizzes as a warm-up or a quick check at the end of a lesson. Because each quiz takes only a few minutes, it fits into a class without taking over. The explanations after each question give the teacher something to discuss when a question splits the room.

The quizzes do not run code and do not test writing programs. They test understanding of concepts. Writing code is a separate skill that needs practice on its own. Use these quizzes to check your foundations, then practise writing small programs to build on them.

How to use the platform step by step

Choose a topic from the list. If you are unsure where to start, begin with variables, then loops, then data structures — that is the order the ideas build on each other. Press start and the first question appears with the timer running.

Read the question and the four options. Tap the option you think is correct — it will be highlighted. If you change your mind, tap a different option. When you are ready, press next to move on. The timer counts down from thirty seconds; if it reaches zero, the question is marked as unanswered and the quiz moves on.

After the eighth question, your score appears with a full review. Read the explanations for every question you missed, and for any you guessed right. The review is where the learning happens. Copy your result if you want to keep a record, then try another topic or retake the same quiz to see if you improve.

Worked example

Suppose you take the loops quiz and answer six of the eight questions correctly. Your score is 6 out of 8. The review shows you missed the question about how many times for (i = 0; i < 10; i += 2) runs, and the question about what continue does.

The explanation for the loop question walks through the values: i takes 0, 2, 4, 6 and 8, which is five values, not ten. The explanation for continue says it skips to the next run of the loop, while break stops the loop entirely. Both are common points of confusion, and both are worth reading twice.

You retake the quiz the next day and score 8 out of 8. That does not mean you have mastered loops — it means you now know the answers to these eight questions. The real test is writing a loop in a program and having it run the right number of times. Use the quiz as a checkpoint, not as a finish line.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is rushing. Thirty seconds feels short, but it is enough to read the question twice. Many wrong answers come from reading too fast — for example, missing the word not in a question, or confusing the loop condition i < 3 with i <= 3. Slow down and read the whole question before tapping.

Another mistake is guessing without thinking. A guess has a one-in-four chance, but a thoughtful answer teaches you something even when it is wrong, because the explanation corrects your reasoning. If you truly do not know, eliminate the options you are sure are wrong first, then choose among the rest.

A third mistake is treating the score as a grade. The quizzes are practice, not an exam. A low score on your first try is normal and useful — it shows exactly which ideas to study. Retaking the same quiz until you score full marks is less useful than studying the topics you missed and then moving on to writing code.

Practising between quizzes

The quizzes work best as part of a short study habit. Take one quiz, read the explanations, then spend ten minutes writing a small program that uses the idea you missed. If you missed a question about loops, write a loop that prints the numbers from 1 to 10, then change it to print only the even numbers. If you missed a question about arrays, write a program that stores five names in a list and prints the third one.

Writing code turns recognition into skill. Reading about a loop and writing a loop are different things, and the gap only closes by typing. Start with programs of five or ten lines. A program that adds up the numbers in a list, or that prints a simple pattern of stars, uses variables, loops and a list together — the three topics in one place.

When your program does not work, read the error message before changing anything. Error messages in most languages point to the line with the problem and describe what went wrong. Learning to read them is a skill in itself, and it saves hours of guessing. If the message does not make sense, search for its exact words — someone else has almost always asked about the same message.

Finally, keep a small notebook of what you learn. Write down one new idea after each study session, in your own words. A notebook of twenty short notes, built over a month, is worth more than a weekend of cramming. Return to the quizzes after a week and retake the topic you found hardest — the improvement shows what stuck.

What the quizzes cannot do

These quizzes test recognition, not production. Knowing what a loop does is different from writing one that works. The quizzes cannot check your code, because they do not run code at all. They are a starting point for learning, not a measure of programming skill.

The quizzes also cannot give you a certificate or a qualification. There is no login, no record of your scores, and no proof of completion. If you need a certificate for a course or a job, you will need a proper assessment from a training provider.

Finally, the quizzes cover only the basics. Real programming involves much more: functions, objects, files, networks, errors and testing. Think of these three topics as the first steps. Once you are comfortable with them, the next step is to write small programs that use variables, loops and lists together.

Questions people ask

How long does each quiz take?

Each quiz has 8 questions with 30 seconds per question, so a full quiz takes at most 4 minutes. Most people finish faster. The review at the end has no time limit, so you can read the explanations at your own pace.

What happens if time runs out on a question?

The quiz moves on to the next question and counts the unanswered one as wrong. The review shows it as no answer, with the correct answer and the explanation, so you can still learn from it.

Do I need an account to take a quiz?

No. There is no login, no sign-up and no record of your scores. Everything runs in your browser, and closing the page clears your result.

Can the quizzes check my code?

No. The quizzes are multiple-choice questions about programming concepts. They do not run code and cannot check a program you have written. They test understanding, not coding skill.

Which topic should I start with?

Start with variables, then loops, then data structures. That is the order the ideas build on each other. If you already know variables well, skip ahead to the topic you are least sure about.

Will I get a certificate for passing?

No. These quizzes are practice only, with no certificate or qualification. They are a quick way to check what you know before moving on to writing real programs.
