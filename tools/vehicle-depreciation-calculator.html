How to use this calculator

Enter what the car cost when it was bought, the year it was bought, and the year you want to value it in. Then pick a rate preset or set your own rates: the first-year rate applies to the first year after purchase, when a car loses the most value, and the later-year rate applies to every year after that. The calculator works out the value at the end of each year, shows the full table, and gives you the current estimated value and the total loss.

The typical preset uses 20 percent for the first year and 12 percent for each later year. Those are common planning figures for an ordinary car in ordinary use — the first year is the expensive one, because a new car becomes a used car the moment it is driven away, and the later years settle into a steadier fall. If your car is a popular model that holds its value well, the later rate may be closer to 10 percent; if it is a rare or thirsty model, it may be closer to 15. The custom option lets you set both rates yourself.

Read the table from top to bottom: each row shows the value at the end of that year and how much was lost during it. The loss is biggest in the first row and shrinks each year after, because the same percent applies to a smaller value each time. That is the shape of depreciation — a steep drop, then a long gentle slope — and it is why the cheapest years of owning a car are usually the later ones, not the first ones.

How depreciation works

Depreciation is the fall in a car's value as it gets older and more used. It is not a fee anyone charges you — it is what the market will pay for the car now compared with what it paid when the car was new. A new car loses value fastest because buyers will pay a premium for a car nobody has driven, with a full warranty and the newest features. Once the car has an owner and some miles on it, that premium is gone, and the value falls to what a used buyer will pay.

The fall is usually worked out as a percent of the car's current value, not of its original price. That matters: a 12 percent fall on a car worth 1500000 is 180000, but the same 12 percent on the same car a few years later, when it is worth 800000, is only 96000. The percent stays the same while the money falls each year, which is why the table's losses shrink as you read down it. A straight-line fall — the same amount every year — is simpler but less realistic, because real cars do not lose a fixed amount each year.

Three things move the rate more than anything else: how far the car has been driven, what condition it is in, and how much people want that model. A car with low mileage and a full service history holds its value better than the same model with high mileage and dents. A popular model with cheap parts holds its value better than a rare one. And the local market matters too — the same car can be worth more in a city where it is in demand than in a place where nobody wants it.

Worked example: a new car

A buyer pays 1500000 for a new car in 2026 and wants to know what it will be worth in 2031, five years later, using the typical preset of 20 percent in the first year and 12 percent after. At the end of the first year the car is worth 80 percent of 1500000, which is 1200000 — a loss of 300000 in a single year. That first-year drop is the biggest cost of buying new, and it is the number most buyers underestimate.

The later years are gentler. At the end of year two the car is worth 88 percent of 1200000, which is 1056000. Year three takes it to 929280, year four to 817766, and year five to 719634. The total loss over five years is 1500000 minus 719634, which is 780366 — more than half the purchase price gone in five years. That is normal for an ordinary car, and it is why buying a car that is already two or three years old is often the cheaper way to own one.

Now try the steady 10 percent preset on the same car. After five years the value is 1500000 times 0.9 five times over, which is 885735 — about 166000 more than the typical preset gives. The difference between the two presets is the difference between a car that holds its value well and one that does not, and it shows why the rate matters more than the starting price. Before you buy, look at what three-year-old examples of the same model actually sell for, and pick the rate that matches reality.

Worked example: a five-year-old car

A car bought for 1500000 in 2021 is now five years old, and the owner wants its current value in 2026. Using the typical preset, the first year took it to 1200000, and four more years at 12 percent take it to 719634 — the same arithmetic as the example above, read as history instead of forecast. The total loss so far is 780366, and the car is now worth less than half of what it cost new.

But the next five years look very different. From 719634, five more years at 12 percent take the car to about 380000 — a further loss of about 340000, less than half of what the first five years cost. The yearly loss in money terms has fallen from 300000 in year one to about 86000 in year six, and it keeps shrinking — by year ten it is about 52000 — because the same percent applies to a much smaller value each time. That is why keeping a car for ten years is usually far cheaper per year than replacing it every three: the expensive years are behind you.

The owner can also use the table to decide about selling. If a buyer offers 650000, that is below the estimate of 719634 — but the estimate assumes average condition and mileage. If the car has high mileage or needs work, 650000 may be fair; if it is in excellent condition with a full history, the owner can hold out for more. The calculator gives a starting point for the negotiation, not the final word.

What affects depreciation most

Mileage is the first thing a buyer looks at after the year, and high mileage pushes the value down faster than the year alone would. A car that has covered twice the average distance for its age is worth noticeably less than the table suggests, while a genuinely low-mileage car can be worth more. If your car has high mileage, raise the later-year rate by a few points; if it has very low mileage, lower it. The calculator cannot see the odometer, so you have to adjust for it yourself.

Condition is the second factor, and it is the one you control. A full service history, clean bodywork, working air conditioning and good tyres all hold the value up, while dents, warning lights, a stained interior and missing paperwork push it down. Spending a little on cleaning, small repairs and a service before selling usually pays back more than it costs, because buyers pay for the car they can see, not the car the table describes.

Brand and model are the third factor, and they are fixed the day you buy. Some brands hold their value because they are trusted, cheap to repair, or in short supply; others fall fast because they are expensive to run or hard to sell on. Fuel economy matters more than it used to, because running costs are part of what a buyer pays for. Before buying a car, look at what three-year-old examples sell for — that tells you more about depreciation than any brochure.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is using one rate for every year. A single steady rate misses the first-year drop, which is the biggest loss of all, and it makes a new car look cheaper to own than it really is. The typical preset exists for this reason: 20 percent in the first year, then a steadier rate after. If you use a single rate, use it only for a car that is already a few years old, where the first-year drop is history.

The second mistake is confusing the table's value with a selling price. The table is an estimate from a formula, not a quote from a buyer. Real prices depend on who is buying, how quickly you need to sell, and what similar cars are actually advertised for in your area. Use the table to plan and to negotiate, then check real asking prices for your model before you set a price or accept an offer.

The third mistake is forgetting the costs of keeping the car. Depreciation is only one part of what a car costs: fuel, insurance, servicing, repairs and tax all add up, and an old car that has stopped depreciating can still be expensive if it needs frequent repairs. A full picture of car costs needs the running costs too, not just the value table — which is why this site also has a comparison tool that puts purchase price and yearly running cost side by side.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot value your specific car. It cannot see the mileage, the condition, the service history, the colour, or the local market, and all of those move the real price by a wide margin. It cannot predict future prices either — a new model, a fuel price change, or a change in tax rules can move used values in ways no formula sees. What it can do is show you the shape of depreciation and a fair estimate to start from.

It also cannot tell you whether to buy or sell. That decision depends on your money, your needs, and what else you would do with the cash — not on a table of values. Use the calculator to understand what a car will cost you over the years you plan to keep it, compare that with the alternatives, and decide with the full picture in front of you.

Questions people ask

How fast does a new car lose value?

Fastest in the first year — typically around 20 percent of its price, though the exact figure varies by model and market. After that the fall slows to roughly 10 to 15 percent a year. The calculator's typical preset uses 20 percent for year one and 12 percent after, which matches an ordinary car in ordinary use.

What affects depreciation most?

Mileage, condition and how much people want that model. High mileage and poor condition push the value down faster, while a full service history, clean bodywork and a popular model hold it up. The local market matters too — the same car can be worth more where it is in demand.

Is the rate the same every year?

No. The first year is the steepest, because a new car becomes a used car the moment it is driven away. Later years settle into a steadier fall, and the money lost each year shrinks because the same percent applies to a smaller value each time.

How do I estimate my car's current value?

Enter the purchase price and year, pick the rate that fits your car, and read the current value from the table. Then check real asking prices for the same model and age in your area, and adjust for your mileage and condition — the table is a starting point, not a quote.

Does mileage change the result?

Yes, and the calculator cannot see your odometer, so adjust the rate yourself. High mileage for the car's age means a higher later-year rate; genuinely low mileage means a lower one. A car with twice the average mileage is worth noticeably less than the table suggests.

Should I sell before a big value drop?

The big drop is the first year, which has already happened for most owners. After that the yearly loss shrinks, so keeping a car longer usually costs less per year than replacing it. Sell when the car no longer suits you or the repair bills grow — not because the table says it lost value.
