Pallet Loading Calculator

Work out how many boxes fit on one pallet, how many layers you can stack, and what the loaded pallet will weigh. Enter your box size, pallet size and weight limit to get the full loading plan with the working shown.

EUR pallet 1200 x 800 mm is the standard EUR size; GMA pallet 1219 x 1016 mm is the standard North American size. The weight limit is the one you enter — check the pallet rating before you load.

This calculator checks size and weight only — it does not check whether the load is stable, whether the boxes can bear the weight above them, or whether the pallet itself is rated for the load. Check the pallet rating and stack heavier boxes at the bottom.

How the calculator works

Enter the length, width and height of one box, its weight, the length and width of the pallet, the maximum height you will stack to, the maximum number of layers, and the weight limit for the loaded pallet. The calculator tries the boxes in both directions on the pallet, keeps the direction that fits more boxes per layer, and then works out how many layers fit within your height and layer limits.

The result is the full loading plan: boxes per layer, layers, total boxes and total weight, with a warning if the total weight passes the limit you entered. Every step of the working is shown, so you can check the arithmetic before anyone lifts a box.

One thing the calculator does not do is check stability. It counts boxes that fit by size and weight, and it is up to you to stack them so the load stands up. A tall narrow stack that fits on paper can still topple in a moving truck, so the sections below cover the loading habits that keep a well-counted pallet standing.

Which way round do the boxes go?

Boxes can sit on a pallet in two directions, and the difference is often bigger than people expect. In one direction the box length runs along the pallet length; in the other, the box is turned 90 degrees. The calculator works out both and keeps the better one, because the worse direction can waste a surprising amount of pallet.

Take the worked example below: a 40 by 30 centimetre box on a 120 by 80 centimetre pallet. In the first direction, 3 boxes fit along the length and 2 along the width, giving 6 per layer. Turned 90 degrees, 4 fit along the length and 2 along the width, giving 8 per layer. Same boxes, same pallet — a third more per layer, just from turning them.

Real loading is rarely as tidy as the arithmetic, because boxes are not always identical and labels or openings may need to face a particular way. Treat the calculator's best direction as the starting plan, and adjust it on the floor if the goods need a different arrangement. If the two directions give the same count, choose the one that makes the load easier to wrap and handle.

Worked example: 40 boxes on a EUR pallet

Take boxes 40 centimetres long, 30 wide and 25 high, weighing 8 kilograms each, loaded onto a EUR pallet 120 by 80 centimetres, with a maximum stack height of 150 centimetres, a maximum of 5 layers and a weight limit of 500 kilograms. The calculator tries both directions first. Direction A gives 3 along the length times 2 along the width, which is 6 per layer. Direction B gives 4 along the length times 2 along the width, which is 8 per layer. Direction B wins.

Next come the layers. The height limit allows 150 divided by 25, which is 6 layers, but the maximum layers you entered is 5, so the load is 5 layers high. Five layers of 8 boxes gives 40 boxes on the pallet.

The weight is 40 boxes times 8 kilograms, which is 320 kilograms — comfortably within the 500 kilogram limit. If the boxes had weighed 14 kilograms each instead, the total would have been 560 kilograms and the calculator would have warned you that the load is over the limit, even though the boxes fit by size. Size and weight are separate checks, and both must pass.

Stack height and layers

The number of layers is set by two limits at once: the height you enter and the maximum layers you enter, and the lower of the two wins. The height limit usually comes from the truck, the racking or the warehouse door, while the layer limit usually comes from the boxes themselves — the bottom layer has to carry everything above it.

Cardboard boxes have a stacking strength, and it falls sharply when boxes are damp or damaged. A box that holds five layers in a dry warehouse may fail at three in humid conditions. If you are unsure, test one pallet and check the bottom boxes after a day before you load the rest the same way.

Height also affects stability. A tall stack has a high centre of gravity and is more likely to lean or topple when the forklift brakes or the truck turns. If the load feels top-heavy, removing one layer and spreading the boxes across two pallets is cheaper than collecting a spilled load from a warehouse floor.

When the height limit and the layer limit disagree, the lower one always wins, and the calculator applies that rule for you. In the worked example the height allowed 6 layers but the layer limit was 5, so the load was 5 layers. If the height had allowed only 4, the load would have been 4 layers even though the layer limit was higher. Check both limits before you start, because discovering the lower one halfway through loading wastes the work already done.

Weight limits: pallet, truck and floor

The weight limit you enter should be the lowest of several limits, not just the pallet's rating. The pallet itself has a rated load, the forklift has a lifting capacity, the truck has a payload, and the warehouse floor or racking has a load limit. The weakest of these sets the real limit for the load.

Check the pallet rating before you load. Wooden pallets vary widely: a new hardwood pallet carries far more than an old softwood one with cracked boards. Never load a damaged pallet to its nominal rating, and never stack a heavy load on a pallet you have not inspected.

Remember that the calculator's total is the weight of the boxes only. The pallet itself adds weight, and so does any wrapping, strapping or corner protection. If your limit is tight, weigh one finished pallet on a floor scale rather than trusting the arithmetic alone.

Loading for a safe journey

A load that fits and weighs right can still fail on the road if it is stacked badly. Put the heaviest boxes at the bottom and the lightest at the top, so the centre of gravity stays low. Alternate the direction of each layer where the box size allows, so the layers lock together like brickwork instead of forming straight columns that can slide.

Keep the load inside the pallet footprint. Boxes that overhang the edge are easily crushed by neighbouring pallets, they catch on racking, and they make the load unstable. If the boxes do not fill the pallet neatly, fill the gaps with dunnage — cardboard, foam or air bags — rather than leaving empty space for the load to shift into.

Wrap or strap the finished load so it moves as one unit. Stretch wrap around the pallet and the load together, with several turns at the base, stops the bottom layer sliding. Corner boards protect the edges and give the strapping something firm to bite on. A wrapped load is a load that arrives the way it left.

Before the pallet leaves, walk around it and look at it from all four sides. Check that nothing overhangs, that the top layer is level, and that the wrap is tight with no loose ends. A minute of inspection at the dock saves hours of sorting out a load that shifted in transit, and it is the last chance to fix a problem cheaply.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is trying only one direction and missing the better one. Always compare both, because the difference can be a third of the pallet. The second mistake is counting layers by height alone and forgetting that the bottom boxes must carry the weight above them.

Another frequent error is entering the pallet's nominal rating as the weight limit without checking the pallet's condition or the forklift's capacity. A limit is only as strong as its weakest link, and a cracked board or a small forklift can set that link far below the number on the pallet's stamp.

Finally, do not treat the calculator's box count as a promise. Real boxes vary slightly in size, pallets vary in condition, and a tight fit on paper can become an impossible fit on the floor. Leave a little slack in the plan, and load one pallet as a test before committing to the full count.

Questions people ask

How many boxes fit on a pallet?

It depends on the box size and the pallet size. The calculator tries the boxes in both directions, keeps the direction that fits more per layer, and multiplies by the number of layers that fit within your height and layer limits.

What is the best way to stack boxes on a pallet?

Put the heaviest boxes at the bottom, alternate the direction of each layer where the box size allows so the layers lock together, keep the load inside the pallet footprint, and wrap or strap the finished load so it moves as one unit.

How high can I stack boxes?

It depends on the height limit of your truck or racking and on what the bottom boxes can bear. Enter your maximum stack height and your maximum number of layers, and the calculator uses whichever limit is lower.

What happens if the load is over the weight limit?

The calculator warns you that the load is over the limit you entered. Remove a layer, split the boxes across two pallets, or check whether a higher-rated pallet is available. Never send an overweight pallet and hope it holds.

What size is a standard pallet?

The two most common standards are the EUR pallet at 1200 by 800 millimetres and the GMA pallet at 1219 by 1016 millimetres. Both are offered as presets, but always measure your own pallets, because sizes vary.

Does the calculator check if the load is stable?

No. It checks size and weight only. Stability depends on how the boxes are stacked, whether the layers interlock, and whether the load is wrapped — all of which are your checks on the floor.
