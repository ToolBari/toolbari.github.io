Soil Volume Calculator

The volume is exact arithmetic on the numbers you enter, but real beds vary — soil settles after watering, and an uneven bed holds a little more or less than the box it is measured as. Buy a little extra, and check the depth in more than one place before you order.

How to use this calculator

Enter the length, width and depth of the bed you want to fill, pick the units you measured in (centimetres, metres, inches or feet), and enter the size of the bags you buy in litres — or leave the bag box blank if you buy soil in bulk. The calculator shows the volume in litres, cubic metres and cubic feet, and the number of bags you need, rounded up to whole bags. The working is shown underneath, so you can check the arithmetic.

Measure the inside of the bed, not the outside: the soil goes inside the walls, and the walls themselves take up space. Measure the depth to the level you want the soil to reach, not to the top of the boards — most beds are filled a few centimetres below the rim, so that water stays in when you water. If the bed is not level, measure the depth in two or three places and use the average.

If your bed is not a rectangle — an L-shape, a circle, a bed with a curved edge — split it into rectangles, work out each part separately, and add the volumes together. A circle is the area of the circle (3.14 times the radius squared) times the depth. The calculator handles rectangles; your tape measure and a little splitting handle the rest.

The formula

Soil volume is length times width times depth. A bed 200 centimetres long, 100 centimetres wide and 30 centimetres deep holds 200 times 100 times 30 cubic centimetres, which is 600,000 cubic centimetres. A litre is 1000 cubic centimetres, so that is 600 litres. A cubic metre is a million cubic centimetres, so it is 0.6 cubic metres. A cubic foot is 28,317 cubic centimetres, so it is about 21.2 cubic feet. The calculator shows all three, because soil is sold in litres in some places, in cubic metres in others, and in cubic feet in others still.

Unit conversions are built into the calculator: centimetres divide by 100 to give metres, inches multiply by 0.0254, and feet multiply by 0.3048. If you measured in mixed units — feet for the length and inches for the depth, for example — convert them to one unit first, or run the calculator twice: once for the length and width in one unit, and once to check. The cleanest habit is to measure everything in the same unit before you start.

Bag counts are arithmetic on the bag size you enter: the volume in litres divided by the litres per bag, rounded up to the next whole bag. A 600 litre bed with 40 litre bags needs 15 bags exactly; a 620 litre bed needs 15.5 bags, so 16. The calculator always rounds up, because half a bag is not sold and a bed short of soil is worse than a bag left over.

Litres, cubic feet and bags

Litres are the everyday unit for bagged soil: a standard bag is often 25, 40 or 50 litres, and the bag size is printed on the front. Cubic metres are the unit for bulk soil, delivered by the lorry load: one cubic metre is 1000 litres, and a bulk delivery is usually measured in whole or half cubic metres. Cubic feet are the unit in countries that sell soil by the foot: one cubic foot is about 28.3 litres, and a cubic yard (27 cubic feet) is about 765 litres.

The three units describe the same volume, so the choice is about how you buy, not about the soil. If you buy bags, read the litres and the bag count. If you buy in bulk, read the cubic metres and order a little more than the number — bulk soil settles in transport, and a delivery measured loosely can come up short. If you buy by the cubic foot, read that column and check whether your supplier sells by the foot or by the yard.

Whatever unit you use, the honest habit is to buy a little extra — about 10 percent more than the calculator says. Soil settles after the first few waterings, losing a few centimetres of depth, and a bed that looked full on day one can look short by the end of the first month. The extra bag or the extra tenth of a cubic metre is the top-up you will want anyway.

Worked example

Take a raised bed 200 centimetres long, 100 centimetres wide, filled to a depth of 30 centimetres. The volume is 200 times 100 times 30, which is 600,000 cubic centimetres. Divide by 1000 to get litres: 600 litres. Divide by a million to get cubic metres: 0.6 cubic metres. Divide by 28,317 to get cubic feet: about 21.2 cubic feet. With 40 litre bags, the count is 600 divided by 40, which is 15 bags exactly.

Now add the settling allowance: 10 percent of 600 litres is 60 litres, so buy 660 litres — 16.5 bags, rounded up to 17. The extra two bags are the top-up after the first month, when the soil has settled and the level has dropped. Buying them with the first order saves a second trip.

The same bed measured in feet — about 6.6 feet long, 3.3 feet wide, 1 foot deep — gives the same answer by the other route: 6.6 times 3.3 times 1 is about 21.8 cubic feet, close to the 21.2 from the centimetre route (the small difference is rounding in the conversion). The calculator handles the units for you, so the route does not matter — only the measurements do.

A note on compost mix

A raised bed is rarely filled with plain soil alone. A common starting mix for vegetables is roughly 60 percent topsoil, 30 percent compost and 10 percent drainage material such as coarse sand or perlite — a mix that holds water, feeds the plants and drains freely. That is a starting point, not a rule: heavy clay soil wants more compost and more drainage material, and sandy soil wants more compost and less drainage material.

To work out the parts, multiply the total volume by each share. For the 600 litre bed above, 60 percent topsoil is 360 litres, 30 percent compost is 180 litres, and 10 percent drainage material is 60 litres. Buy the parts separately and mix them in the bed, a layer at a time, rather than mixing a mountain on the ground and moving it twice.

Compost here means finished compost — dark, crumbly, smelling of earth — not fresh manure or unrotted garden waste, which can burn roots and carry weed seeds. If you are unsure whether your compost is finished, it probably is not: finished compost looks and smells like soil, and you cannot recognise what it was made from.

How deep should a bed be?

Depth depends on what you grow. Most vegetables are happy with 15 to 30 centimetres of good soil: lettuce, herbs, strawberries and salad leaves manage on 15, while tomatoes, beans and courgettes want 25 to 30. Root crops want more — carrots and parsnips need 30 centimetres or deeper, in soil without stones, so the roots grow straight. A bed deeper than 30 centimetres is a luxury rather than a need for most vegetables, and it costs more soil for little gain.

If your bed sits on open ground, the roots can grow down past the bed into the soil below, so a shallower bed works — the bed is really a frame for good soil on top of whatever is underneath. If your bed sits on concrete or paving, the bed is all the soil there is, so depth matters more: 30 centimetres is the minimum for most vegetables, and deeper is better for anything with a long root.

Depth also affects watering. A shallow bed dries out faster than a deep one, because there is less soil to hold water. In a hot, dry place, a deeper bed is easier to keep watered, and a mulch on top — straw, bark or compost — slows the drying further. The depth you choose is a balance between the cost of the soil and the ease of the watering.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is measuring the outside of the bed. The boards take up space, and a bed that measures 200 by 100 on the outside may be 190 by 90 on the inside — a difference of about 10 percent in volume. Measure inside, where the soil goes.

The second mistake is forgetting that soil settles. Freshly filled soil looks full, then drops by a few centimetres after the first waterings as the air pockets collapse. Buying exactly the calculated volume leaves you short within a month. The 10 percent allowance is not waste — it is the top-up you will want.

The third mistake is filling to the very top of the boards. Soil level with the rim spills when you water and leaves nowhere for mulch. Fill to a few centimetres below the rim, and measure the depth to that level, not to the top of the boards.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot measure your bed for you, and it cannot know whether your bed is level, whether the boards bow outward, or whether the soil underneath is soft enough for roots to pass through. It computes the volume of a rectangular box from the numbers you enter — a real bed is close to a box, but never exactly one, and the answer is exact arithmetic on approximate measurements.

It also cannot tell you what soil to buy. Topsoil, compost and drainage material differ by supplier, and a cheap topsoil can be mostly clay while a good one is loam. The calculator tells you how much you need; the choice of what to fill it with is yours, and the compost-mix note above is a starting point, not a prescription.

Questions people ask

How much soil do I need for a raised bed?

Multiply length by width by depth, all in the same unit, to get the volume. A bed 200 by 100 by 30 centimetres needs 600 litres. Enter your bed's measurements in the calculator to get the volume in litres, cubic metres and cubic feet, plus the number of bags.

How do I convert cubic metres to litres?

One cubic metre is 1000 litres, so multiply by 1000: 0.6 cubic metres is 600 litres. One cubic foot is about 28.3 litres. The calculator shows all three units, so you do not need to convert by hand.

What if my bed is not a rectangle?

Split it into rectangles, work out each part separately, and add the volumes together. A circular bed is 3.14 times the radius squared times the depth. An L-shaped bed is two rectangles. Measure the inside of each part, not the outside.

How many bags of soil do I need?

Divide the volume in litres by the litres per bag, and round up to whole bags. A 600 litre bed with 40 litre bags needs 15 bags. Buy about 10 percent extra — soil settles after watering, and the extra is the top-up you will want within a month.

Should I mix compost into the soil?

Yes, for most beds. A common starting mix is roughly 60 percent topsoil, 30 percent finished compost and 10 percent drainage material such as coarse sand or perlite. Use finished compost — dark, crumbly and smelling of earth — not fresh manure or unrotted waste.

How deep should a raised bed be?
