What this estimator does and who it is for

Second-hand equipment is worth what someone will pay for it — and that is a harder question than it sounds. A machine that cost 8,000 new is not worth 8,000 after four years, but it is not worth nothing either. The answer sits somewhere in between, and it depends on the age, the condition, how hard the machine has worked, and what similar machines are selling for. This estimator gives you a reasoned starting point: a value and a range, worked out by a method you can see and check.

It is built for anyone buying or selling used equipment — a workshop selling a machine it no longer needs, a farmer pricing a tractor, a small business valuing its assets, or a buyer checking whether an asking price is fair. Enter the new price, the age, the useful life, the condition, and the usage, and the estimator works out a value with the method shown step by step.

The result is an estimate, and the page says so wherever the result appears. A real sale price depends on the buyer, the market, and the day — on demand, on location, on how urgently the seller needs to sell. The estimator gives you a number to start from and a range to negotiate within. It is not a market value, not an official valuation, and not a promise of what you will get.

The inputs, explained one by one

The new price is what the equipment cost when it was new — or what the same model costs new today, if you know it. Use the price of the same model and specification, not a bigger or smaller one, and enter it as a plain number in your own currency. If the equipment is old enough that the new price is hard to find, the replacement price of the nearest current model is a reasonable stand-in, and the FAQ below explains the difference.

Age is how old the equipment is, in years, and useful life is how long equipment of this kind typically lasts before it is worn out or obsolete. A machine with a ten-year useful life that is four years old has 60% of its life left, and that share is the starting point of the estimate. Set the useful life honestly: a machine that lasts twenty years should not be given a five-year life, and one that is obsolete in five should not be given twenty.

Condition is your judgement of the equipment's state: excellent means like new and fully working, good means working well with normal wear, fair means working with visible wear or minor faults, and poor means it needs repair or is only partly working. Usage is how hard it has worked: light use means occasional and gentle, standard means normal working use, and heavy means long hours or hard conditions. Both are judgements, and honest judgements give honest estimates.

The formula and how the result is worked out

The method is straight-line depreciation with two adjustments. First, the straight-line base: the new price multiplied by the share of useful life remaining. A machine that cost 8,000 new, with a ten-year life, at four years old, has 60% of its life left, so the base is 8,000 times 0.6, which is 4,800. Straight-line means the value falls by the same amount each year — simple, transparent, and easy to check.

Then the condition factor adjusts the base: excellent keeps it whole at 1.0, good takes 0.9, fair takes 0.75, and poor takes 0.55. The usage factor adjusts it again: light use gives a small lift at 1.05, standard use leaves it at 1.0, and heavy use takes 0.9. The factors are shown with the result, so you can see exactly how each judgement moved the number.

Two more rules shape the result. A residual floor of 5% of the new price applies: the estimate never falls below 5%, because even worn equipment usually has some value as parts or scrap. And the result is shown as a range — the estimate plus or minus 15% — because a single number would pretend to a precision the method does not have. The range is the honest answer; the single figure is its middle.

A worked example with real numbers

Take a machine that cost 8,000 new, is 4 years old, has a useful life of 10 years, is in good condition, and has had standard use. First the straight-line base: 60% of its life remains, so the base is 8,000 times 0.6, which is 4,800. The condition factor for good is 0.9, so 4,800 times 0.9 is 4,320. The usage factor for standard use is 1.0, so the estimate stays at 4,320.

The range is 4,320 plus or minus 15%: the low end is 3,672 and the high end is 4,968. So the estimate is 4,320, with a range of 3,672 to 4,968. That range is the number to use: a buyer offering near the low end is getting a good deal, a seller asking near the high end needs the condition to justify it, and the middle is the fair starting point for a negotiation.

Change one input and the result moves. Put the same machine in poor condition and the condition factor becomes 0.55: the estimate falls to 2,640, with a range of 2,244 to 3,036. Or keep it in good condition but make it 9 years old: only 10% of its life remains, the base falls to 800, and the estimate becomes 720 — close to the 5% floor of 400, which is where very old equipment ends up. Age and condition are where the value lives.

Common mistakes that give a wrong answer

The first mistake is using the wrong new price — the price of a bigger model, a different specification, or a price from years ago that no longer applies. The estimate is built on the new price, so a wrong starting point makes everything wrong. Use the price of the same model, or the nearest current replacement, and say which you used.

The second mistake is optimism about condition. Most owners rate their own equipment a grade higher than a buyer would: fair becomes good, good becomes excellent. Be honest, and if you are selling, ask yourself what a stranger would say after a close look. A condition grade that flatters the machine produces an estimate that flatters the seller — and a buyer who discovers the truth will walk away.

The third mistake is ignoring the market. The estimator works from age and condition; it knows nothing about demand, about whether this model is sought after or hard to sell, or about what similar machines are actually selling for. Always check real listings before you buy or sell. If the listings cluster far from the estimate, the listings are right and the estimate needs its inputs rechecked — usually the useful life or the condition.

How to use the result in a real decision

If you are selling, use the range to set an asking price and a lowest acceptable price. The high end of the range is a fair asking price for equipment in the stated condition; the low end is the floor below which you should not go without a reason. List the equipment with honest photographs and a clear description of its condition, and expect negotiation — the range exists because the final price is a negotiation, not a calculation.

If you are buying, use the estimate to judge an asking price. An asking price inside the range is reasonable; one far above it needs justification — a recent overhaul, new parts, a warranty — and one far below it needs a careful inspection, because a price that looks too good usually has a reason. Inspect before you pay, run the machine if you can, and check what similar machines are listed for.

For anything important — a high-value machine, a business asset, an insurance matter — get a qualified valuer. An estimator gives you a reasoned number to start from; a valuer gives you a professional opinion that stands up. The estimator's job is to make you an informed buyer or seller, not to replace the expert when the stakes are high.

Questions people ask

How is used equipment value worked out?

By straight-line depreciation: the new price times the share of useful life remaining, adjusted for condition and usage, with a 5% residual floor. The result is shown as a range, plus or minus 15%.

What affects second-hand value most?

Age and condition. Age sets the remaining-life share, and condition moves the value by up to nearly half. Usage matters too, but less than the other two.

Why do two identical machines sell for different prices?

Because a sale price depends on the buyer, the market and the day — demand, location, urgency, and how well the machine is presented. The estimator gives a reasoned range, not a fixed price.

Should I use the new price or the replacement price?

Use the new price of the same model if you know it. If the model is no longer sold, the replacement price of the nearest current model is a reasonable stand-in.

How does condition change the value?

Through the condition factor: excellent keeps the full base value, good takes 90%, fair takes 75%, and poor takes 55%. Be honest about condition — a flattering grade gives a flattering estimate.

Is this an official valuation?

No. This is an estimate from your numbers, not a market value or an official valuation. For anything important, get a qualified valuer.
