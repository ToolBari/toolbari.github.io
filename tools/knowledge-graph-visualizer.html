How to use the visualizer

Start by adding ideas, one at a time, in the box above. An idea can be anything: a topic, a question, a project, a person. Then link ideas that connect, by picking two from the dropdowns and tapping add link. The graph draws itself as you go — each idea becomes a dot, each link becomes a line.

Drag any dot to move it, and the lines follow. Tap a dot to highlight it and everything connected to it, so you can see one idea's neighbourhood at a glance; tap it again to clear the highlight. Zoom in and out with the buttons when the graph grows beyond the screen.

Everything stays in this browser only, on this device. Your ideas and links are saved automatically as you add them, so closing the page does not lose your graph — but clearing your browser data will, so do not treat this as your only copy of important thinking.

If your browser cannot draw the graph — if it does not support the drawing canvas this tool needs — the page says so plainly instead of showing you a fake picture. Your ideas are still listed below, and you can still add and remove them, but the drawing itself needs a browser that supports canvas.

What a knowledge graph shows you

A list shows you what you have; a graph shows you how it connects. The same twenty ideas in a list look equal, but in a graph, some sit at the centre with lines running everywhere, and some sit alone at the edge with no lines at all. That difference is information a list cannot give you.

The central ideas are the ones your thinking returns to. If "Garden" connects to ten other ideas and "Composting" connects to one, the graph is telling you where your attention actually goes — which may not be where you thought it went. The graph is a mirror, not a judge, but mirrors are useful.

The lonely ideas matter too. An idea with no links is either a new thought that has not found its place yet, or an old one that never connected to anything. Both are worth a look: the first may deserve linking in, and the second may deserve a question about whether it belongs in your thinking at all.

The shape of the whole graph tells its own story. A graph with one dense centre and a few outlying dots is a mind with a clear focus. A graph in two separate halves is a mind holding two subjects that never meet — which may be fine, or may be an invitation to find the bridge between them.

Building your first graph

Start small. Five to ten ideas is enough for a first graph — enough to see the shape, few enough to keep readable. Pick a subject you know well, list the ideas in it, and link the ones that connect. Do not worry about getting it right first time; a graph is easy to change, and the first version is a draft.

Link ideas that genuinely relate, not ideas that merely sit near each other. A link should mean something: "this idea depends on that one", "this idea is an example of that one", or "these two ideas are in tension". If you cannot say why two ideas are linked, they probably should not be.

Add ideas as they come to you, and link them when the connection is clear. A graph built slowly, over days of thinking, is worth more than one built in a rush, because the slow graph reflects real understanding and the rushed one reflects the order you happened to think of things.

Use short names. A dot labelled "Rose pruning" is readable at a glance; a dot labelled "The complete guide to pruning roses in spring and autumn" is a paragraph pretending to be a dot. Keep names to a few words, and put the detail in your notes, not in the graph.

Reading the clusters

A cluster is a group of ideas that link mostly to each other and rarely to anything outside the group. Clusters usually mean a real subject: your garden ideas cluster together, your work ideas cluster together, and the two clusters may share only one or two links. Seeing the clusters is seeing the shape of your knowledge.

The ideas that sit between clusters are often the most interesting. An idea that links your garden cluster to your cooking cluster — "Herbs", say — is a bridge, and bridges are where new thinking happens. If two clusters have no bridge at all, ask whether one exists and you have not seen it, or whether the subjects really are separate.

A cluster that keeps growing is a subject you are learning deeply. A cluster that never grows may be a subject you have finished with, or one you have been avoiding. The graph does not judge, but it does show you — and what it shows is often a surprise.

Watch for the idea that connects to everything. In many graphs, one idea sits in the middle with lines running in all directions. That idea is the hub of your thinking on that subject, and it is often the idea worth writing about, teaching, or building on — because everything else already connects to it.

When a graph gets too big

A graph with fifty dots on one screen is not a picture, it is a tangle. The lines cross, the labels overlap, and the shape disappears under the mess. This page warns you past about thirty ideas, because that is roughly where a single graph stops being readable for most people.

The answer is not to stop adding ideas — it is to split the graph. One graph per topic is the rule that works: a garden graph, a work graph, a learning graph, each small enough to read. The ideas that bridge topics can live in both, or in a small graph of their own that maps how your subjects connect.

If a graph is already too big, prune it. Remove the ideas that no longer matter, merge the ones that say the same thing, and move a cluster out into its own graph. A graph is a tool for thinking, and a tool that is too big to use is not a tool at all.

Zoom helps, but only up to a point. Zooming in lets you read one corner of a big graph, but it hides the shape of the whole — and the shape is the point. Use zoom to inspect, not to cope with a graph that has outgrown its frame.

What this visualizer cannot do

This visualizer draws only what you type in. It does not fetch ideas from the internet, it does not suggest links, and it cannot tell you which ideas are important — the importance is in the links you make, and the links are yours to make. A tool that suggested links would be guessing at your thinking, and a guess is not knowledge.

It cannot lay out a large graph beautifully. The dots start in a circle, and you drag them where they belong — the layout is yours to arrange, because only you know which ideas belong near each other. An automatic layout would save effort and cost understanding.

It cannot save your graph anywhere but this browser. There is no account, no cloud, and no export to a file — your graph lives on this device, in this browser, until you clear your browser data. If your graph matters to you, take a screenshot, or write its ideas down somewhere safe.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is linking everything to everything. If every idea links to every other idea, the graph is a solid ball of lines that says nothing. Link only the connections that mean something, and let the empty space between unlinked ideas speak for itself.

The second is fifty nodes on one screen. A graph that big is unreadable, and unreadable is useless. Split it by topic, prune what no longer matters, and keep each graph small enough to read at a glance.

The third is long names. A dot is a label, not a paragraph. Keep names to a few words, and put the detail in your notes — or in a personal wiki, where long writing belongs.

The fourth is building the graph and never looking at it. A graph is not a filing system; it is a picture to look at, to think about, and to change as your thinking changes. Open it, look at the shape, ask what surprises you, and let the surprises change what you do next.

The fifth is treating the graph as the knowledge itself. The graph is a map of your thinking, not the thinking. The ideas live in your head and in your notes; the graph only shows how they connect. A beautiful graph of shallow ideas is still shallow — the depth has to come from the thinking, not the drawing.

Questions people ask

What is a knowledge graph?

A knowledge graph is a picture of ideas and their connections: each idea is a dot, each link is a line, and the shape shows which ideas connect and which sit alone. It turns a list into a map of how your thinking fits together.

Is my data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your ideas and links stay in this browser only, on your own device, and this page does not connect to any server. If you clear your browser data, your graph will be removed.

How many ideas can I add?

There is no fixed limit, but a graph past about thirty ideas gets hard to read — this page warns you when you pass thirty. Split big graphs by topic, one graph per subject, to keep each one readable.

Can I move the nodes around?

Yes. Drag any dot to move it, and its links follow. Tap a dot to highlight it and everything connected to it, and use the zoom buttons when the graph grows beyond the screen.

What do the clusters mean?

A cluster is a group of ideas that link mostly to each other — usually a real subject, like your garden ideas or your work ideas. Ideas sitting between clusters are bridges, and they are often where new thinking happens.

Can I save my graph?

Your graph is saved automatically in this browser as you add ideas and links, so closing the page does not lose it. There is no file export — if your graph matters to you, take a screenshot or write its ideas down somewhere safe.
