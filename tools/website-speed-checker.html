How to use the self-test

Start at the top of this page. Tap the button that says Test my connection and this page. The page will measure two things. First, it reads how long this page took to load in your browser, using the timing information your browser already keeps. Second, it downloads this page once more and times it, then works out roughly how fast your connection is from the size of the page and the time it took. The result appears in plain words: how big this page is, how long it took to load, and about how fast your connection is right now.

Run the test more than once. A single test can be fooled by a busy moment on your network, a slow first load, or a page that was already stored in your browser. Test once, wait a minute, and test again. If the numbers jump around a lot, your connection is unsteady, and that matters more than any single result. Test on your phone as well as on your computer, because most visitors will arrive on a phone, and a phone on a mobile connection is a very different test from a desktop on fast home Wi-Fi.

Then work through the checklist below the self-test. Read each question and tick the box only if the answer is honestly yes for your site. If you are not sure, leave it unticked. A guess that flatters your site helps nobody, and an honest low score is more useful than a hopeful high one. When you have answered all twenty questions, tap Score my checklist. You will see a score out of one hundred, a verdict in plain words, and a short note about what to do next. You can copy the summary with the copy button and keep it next to your notes.

What actually makes a site slow

Most slow sites are slow for the same few reasons, and images come first. A photo straight from a camera can be several megabytes, and a page with a few of those asks the visitor to download far more than they need before anything useful appears. The browser then shrinks each photo to fit the screen, which means the visitor paid for pixels they never saw. Resizing an image to the size it is shown at, and compressing it before it goes on the page, usually cuts more loading time than any other single change.

Scripts come second. Every script on a page has to be downloaded, read, and run before the page can finish showing, and many sites carry scripts they no longer need: old chat widgets, tracking code from tools nobody checks, social buttons that load a whole library for one small icon. Each one adds a little time, and together they add a lot. A page that loads three scripts will almost always beat a page that loads thirty, even if the two pages look the same.

After images and scripts, the usual suspects are fonts, caching, and the server itself. A page that loads four font families in six weights is asking the browser to download a small library before it can draw a single word. A page with no caching makes every returning visitor download everything again, even the parts that never change. And a slow server delays everything, because the browser cannot start until the server answers. The checklist on this page covers all of these, one question at a time, so you can see which ones your site gets right and which ones it does not.

The 20-point checklist, explained

The checklist asks twenty plain questions about your site. They fall into four groups. The first group is about images: are they the right size, are they compressed, and do the ones below the fold wait until the visitor scrolls to them? The second group is about scripts and code: are there too many scripts, do any of them block the page from showing, is the code minified, and are third-party scripts kept under control? The third group is about how the site is served: caching, compression, redirects, the server, and whether a content delivery network is in use. The fourth group is about the extras that quietly slow a page down: heavy fonts, video that plays by itself, chat widgets, and adverts.

Answer each question for the site you are checking, not for this page. The checklist is a mirror, not a test with right answers hidden somewhere. If your site has no video at all, the video question is an easy yes, because there is nothing slowing the page down. If your site has no database, the database question is also a yes for the same reason. The point is to find the real problems on your site, not to collect points.

The score is five points per yes, out of one hundred. A score of ninety or above means your site follows most of the habits that keep a page fast. Seventy to eighty-nine means the site is in decent shape with a few things to fix. Fifty to sixty-nine means several habits are missing, and below fifty means the basics need attention soon. Whatever your score, the useful part is the list of unticked boxes. Those are your jobs, in the order the checklist gives them.

Fixing the top 5 problems first

If your score is low, do not try to fix everything at once. Start with the five changes that help most sites the most. First, resize and compress your images. Make each image the size it is shown at, and compress it before upload. This one change often halves the size of a page.

Second, remove the scripts you do not need. Open your site, list what loads, and take out anything that serves no visitor: old widgets, duplicate tracking, social buttons nobody taps. Third, switch on caching and compression, so returning visitors download less and every visitor downloads smaller files. These are usually settings on your host or your site platform, not code you have to write.

Fourth, cut the fonts down. One or two font families in one or two weights is enough for almost every site. Fifth, stop video from playing by itself. A video that waits for a tap costs the visitor nothing until they choose to watch it. Fix these five, then run the checklist again. The score will have moved, and the next five jobs will be clear.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is testing once on fast Wi-Fi and calling it done. A site that loads in a second on a fast home connection can take ten seconds on a phone in a weak signal area, and the visitor on the weak signal is the one you are most likely to lose. Test on a phone, test on a mobile connection, and test more than once.

The second mistake is fixing the easy items and skipping the hard ones. Changing a font is quick, but it will not save a page whose images are ten times too big. Start with the biggest files and the most scripts, even when the work is dull. The third mistake is adding speed tools on top of a slow site instead of removing what is slow. A caching plugin cannot fix a page full of giant images. Remove the weight first, then add the polish.

The fourth mistake is trusting one number. A single speed score from one tool on one day is a snapshot, not the truth. Run the test a few times, on more than one tool, and look at what the tools agree on. If every tool says the images are too big, the images are too big.

What it cannot do

This page tests YOUR connection and THIS page's load time, and scores a checklist you fill in. It cannot measure another website's speed — no static page can.

That is a real limit, not a missing feature. A page that runs in your browser can only see your connection and the page it sits on. It cannot reach out and time another website, and any page that claims to do that from a typed address is guessing or pretending. The honest way to measure a real website is to use a tool that runs the test from its own servers.

To measure a real website properly, use the official PageSpeed Insights tool at pagespeed.web.dev, the official GTmetrix tool at gtmetrix.com, or the official WebPageTest tool at webpagetest.org. Each one loads the site you name from its own servers and shows what is slow. Use one of those for the real number, and use this page for a quick self-test and an honest checklist.

Questions people ask

Can this page test any website I type in?

No. This page tests your own connection and this page's load time, and it scores a checklist you fill in. It cannot measure another website's speed, and it never shows a fake score for a web address you type in. To measure a real site, use PageSpeed Insights, GTmetrix or WebPageTest, which run the test from their own servers.

What is a good page load time?

Most visitors expect a page to show in a couple of seconds. If a page takes much longer on an ordinary connection, visitors leave before they read anything. Use the checklist on this page to find what is taking the time, and fix images and scripts first.

Why does my site load slowly on my phone but fast on my computer?

A phone has a smaller processor, a slower connection and less memory than a desktop computer, so the same page takes longer to load and show. A page that feels fine on fast home Wi-Fi can feel slow on a mobile connection. Always test on a real phone, not only on a desktop.

Do images really slow a site down that much?

Yes. On most pages, images are the largest part of what the browser has to download. A single photo straight from a camera can be larger than the rest of the page put together. Resizing images to the size they are shown at, and compressing them before upload, is usually the biggest single win.

What does the 20-point checklist score mean?

Each question you answer yes to is worth 5 points, so the score runs from 0 to 100. A score of 90 or above is excellent, 70 to 89 is good, 50 to 69 needs work, and below 50 needs urgent work. The score reflects what you report about your own site, so answer honestly.

Which free tool measures a real website's speed?

PageSpeed Insights, GTmetrix and WebPageTest all measure a real website from their own servers and show what is slow. They are the right tools when you need a real number for a site you own. This page is for a quick self-test and a checklist, not a server-side measurement.
