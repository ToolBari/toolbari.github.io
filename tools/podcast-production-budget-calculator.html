What this calculator does and who it is for

Starting a podcast sounds cheap — a microphone and a free app — until the costs arrive one by one: the mic, the hosting that renews every month, the editing that takes hours, the music licence. This calculator adds them all up before you start, so you know the setup cost, the monthly cost and the cost per episode while the numbers are still on paper.

It is built for anyone planning a podcast: a first-time host with an idea, a small team turning a show into a regular habit, or a business weighing whether a podcast is worth the money. You do not need any podcast experience to use it — just your plan and a few prices you have seen.

What it will not do is tell you what anything costs. Every figure comes from you: the gear price you found, the hosting plan you are considering, the editing rate you were quoted or the zero for doing it yourself. The calculator does the arithmetic; the prices are yours to check.

How to use the calculator on your phone or computer

Start with the episodes per month — how often you actually plan to publish, not how often you hope to. Then enter the gear cost as a one-off figure: the mic, headphones and anything else you need to buy once. Hosting is a monthly figure, editing is a per-episode figure (enter 0 if you edit yourself), and music and other costs are monthly. Enter all amounts in your own currency.

Tap Work out my budget and the calculator shows three numbers: the setup cost, the monthly cost and the cost per episode. The cost per episode spreads the gear cost over the first 12 months, because gear is bought once but used for a year of episodes — counting it all against the first episode would make the first episode look absurdly expensive.

The copy button copies the whole budget as plain text, so you can keep it with your plan or share it with a co-host. Nothing you enter leaves your browser — there is no account, no upload and no record kept anywhere else. Run the numbers for different plans and compare them before you spend anything.

The costs explained: mic, hosting, editing, music

Gear is the one-off cost and the one people overspend on first. A decent USB microphone, a pair of closed-back headphones and a pop filter are enough to start, and they cost far less than a studio setup. The mic matters most: listeners forgive a plain cover image, but they leave a show they cannot hear clearly. Buy the best mic you can afford, and skip the rest until the show earns it.

Hosting is the monthly cost that never stops. A podcast host stores your audio files and serves them to listeners, and most hosts charge a monthly fee that rises with the number of episodes or the amount of storage. Some hosts offer a free tier with limits — check what the limits are, because a free tier that runs out mid-season is a problem, not a saving.

Editing is the cost people forget, and it is usually the biggest. Editing one episode takes two to four times the length of the episode itself, and either you pay for that time with your own hours or you pay an editor per episode. If you edit yourself, enter 0 — but count the hours honestly, because an hour of editing is an hour you cannot spend recording or promoting.

Music and other costs are smaller but real: a licence for intro music, a cover image, a domain name, a transcription service. None of them is expensive alone, and together they add up to a noticeable monthly figure. The calculator has a single box for them so they are not forgotten — a budget that ignores small costs is a budget that surprises you.

Starting cheap versus starting right

You can start a podcast for almost nothing: a phone microphone, a free host, editing you do yourself. That is a real option, and for a first season it is often the right one — the show matters more than the gear, and a good idea recorded plainly beats a weak idea recorded beautifully.

But cheap has limits. A phone microphone in a noisy room produces audio that drives listeners away, and a free host with tight limits can force a move mid-season. Starting right does not mean starting expensively — it means spending on the two things that matter most, a decent mic and a quiet room, and skipping everything else until the show has an audience.

The sensible path is to start cheap on everything except sound. Buy a good mic, record somewhere quiet, use a free or cheap host, and edit yourself for the first season. If the show finds listeners, the budget can grow with it. If it does not, you have lost little — and you have learned whether podcasting is for you.

The per-episode number that matters

The cost per episode is the number that decides whether a podcast is sustainable, because it is the number you pay every time you publish. A show that costs a little per episode can run for years; a show that costs a lot per episode stops when the money or the energy runs out. Work it out before you start, not after ten episodes.

The per-episode figure in this calculator includes the gear spread over the first year, so it falls after the first year when the gear is paid for. That is realistic: gear is a one-off, while hosting and editing repeat. If the first-year figure looks high, check the second-year figure — the monthly cost divided by the episodes — to see what the show costs once it is running.

Compare the per-episode cost with what the show gives back. A hobby show gives back enjoyment, and the cost is what you are willing to pay for it. A business show gives back listeners, and the cost is worth judging against what those listeners are worth. Either way, the number is only useful if it is honest — enter real figures, not hopeful ones.

A worked example with real numbers

Imagine a weekly show: four episodes a month. Gear costs 15,000 once — a mic, headphones and a stand. Hosting costs 1,500 a month. Editing is done by the host, so editing costs 0. Music and other costs are 500 a month. The setup cost is 15,000. The monthly cost is 1,500 plus 0 plus 500, which is 2,000.

The cost per episode spreads the gear over 12 months: 15,000 divided by 12 is 1,250 a month for gear, plus the monthly 2,000, which is 3,250 a month in the first year. Divided by four episodes, the cost per episode is about 813 in the first year. After the first year, with the gear paid for, it falls to 2,000 divided by four — 500 per episode.

Now change one input: pay an editor 3,000 per episode instead of editing yourself. The monthly cost becomes 1,500 plus 12,000 plus 500, which is 14,000, and the first-year cost per episode rises to about 3,813. That single choice — editing yourself or paying for it — changes the budget more than any other. It is the decision to think hardest about.

Common mistakes that blow a podcast budget

The first mistake is buying a studio mic before a first episode. Expensive gear does not make a show good, and a mic bought before you know whether you enjoy podcasting is money spent on a hobby you may not keep. Start with a good-enough mic and upgrade when the show earns it.

The second mistake is forgetting that hosting renews monthly. A hosting plan that looks cheap for the first month is not cheap if it renews at a higher price, and a free tier that runs out mid-season forces a move at the worst moment. Read the renewal price and the limits before you sign up.

The third mistake is forgetting your own time. Editing, planning and promoting take hours every week, and those hours are a real cost even when no money changes hands. If you edit yourself, count the hours honestly — a show that takes ten hours a week is a part-time job, and it should be budgeted like one.

Questions people ask

How much does it cost to start a podcast?

It can cost almost nothing with a phone and a free host, or a noticeable amount with good gear and paid editing. Enter your own figures in this calculator: the setup cost, monthly cost and cost per episode all depend on the choices you make.

Do I need expensive gear?

No. A decent microphone and a quiet room matter far more than expensive gear. Start with a good-enough mic, record somewhere quiet, and upgrade when the show has an audience and earns the investment.

What does podcast hosting cost?

It varies by host and by plan, and most hosts charge monthly. Some offer a free tier with limits on episodes or storage. Check the renewal price and the limits before signing up — a free tier that runs out mid-season is a problem.

How much is editing per episode?

It depends on the editor and the episode length, and editing yourself costs 0 in money but hours of your time. Enter 0 if you edit yourself, or the rate you were quoted. Editing is usually the biggest cost in a podcast budget.

Can I podcast for free?

Almost — a phone microphone, a free host and editing you do yourself can cost nothing in money. The cost is your time: planning, recording, editing and promoting take hours every week, and that time is real.

What is the cost per episode?

The total cost divided by the number of episodes: monthly costs plus gear spread over the first year, divided by the episodes per month. It is the number that decides whether a show is sustainable, so work it out before you start.
