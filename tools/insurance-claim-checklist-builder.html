What this tool does and who it is for

An insurance claim checklist builder turns a stressful situation into a list of steps. You pick the type of claim, and the page shows the steps to follow and the documents to collect, each with a tick box. Tick each step as you complete it, and the page shows what is still open. The copy button turns the whole checklist into plain text you can print or save.

This page is made for anyone who has had an accident, a loss, or damage, and needs to claim on insurance. It is also useful before anything happens: building the checklist in advance teaches you what your insurer will ask for, so you can keep the right documents ready instead of hunting for them under pressure.

The builder covers five claim types: vehicle, home, health, travel, and other. Each type has its own steps and documents, because a car accident needs different records than a burst pipe or a hospital stay. The lists are general guidance, built from what insurers usually ask for, and they never promise a particular outcome.

Everything stays in your browser. There is no account, no server, and no one else who can see your checklist. The ticks are saved on your device, so the list is there when you come back. The page never asks for your policy number, your personal details, or anything about the claim itself.

How to use it, step by step

Start by picking the claim type that matches your situation: vehicle, home, health, travel, or other. The page shows two lists: the steps to follow, in order, and the documents to collect. Each item has a tick box.

Work through the steps in order. The first steps are usually about safety and reporting: make sure everyone is safe, report the incident to the insurer promptly, and take photos before anything is moved or repaired. Tick each step as you complete it. The progress count shows how many steps and documents are done.

Collect the documents as you go. The documents list names what an insurer usually asks for: the policy details, photos of the damage, receipts, reports, and any reference numbers. Tick each document when you have it ready. If a document does not exist, note that in your own records and tell the insurer, rather than leaving the item unticked and unexplained.

  • Report the incident to your insurer promptly, even before you have all the documents
  • Take photos before anything is moved, cleaned or repaired
  • Keep copies of everything you send, and note the date and the name of anyone you speak to

What the output means

The progress count tells you how many steps and documents are ticked out of the total for your claim type. A vehicle claim with 8 of 12 steps done is moving well, and the open items are named in the copied checklist. The count is a quick check: if it is low a week after the incident, something is waiting that should not be.

The copied checklist is the real output. It lists the steps you have completed, the steps still open, the documents you have, and the documents still missing. Take the printed list with you when you speak to the insurer, and use it to check that nothing was forgotten and nothing was promised without a note.

The order of the steps matters. Reporting comes before repairing, photos come before cleaning, and keeping receipts comes before throwing anything away. The list is ordered that way on purpose, so following it in order protects the claim. Skipping ahead is how claims lose the evidence they need.

Nothing in the output is a promise about your claim. The page lists steps and documents; it cannot say whether a claim will be accepted, how much it will pay, or how long it will take. Those answers come from your insurer, under the terms of your policy.

A worked example

Take a home claim after a burst pipe. The owner picks Home, and the page shows the steps: make the area safe, turn off the water, take photos of the damage, report the claim to the insurer, keep damaged items for inspection, collect receipts for emergency repairs, and keep a note of everything.

On the first day, the owner makes the area safe, turns off the water, takes photos, and calls the insurer. Four steps ticked. The documents list asks for the policy details, photos, receipts for emergency repairs, and a list of damaged items. The owner has the policy details and the photos, so two documents ticked.

Over the next week, the owner keeps the damaged items as asked, collects the plumber's receipt, and writes the list of damaged items. The steps count moves to 7 of 7, and the documents count to 4 of 4. The copied checklist shows everything complete, and the owner prints it and keeps it with the claim file.

Now suppose the insurer asks for a report the owner does not have. The owner tells the insurer it does not exist, notes the conversation, and asks what to send instead. The checklist cannot answer that question, but it makes the gap visible, which is what lets the owner ask the right question at the right time.

Tips for a good result

Report promptly. Most policies set a time limit for reporting a claim, and a late report can weaken or lose a claim. You do not need all the documents before you report: report first, then collect. The insurer will tell you what it needs and when.

Take photos before you clean, move or repair anything. Photos taken after the clean-up show a clean room, not the damage. The damage is the evidence, and the evidence disappears the moment you tidy up. Take more photos than you think you need, from more angles.

Keep everything. Keep damaged items until the insurer has seen them or told you to throw them away. Keep every receipt, every message, and a note of every phone call, with the date and the name of the person you spoke to. A claim file with too much evidence is far better than one with too little.

Keep a claim diary. From the day of the incident, note every call, every message, and every letter, with the date and the name of the person you spoke to. A diary turns a long claim into a clear record, and it answers the question of who said what and when, which is the question that decides most disputes. A diary kept in a notebook is enough; it does not need to be formal, only honest.

Do not throw anything away until the claim is settled. Damaged items, old parts, torn clothing and broken fittings are all evidence, and an insurer may want to see them weeks after the incident. Bag them, label them, and put them somewhere out of the way. The space they take up is small, and the claim they protect is not.

  • Write down what happened while it is fresh, with dates and times
  • Do not admit fault at the scene of an accident; state the facts and let the insurer decide
  • Do not sign anything you do not understand; ask the insurer to explain it
  • If a document does not exist, say so plainly instead of leaving the item blank

Limits of the tool — what it cannot do

This builder cannot file a claim for you. It lists steps and documents; the claim itself is filed with your insurer, by you, following the insurer's process. No page can do that job, and any page that claims to file claims on your behalf should not be trusted.

It cannot tell you what your policy covers. Policies differ widely, even from the same insurer, and the checklist is general guidance, not a reading of your policy. Read your own policy document, and ask your insurer about anything in it you do not understand.

It cannot promise an outcome. Whether a claim is accepted, how much it pays, and how long it takes all depend on the policy, the evidence, and the insurer's judgement. The checklist helps you prepare well; it cannot decide the result.

This is general information only, not a promise that a claim will be accepted. Claim rules, time limits and documents differ by insurer and by country, so always follow your insurer's instructions. Contact your insurer promptly after an accident or loss, and keep copies of everything you send.

Questions people ask

What should I do first after an accident or loss?

Make sure everyone is safe first, then take photos of the damage before anything is moved or cleaned. Report the incident to your insurer promptly, even before you have all the documents. Then work through the checklist on this page, keeping copies of everything you send and notes of every conversation.

What documents does an insurer usually ask for?

An insurer usually asks for your policy details, photos of the damage, receipts for any emergency repairs, a list of damaged or lost items, and any reports that apply, such as a police report or a medical report. The checklist on this page names the usual documents for each claim type, but your insurer may ask for more.

How long do I have to file a claim?

Time limits differ by insurer and by country, and they are set in your policy. Some policies ask for a report within days, others allow longer. The safest course is to report promptly, on the day of the incident if you can, and to ask your insurer for its deadline in writing.

What if the insurer asks for something I do not have?

Say so plainly, and ask what to send instead. A document that does not exist cannot be produced, and an insurer will usually accept an alternative or a written explanation. Note the conversation, with the date and the name of the person you spoke to, and keep a copy of anything you send.

Should I repair anything before the insurer sees it?

Do not repair or throw away damaged items until the insurer has seen them or told you it is fine to do so. Emergency repairs to stop further damage, such as turning off water or boarding a window, are usually expected, and you should keep the receipts. For anything else, wait for the insurer's instruction.

Can I print my claim checklist?

Yes. Press the copy button to turn the checklist into plain text, including the steps you have completed, the steps still open, and the documents you have and still need. Paste it into any document or note and print it from there, then keep the printed page with your claim file.
