One minute you are splitting a bill between friends, the next you are working out how old someone is, and the minute after that you want to know how many calories a walk burned. Each of those jobs has its own calculator on ToolBari, and this page brings them all together in one place so you do not have to search for them one by one.

At the top of the page sits a basic calculator that is built in, ready to use. It adds, subtracts, multiplies and divides, and it works out percentages. Below the basic calculator are the cards. The right tool for the job gives you a better answer than a general calculator ever could, because it asks for the right numbers and shows the working in the right way.

Everything on this page runs in your browser, on your own device. The numbers you type are never sent anywhere and never saved. You can use the page on a phone with one thumb, on a tablet, or on a desktop computer with a keyboard. The buttons are large, the inputs are large, and the answers are shown in large text so you can read them at a glance.

The built-in basic calculator

The calculator at the top of this page is deliberately simple. There are two number boxes and a choice of four operations: add, subtract, multiply and divide. Type the first number, pick the operation, type the second number, and tap the Calculate button. The answer appears below the button, with the sum written out in words so you can check that you typed the right numbers.

Below the four operations sits the percentage tool. It answers the question "what is this number as a percentage of that number?" You type the part in the first box and the whole in the second box, and the tool gives you the percentage. This is the tool for marks on a test, a share of a total, or how much of a target you have reached. If you need a different percentage job, such as a percentage increase or a discount, open the dedicated percentage calculator from the cards below.

On a phone, tap a box and the number keyboard appears. On a desktop computer, you can click into a box and type, or use the Tab key to move between the boxes. The buttons are large enough to tap without hitting the wrong one, and the answer stays on the screen until you change the numbers and calculate again.

Worked examples

A bill split is the most common everyday sum. Four friends share a meal that costs 2400, and they want to split it equally. Type 2400 as the first number, choose divide, type 4 as the second number, and the answer is 600 each. If one friend only had a drink and the others agree to cover it, the same calculator divides 2400 by 3 instead, which gives 800 each.

A test score is a percentage question. A student answers 18 questions correctly out of 45. Type 18 as the part and 45 as the whole, and the percentage tool gives 40. That is the score as a percentage: 40 percent. The same tool answers a savings question just as easily. If you have saved 750 toward a target of 3000, then 750 as a percentage of 3000 is 25, so you are a quarter of the way there.

A discount needs two steps. A jacket is priced at 850 with 15 percent off. First, work out 15 percent of 850, which is 127.5. Then subtract that from the price: 850 minus 127.5 is 722.5. The basic calculator handles both steps, and the dedicated percentage calculator handles the whole job in one go if you prefer.

An age question is a subtraction with a twist. Someone born in 1998 wants to know their age in 2026. Subtract 1998 from 2026 and you get 28, but that is only right if their birthday has already passed this year. If it has not, they are still 27. That is exactly why the dedicated age calculator exists: it works with the full birth date and gives the exact answer in years, months and days, so you never have to guess about the birthday.

Common mistakes with calculators

The most common mistake is typing the wrong number and trusting the answer. A calculator never questions your input, so a finger slip turns 2400 into 24000 and the answer looks just as confident as a correct one. Read the sum back before you act on the answer, especially when money is involved. The basic calculator on this page shows the sum in words under the answer, which makes a wrong number easy to spot.

The second common mistake is mixing units. If one number is in metres and the other is in centimetres, the answer is meaningless. If a weight is in kilograms in one box and grams in the other, the answer is wrong by a factor of a thousand. Convert everything to the same unit first, or use the unit converter card below before you start.

The third common mistake is rounding too early. If a sum has several steps, keep the full numbers through the middle steps and round only the final answer. Rounding in the middle throws the later steps off, and on a long calculation the error grows with every step. The basic calculator shows the full answer, so you can carry it into the next step yourself.

The fourth common mistake is taking a percentage of the wrong whole. A 20 percent discount followed by a 20 percent increase does not bring you back to the starting price, because the second percentage is taken from the smaller discounted price, not from the original. Whenever a percentage question confuses you, stop and ask: percentage of what? The answer to that question tells you which number goes in the "whole" box.

The fifth common mistake is trusting a calculator over the real bill. A calculator answers the numbers you gave it. It does not know about a delivery charge, a tax, a fee, or a rounding rule on the real invoice. Use the calculator to plan, then check the result against the real document before you pay or promise anything.

What this page cannot do

This page does arithmetic, and only arithmetic. It cannot tell you whether a loan is a good idea, whether a price is fair, or whether a health number means you should change anything. Those are decisions that need a person: a bank adviser for a loan, a doctor for a health question, a teacher for a school result. The calculators give you the numbers to take into those conversations, and that is where their job ends.

This page also cannot check whether your inputs are right. If you type a height in centimetres into a box that expects metres, the answer will be wrong and the page will not warn you. Read the labels on the boxes, check your units, and read the sum back before you trust the answer.

Finally, this page does not save your results. When you close the tab, your numbers are gone, which is deliberate: nothing leaves your device, so nothing about your money or your health is stored anywhere. If you need a result later, write it down or take a screenshot before you close the page.
