How the practice sets work

This platform offers three practice sets on the basics of bookkeeping: debits and credits, the balance sheet, and journals. Each set has eight multiple-choice questions. Unlike a timed quiz, there is no clock here — you answer each question and get the correct answer with an explanation straight away.

To start, pick a topic and press the start button. Read the question, tap the answer you think is right, and the correct answer is highlighted at once, with a short explanation of the rule behind it. Read the explanation before moving on — that is where the learning happens. Press next to move to the following question.

At the end of the set you see your score out of eight. The score is a guide, not a grade. The point of the practice is to learn the rules, and the instant feedback is designed so that a wrong answer teaches as much as a right one.

What the three topics cover

Debits and credits is the foundation of double-entry bookkeeping. The questions cover what a debit and a credit do to each type of account, which account is debited and credited in common transactions, and why total debits must always equal total credits. If debits and credits feel confusing, start here and work through the set slowly.

Balance sheet covers what the balance sheet shows and how it is put together: the accounting equation, what counts as an asset, a liability or equity, and how a transaction changes the picture. The questions test whether you can tell the three categories apart and explain what happens when money moves between them.

Journals covers how transactions are recorded step by step: what a journal entry is, which entry records a given transaction, and how the journal, the ledger and the trial balance fit together. The questions use everyday transactions — buying goods, making a sale, paying a supplier — so the rules feel concrete rather than abstract.

Who the practice sets are for

The practice sets are for students learning bookkeeping, for small business owners who keep their own books, and for anyone who wants to understand what their accountant is talking about. They assume no prior knowledge beyond basic arithmetic, and every question is explained in plain words.

They are also useful as a refresher. If you studied accounting years ago and the rules have gone rusty, working through the three sets will bring the main ideas back quickly. Each set takes only a few minutes, so you can do one set at a time.

The practice sets are not a course and not a qualification. They cover the basics of double-entry bookkeeping only. Real bookkeeping involves much more — tax rules, payroll, depreciation, and the specific rules of your country — and none of that is covered here. Use the practice to build a foundation, then study further or ask a qualified accountant for the rest.

How to use the platform step by step

Worked example

Suppose you take the debits and credits set and score 6 out of 8. The review shows you missed the question about buying goods on credit and the question about what a credit does to a liability account.

The explanation for the first question says: buying on credit increases purchases, which is a debit, and increases what is owed to the supplier, which is a credit. The explanation for the second says: liabilities increase with credits and decrease with debits — the opposite of assets.

Both mistakes come from the same confusion: which side increases which type of account. The rule to remember is that assets and expenses increase with debits, while liabilities, equity and income increase with credits. Once that rule is clear, most of the questions in the set follow from it. Retake the set the next day and the score usually improves.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is thinking that debit means bad and credit means good. In bookkeeping the words have no such meaning — they are simply the left and right sides of an account. A debit increases an asset and decreases a liability. A credit does the opposite. Forget the everyday meanings of the words and learn the bookkeeping rules.

Another mistake is rushing past the explanations. The instant answer is only useful if you read it. If you tap through the questions quickly and only look at the score, you learn very little. Slow down, read the explanation for every question, and say the rule back to yourself in your own words.

A third mistake is memorising the answers instead of the rules. The questions are examples of the rules, not the rules themselves. If you can explain why buying equipment with cash debits equipment and credits cash, you understand the rule — and you can answer any question like it, not just this one.

How to study with the practice sets

The practice sets work best as part of a short study habit. Take one set, read every explanation, then write down the rules you missed in your own words. If you missed the question about buying goods on credit, write the rule: purchases increase with a debit, and what is owed to the supplier increases with a credit. Writing the rule yourself helps you remember it far better than reading it twice.

After the set, practise with real examples from your own life or business. Work out the journal entry for buying lunch with cash, for receiving money from a customer, or for paying a bill you owed. Everyday transactions use the same rules as the questions, and practising on familiar examples makes the rules feel concrete.

Keep a one-page summary of the rules as you learn them. The most useful summary fits on a single page: assets and expenses increase with debits; liabilities, equity and income increase with credits; every transaction has a debit and a credit; total debits always equal total credits; assets equal liabilities plus equity. If you can explain each line of that summary to someone else, you understand the basics.

Return to the sets after a week and retake the topic you found hardest. The improvement shows what stuck. If the same question still trips you up, study that rule on its own before moving on — a shaky foundation makes everything built on it harder.

What the practice sets cannot do

The practice sets test understanding of basic rules. They cannot check real bookkeeping work, because they do not look at your books. They cannot tell you whether your accounts are correct, whether you owe tax, or whether a transaction should be recorded in a particular way for your business.

They also cannot give you a certificate or a qualification. There is no login, no record of your scores, and no proof of completion. If you need a qualification in bookkeeping, you will need a proper course and assessment from a training provider.

Finally, the practice sets use general rules that apply in most places. Tax rules, reporting rules and the exact names of accounts differ by country. For anything that affects your tax or your legal duties, ask a qualified accountant in your own country.
