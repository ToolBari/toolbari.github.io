Advertising Budget Calculator

Work out what your advertising will earn before you spend it: enter your monthly budget, cost per click and conversion rate to see your expected clicks, sales and return on ad spend. The verdict tells you plainly whether the ads pay, roughly break even, or lose money.

All money figures use your own money units — type the numbers without any symbol. The platform choice changes the tips you see, not the maths: the maths uses the cost per click and conversion rate you enter.

These are estimates, not promises. Real costs vary by market, audience, season and how good your ad is — use the calculator to plan, then start small and check the real numbers after a week of running ads.

How to use this calculator

Start with the money you plan to spend in a month, then add the three numbers that decide what it earns: what each click costs, what share of clicks turn into sales, and what the average buyer spends. The calculator turns those four numbers into expected clicks, expected sales, the cost of each sale, and the return on your ad spend — the revenue each unit of ad spend brings back. Read the verdict last: it tells you plainly whether the plan pays, roughly breaks even, or loses money.

If you do not know your cost per click yet, look at the ad platform's own estimate for your keywords or audience, and use the higher end of the range for planning — it is better to be pleasantly surprised than to run out of budget halfway through the month. If you do not know your conversion rate, start with 1 to 2 percent for a shop that is new to advertising, and replace it with your real number as soon as you have a week of data. The average order value is the easiest number of all: take your total sales over the last month and divide by the number of orders.

Change one number at a time and watch what moves. Raising the conversion rate usually helps more than raising the budget, because every click you already pay for becomes worth more. Lowering the cost per click helps too, but only if the cheaper clicks still buy — very cheap clicks from the wrong audience can look good on paper and earn nothing. The calculator is a planning tool, not a promise: use it to compare plans before you spend, then check the real numbers after a week of running ads.

What the numbers mean

Expected clicks is the simplest number: your monthly budget divided by the cost per click. A budget of 30000 with a cost per click of 15 buys 2000 clicks. It is a ceiling, not a guarantee — some days the platform may show your ad less, and some days the cost per click rises because more advertisers are bidding for the same audience. Treat it as the size of the opportunity, not as a delivery promise.

Expected sales is clicks times conversion rate. If 2000 people click and 2 percent buy, that is 40 sales. Cost per sale is the budget divided by those sales — 30000 divided by 40 is 750 per sale. That number is the one to compare with your profit on an average order: if you make more than 750 profit on a typical order, the ads pay; if you make less, they do not, however good the click numbers look.

Return on ad spend, usually shortened to ROAS, is the revenue divided by the ad spend. Forty sales at an average order value of 1200 bring in 48000, and 48000 divided by a budget of 30000 is a return of 1.6 — each unit of ad spend brings back 1.6 units of revenue. A return above 1 means the ads bring in more revenue than they cost, but remember that revenue is not profit: the product, the delivery and the fees still come out of it. That is why the verdict treats a return between 0.8 and 1.2 as break-even — the ads roughly pay for themselves, but there is little left over.

Worked example: a small shop's first month

A small shop selling handmade soap plans its first month of search ads. The monthly budget is 30000, the expected cost per click is 15, the conversion rate is 2 percent, and the average order value is 1200. The calculator divides 30000 by 15 to get 2000 expected clicks, multiplies by 2 percent to get 40 expected sales, and divides 30000 by 40 to get a cost per sale of 750. Forty sales at 1200 each bring in 48000, so the return on ad spend is 48000 divided by 30000, which is 1.6 — the verdict is profitable.

Now change one number and see how fragile the plan is. If the conversion rate is 1 percent instead of 2, the same budget buys 20 sales, the cost per sale doubles to 1500, and the revenue falls to 24000 — a return of 0.8, which the calculator calls break-even at best and losing money in practice once product costs are counted. That single change turns a good plan into a poor one, which is why the conversion rate matters more than the budget. Before spending more on ads, the shop would be wiser to fix its product page and lift the rate from 1 percent toward 2.

The same shop tries social ads next, where the cost per click is 6 instead of 15. The budget now buys 5000 clicks, but social visitors buy less often — say 0.8 percent — which gives 40 sales again, the same cost per sale of 750, and the same return of 1.6. The two platforms look equal on paper, which is exactly the point: the cheapest clicks are not automatically the best clicks. The calculator lets you compare plans honestly, with the same maths on both sides.

Search, social or display — which platform first

Search ads reach people who are already looking for what you sell, because they show up when someone types a query. That intent is why search clicks usually cost more and convert more often. If you sell something people search for by name — a plumber in a city, a spare part, a course — search is usually the best place to start, because you are answering a question the visitor has already asked.

Social ads reach people by who they are and what they like, not by what they typed. They are good for products people did not know they wanted — a gift, a new fashion item, a local event — and the clicks are usually cheaper. The trade-off is that social visitors are browsing, not buying, so the conversion rate is usually lower and you need a stronger offer or a longer follow-up to turn a click into a sale. Social ads reward good pictures and short, clear copy.

Display ads are the banners and pictures shown across websites and apps. They are usually the cheapest per click and the weakest at converting, which makes them better for reminding people about a brand they already know than for winning a first sale. A common pattern is to start with search, add social once the product page converts well, and use display later to remind past visitors. Whatever you choose, start with one platform and learn it properly before adding a second.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is planning with the best numbers instead of honest ones. A conversion rate of 5 percent sounds good in a plan, but if your page has never converted above 1 percent, the plan is fiction. Use your real numbers, or low guesses, and let the calculator show you what is actually possible. A plan built on honest numbers that says break-even is worth more than a plan built on hopeful numbers that says profitable.

The second mistake is forgetting what a sale costs beyond the ad. The calculator shows revenue, not profit. If the product costs half the order value to make and deliver, a return of 1.6 leaves only a thin margin after the ad spend — and a return of 1.2 leaves nothing at all. Work out your profit on an average order first, then compare it with the cost per sale the calculator shows. If the cost per sale is higher than the profit per order, the ads lose money no matter how good the click numbers look.

The third mistake is judging a campaign after one day. Ad platforms need a few days to learn who clicks and who buys, and the first day's numbers jump around. Give a new campaign at least a week — or at least a hundred clicks — before you decide it is working or failing, and change only one thing at a time when you adjust it. Changing the budget, the ad text and the audience all at once teaches you nothing, because you cannot tell which change did what.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot predict your real cost per click or conversion rate — those come from the market and from your own page, and only real running ads reveal them. It cannot tell you which keywords to bid on, which audience to target, or which ad picture will work. It cannot see your competitors, the season, or the quality of your landing page, and all of those move the real numbers. What it can do is turn your guesses into honest arithmetic, so you can compare plans and spot a losing plan before you pay for it.

It also cannot manage your campaign. It does not connect to any ad platform, it does not place ads, and it does not read your results — it is a planning tool that runs entirely in your browser. Use it to set a budget you can afford, to agree on the numbers that would make the campaign a success, and to check the real results against the plan each week. If the real numbers are far from the plan, the plan was wrong, not the calculator — update the inputs with the real numbers and plan the next month from there.

Questions people ask

How much should a small business spend on ads?

Start with an amount you can afford to lose for one month, because the first month is mostly learning. Many small shops start with a modest monthly budget and raise it only when the numbers show the ads pay. The calculator helps you work backwards: decide what a sale is worth to you, then see what budget the numbers support.

What is a good cost per click?

There is no single good number — it depends on what you sell and where you advertise. A click is cheap if the sale it leads to is worth far more than the click cost, and expensive if it is not. Compare the cost per click with your cost per sale and your profit per order, not with anyone else's numbers.

What is return on ad spend?

Return on ad spend is the revenue your ads bring in divided by what you spent on them. A return of 1.6 means each unit of ad spend brought back 1.6 units of revenue. Remember it measures revenue, not profit — product costs, delivery and fees still come out of that revenue.

How do I know if my ads lose money?

Compare the cost per sale with the profit you make on an average order. If the cost per sale is higher than the profit per order, the ads lose money, however many clicks they bring. The calculator shows the cost per sale and a plain verdict so you can see this before you spend.

Should I start with search or social ads?

If people already search for what you sell, start with search ads — the visitor has already asked for it. If your product is new or visual and people do not search for it yet, social ads can work better. Start with one platform, learn it, and add a second only when the first one pays.

Can I use this for a daily budget too?

Yes. Multiply your daily budget by 30 to get a monthly figure and enter that, or enter the daily budget and read the results as daily numbers — the maths is the same either way. Just keep the budget and the results in the same period, so you do not mix a daily budget with a monthly sales target.
