What these language learning tools are

Learning a new language starts with a small set of words you can actually use. Hello, thank you, please, the numbers from one to ten, the days of the week: these are the phrases that open conversations, help you buy a ticket, ask for directions, and make a good first impression. This page collects those starting phrases in one place, and gives you three simple tools to learn them: a phrase list you translate into your own language, a practice quiz that tests what you remember, and a short daily routine that keeps you going.

The phrase list is the heart of the page. Every phrase comes with a plain English meaning, so you always know exactly what you are learning. Next to each phrase is a blank box where you type the translation in your own language. That step matters. Writing the translation yourself makes the phrase stick in a way that reading alone never does, and it means this page works for any language you want to learn, from French to Bangla to Arabic.

The practice quiz turns the list into a game. It shows you a phrase and four possible meanings, and you pick the one you think is right. Your score updates as you go, so you can see yourself improving from one day to the next. The daily routine ties it all together: ten minutes a day, split into short steps, so learning a language fits into a busy life instead of demanding hours you do not have.

Everything runs in your browser. There is nothing to download, no account to create, and no cost. Your translations are saved on your own device, so they are still there when you come back tomorrow.

The 10-minute daily routine

Ten minutes is short on purpose. A long study session sounds impressive, but it is easy to put off, and a missed session often turns into a missed week. Ten minutes, on the other hand, fits into almost any day: on the bus, over a cup of tea, or in the quiet minutes before bed. The secret of learning a language is not studying for hours once in a while. It is showing up for a few minutes every day.

The routine has four steps. In the first two minutes, say yesterday's phrases out loud. Speaking matters from the very first day, because a phrase you have only read is not a phrase you can use. In the next three minutes, learn three new phrases from the list, and write your translations in the boxes. Three is enough: small steps add up faster than big leaps.

In the following three minutes, take the practice quiz. Testing yourself is one of the best ways to remember, and the quiz quickly shows you which phrases are solid and which ones need another look. In the last two minutes, say all of today's phrases out loud once more, slowly and clearly.

Try to practise at the same time each day, so it becomes a habit rather than a decision. After breakfast, after lunch, or before bed: pick a time that suits you and keep it. If you miss a day, do not worry, and do not try to catch up by doubling the next session. Just start again the next day. One missed day changes nothing. Quitting after a missed day changes everything.

The phrase lists: greetings, polite words, numbers and days

The four lists on this page cover the phrases you are most likely to need first. Greetings come first because every conversation starts with one. Hello, good morning, good evening, and goodbye are small words, but they open doors. A friendly greeting tells the other person you are making an effort, and people almost always respond warmly to that.

Polite words come next. Please, thank you, sorry, and excuse me cost nothing and change everything. They turn a demand into a request and a mistake into a moment of grace. In a new language, polite words are also a safety net: even if the rest of your sentence is wrong, a polite word shows good intentions, and the person you are speaking to will usually help you find the words you need.

Numbers from one to ten are more useful than they look. You need them for prices, for times, for addresses, for phone numbers, and for counting anything at all. Once you know the first ten numbers, larger numbers usually follow a pattern in most languages, so these ten are the foundation for all the counting you will ever do.

The days of the week are just as practical. You need them to make plans, to understand opening times, to talk about what happened yesterday and what will happen tomorrow. Learning the seven days takes only a few minutes, and you will use them every single day.

Work through the lists in order, a few phrases at a time. Say each phrase out loud as you learn it, and try to use it the same day, even if only in a sentence you say to yourself. When you are unsure about a translation, check it with a dictionary or ask someone who speaks the language. A correct translation learned slowly is worth far more than a quick guess that turns out to be wrong.

How the practice quiz works

The practice quiz is simple by design. Press the quiz button and the page shows you a phrase from the list, together with four possible meanings. Only one of them is right. Read the phrase, think about what it means, and tap the answer you believe is correct. Your score updates straight away, so you always know how you are doing.

Each round of the quiz uses five different phrases, picked at random from the whole list. That means no two rounds are exactly the same, and you cannot pass by memorising the order of the questions. When you finish a round, press the quiz button again for a fresh set of phrases.

Getting an answer wrong is part of the process, not a failure. A wrong answer tells you exactly which phrase to look at again, and that moment of surprise helps the correct meaning stick. If the same phrase keeps catching you out, go back to the list, read its meaning again, say it out loud a few times, and try the quiz again later in the day.

Use the quiz after every learning session, and again the next day before you learn new phrases. That second test, a day later, is the real measure of what you remember. Do not chase a perfect score. Chase a better score than yesterday, and let the habit do the rest.

Common mistakes when learning a language

The first mistake is only learning words and never speaking. A list of words is useful, but a language lives in the mouth, not on the page. Every phrase you learn should be said out loud, more than once, until it feels natural. If you feel shy, practise alone first: say the phrases to yourself while you walk, cook, or wait for the bus. Nobody is listening, and your mouth is learning the shapes of new sounds.

The second mistake is never speaking to real people at all. Many learners wait until they feel ready, and ready never comes. You do not need perfect grammar to say hello, thank you, or please. Start with those three words in a real conversation, and let the rest follow. Most people are delighted when someone tries to speak their language, and they will forgive every mistake you make.

The third mistake is quitting after three days. The first days of learning a language feel slow, because everything is new and nothing has had time to settle. That is normal. The phrases you learn today will feel easier tomorrow, and easier still next week, but only if you keep showing up. Give the routine at least two weeks before you judge it.

The fourth mistake is trying to learn everything at once. A long list of new words feels like progress, but most of them will be forgotten by tomorrow. Three new phrases a day, learned properly and tested the next day, will take you further than thirty phrases learned in a rush.

The final mistake is comparing yourself with other people. Someone else may learn faster, speak more smoothly, or know more words. That says nothing about you. You started where you started, and every phrase you learn is yours to keep. Learn at your own pace, keep your daily ten minutes, and the language will come.

Questions people ask

Do I need to sign up to use these language learning tools?

No. Everything works in your browser with no account and no signup. Your translations are saved on your own device, so they stay private to you.

Which language can I learn with this page?

Any language you want. The phrases start in English and you write the translation in your own language, so the same page works for French, Spanish, Arabic, Bangla, or any other language.

How long should I study each day?

Ten minutes a day is enough to start. A short daily session you actually do beats a long session you keep putting off, and the routine on this page is built around ten minutes.

Will my translations still be there tomorrow?

Yes, as long as you use the same browser on the same device. Your translations are saved in your browser, so they are still there when you come back. If you clear your browser data or switch devices, they will be gone.

How does the practice quiz work?

The quiz shows you a phrase and four possible meanings. Pick the one you think is right and your score updates straight away. Each round uses different phrases, so you can play again and keep practising.

Can this page teach me a whole language?

No, and no single page can. These tools give you a strong start with everyday phrases, numbers and days, and a daily habit. For full fluency you will also need speaking practice, listening and reading in the language.
