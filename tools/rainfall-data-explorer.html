How to use the log

This log is simple on purpose. Each day you measure rain, add the date and the amount in millimetres, and the explorer does the rest: it adds up your monthly totals, finds your wettest and driest months, and works out your running average per logged day. The more days you log, the more the numbers mean — a month with three entries is a hint, a month with thirty is a record.

Log zero days too, when you can. A day with no rain is just as much a fact as a day with 20 mm, and it matters for the average: if you only log rainy days, your average will look far wetter than your weather really is. If you measured and got nothing, add the date with 0 mm.

Everything stays in this browser only, on your own device. This page does not fetch live weather data and it does not know whether it rained at your home — it only works with the numbers you type in. If you clear your browser data, your log goes with it, so use the copy button now and then to keep a backup of your summary.

Measuring rain at home with a simple gauge

You do not need expensive equipment to measure rain. A rain gauge is a straight-sided container with a scale marked in millimetres, and even a simple plastic one is good enough for a home log. Place it in the open, away from walls, fences and trees, with its top level and its opening clear of anything overhead. Read it at the same time each day, and empty it after reading.

If you do not have a gauge, a straight-sided jar and a ruler will do for a rough record: measure the depth of water in millimetres after the rain, and write it down. The reading will not be as precise as a proper gauge, but it is far better than guessing, and the habit of measuring is what makes the log useful.

Where you put the gauge matters more than what it cost. Rain does not fall straight down in wind, and a gauge tucked against a wall will catch less than one in the open. A gauge under a tree will catch drips long after the rain has stopped, and a gauge near a roof edge can catch splash-back that never fell from the sky. An open spot, away from anything taller than the gauge itself, is the honest place.

Reading the totals

The monthly total is the headline number: all the rain you logged in that month, added up. Compare months with each other, but remember that a month with missing days is not a fair comparison — if you only logged half of March, the March total is a half-month total, not a month. The explorer shows how many days you logged, so you can judge how complete each month is.

The wettest and driest months tell you about the shape of your year. In many places one or two months carry most of the rain, and the rest of the year is dry by comparison. Knowing which months those are helps you plan a garden, a building job, or a trip — not because the pattern repeats exactly, but because the shape of a year usually holds.

The running average — total millimetres divided by the number of logged days — is the number to watch over time. It smooths out single storms and dry spells, and it is the fairest way to compare one period with another. If your average over the last three months is well above the same period last year, your area really has been wetter, and it is not just one big storm fooling you.

What 10 mm of rain really means

Rainfall in millimetres is a depth, not a volume, and that confuses people. Ten millimetres of rain means that if the rain had fallen on a flat surface and stayed there, it would stand 10 millimetres deep. Over one square metre of ground, 10 mm of rain is 10 litres of water — a full watering can. Over a garden of 100 square metres, the same 10 mm is 1,000 litres.

That is the worked example to keep in mind: millimetres times square metres gives litres. A light shower of 2 mm over a roof of 50 square metres is 100 litres into the gutters. A heavy day of 40 mm over the same roof is 2,000 litres. When you read a forecast that says 5 mm, you now know what it means for your garden, your water butt, or your roof.

It also explains why a small number can matter. Five millimetres sounds like nothing, but over a field it is thousands of litres, and over a dry garden it is the difference between plants surviving and plants wilting. The millimetre is small; the area it falls on is what turns it into real water.

What the numbers mean for plants and flooding

For plants, what matters is not just how much rain fell, but when. A month with 60 mm spread evenly across the month keeps soil damp and plants happy; the same 60 mm in two storms, with three dry weeks between, leaves plants thirsty in between and the soil washed out after. Your log shows the pattern, not just the total, and the pattern is what a gardener needs.

For flooding, the danger number is intensity: how much falls in how short a time. Drains and soil can absorb a steady rain, but a short, violent downpour overwhelms them, and the water runs off instead of soaking in. If your log shows a day with a very high number, note it — that is the kind of day that floods roads and gardens, even in a month that looks dry overall.

For a water butt or a tank, the log helps you plan. If your wettest months reliably deliver far more than you use, the tank will fill then, and the question is whether it lasts through the dry months. Your own monthly totals, over a year or two, are the best guide to the size of tank you actually need.

Wet-season vs dry-season patterns

Most places have a shape to their year: a wet season and a dry season, even if the line between them is not sharp. Your log will show the shape for your own home, which can differ from the official pattern for your region — a hill, a valley, or the side of a city you live on can all shift the rain you get.

For official rainfall records, check your country's weather service, which publishes measurements from its own gauges. Those records are the right source for questions like "was this the wettest June on record?" Your own log answers a different question: "how much rain fell on my home?" Both are useful, and they complement each other rather than competing.

Over a year or two, your log becomes a small climate record of your own. You will see which month is usually wettest, how long dry spells last, and whether this year is wetter or drier than last. That is knowledge no forecast can give you, because a forecast looks forward a few days and your log looks back over seasons.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is measuring next to a wall. A wall shelters the gauge from wind-driven rain on one side and bounces splash into it on the other, so the reading is wrong in ways you cannot correct for. Move the gauge into the open, even if it is less convenient to reach.

The second is mixing up millimetres and centimetres. A reading of 1.2 cm is 12 mm, and a reading of 12 cm would be a flood. Always record in millimetres, and if your gauge is marked in centimetres, multiply by ten before you log the number.

The third is logging only rainy days. If you skip the dry days, your average is the average of rainy days only, which is a different number from your real average — and a misleading one. Log the zeros. They are quick to add and they make every other number in the log honest.

The fourth is forgetting to empty the gauge. A gauge left full after a storm will overflow in the next one, and the overflow is lost rain that never gets recorded. Empty it after each reading, and read it at the same time each day so no day is counted twice.

The fifth is treating one wet month as a trend. Weather varies, and a single wet or dry month proves nothing about the climate. Wait for a full year of entries before you draw conclusions, and compare like with like — the same month in different years, not March with August.

Questions people ask

How is rainfall measured?

Rainfall is measured as a depth in millimetres, using a rain gauge — a straight-sided container with a scale. The depth tells you how deep the rain would stand on a flat surface, and over one square metre, 1 mm of rain is 1 litre of water.

What does 10 mm of rain mean?

It means 10 litres of water fell on every square metre of ground — a full watering can per square metre. Over a garden of 100 square metres, the same 10 mm is 1,000 litres of water.

How do I measure rain at home?

Place a rain gauge — or a straight-sided jar and a ruler — in the open, away from walls, fences and trees, with its top level. Read it at the same time each day, record the depth in millimetres, and empty it after reading.

Where can I find official rainfall records?

Your country's weather service publishes official rainfall measurements from its own gauges. Those records are the right source for regional questions; your own log records what fell on your home.

Is my log data uploaded anywhere?

No. Your entries stay in this browser only, on your own device, and this page does not fetch weather data or connect to any server. If you clear your browser data, your log will be removed, so copy your summary to keep a backup.

Which month is usually wettest?

It depends on where you live — most places have a wet season, but the month varies by region. Your own log, over a year or two, will show which month is wettest at your home, and the explorer highlights it for you.
