How to use this guide

Merging and splitting are the two most common PDF jobs, and both are free to do. Merging joins several PDF files into one document, so a report, a set of scanned pages, or a bundle of forms becomes a single file that is easy to send and easy to read. Splitting does the opposite: it takes one long PDF and breaks it into smaller files, so you can share one chapter, pull out a single page, or cut a huge file down to a size that fits an upload limit.

This guide is honest about what happens here and what happens elsewhere. This page does not merge or split your files, because a web page on its own cannot change a PDF file. What it does is show you the free tools that can, with the steps for each one, and a split planner that works out your page ranges before you start. Use the planner first if you are splitting: knowing your ranges in advance saves you from guessing and re-doing the job.

You do not need to install anything to follow this guide, and you do not need to pay anything. Every method below is free. If a tool asks you to pay before it lets you download the result, you have reached the wrong tool: close it and try one of the free ones named here. The free tools named in this guide all work the same basic way, so once you have used one of them, the others will feel familiar.

How to merge PDF files

Method 1 — a free online tool. The quickest way to merge PDFs is a free online tool that runs in your browser. Open one of the well-known free PDF sites, such as iLovePDF or Smallpdf, and look for the merge tool. Upload the files you want to join, drag them into the order you want, and press the merge button. The site builds one PDF and gives you a download link. The whole job takes a minute or two, and it works the same way on a phone, a tablet, or a computer. The only thing to remember is that your files travel to the site's server to be merged, so this method is best for ordinary files, not private ones.

Method 2 — a free desktop app. If your files are private, or if you merge PDFs often, a free desktop app is the better choice, because nothing leaves your computer. PDF24 Tools offers a free desktop app for Windows that includes a merge tool, and Sejda offers a free desktop version too. Install the app once, open the merge tool inside it, add your files, arrange them, and save the result. The first setup takes a few minutes, but after that each merge is a matter of seconds, and you can work without an internet connection. A desktop app is also the right choice when your files are very large, because uploading a huge file to a website can be slow.

Method 3 — on your phone. On a phone, the simplest route is the browser method: open a free online PDF tool in your phone's browser, pick the files from your phone storage, and merge them the same way you would on a computer. Some phones also have a built-in way to combine PDFs through the Files app or a print-to-PDF trick, but the steps vary by phone, so the browser method is the reliable one to remember. Merging on a phone works best with a small number of files: arranging twenty files in order on a small screen is slow and easy to get wrong, so for a big job, use a computer if you can. If you only have a phone, merge in small batches, two or three files at a time, and check each result before you move on.

How to split a PDF

Method 1 — split by page range. This is the most useful way to split, and it is what the planner above is for. Decide how you want the file divided, then use a free splitting tool to cut it at those pages. For example, a 47-page report might become five parts of about ten pages each, or a 100-page manual might become four parts of 25 pages. Enter your numbers in the planner, copy the ranges it gives you, and type those ranges into the splitting tool. Most free tools accept ranges written exactly like this: 1-10, 11-20, and so on. Splitting by range is the right choice when you need parts of a similar size, for example to fit an upload limit that each part must stay under.

Method 2 — split by chapter. If your PDF is a book or a long report with chapters, splitting by chapter is often more useful than splitting by equal sizes. Look at the table of contents, note the first page of each chapter, and use those page numbers as your split points. Each chapter becomes its own file, which is much easier to share or print than a file that stops in the middle of a chapter. The planner can help here too: a chapter's range runs from its first page to the page just before the next chapter starts, so if chapter two starts on page 31, chapter one is pages 1 to 30. Name each part after its chapter, so anyone who receives the file can tell at a glance what is inside it.

Method 3 — one page at a time. Sometimes you need just one page out of a long file: a single form, a single certificate, or one page to send to someone. Most free splitting tools have an option to extract a single page, or to split the file into one file per page. If you only need one page, extract that page and ignore the rest. If you need every page separately, the one-file-per-page option does the whole job in one go. A 20-page PDF becomes 20 one-page files, each named in order, which is handy when you need to print or share pages one by one. Keep the original file until you have checked the extracted pages, in case you picked the wrong page number.

Keeping the page order right

The most common merging mistake is a wrong order, and it is also the easiest to avoid. Before you start, list your files in the order they should appear, and check the list twice. If the files are named in a confusing way, rename them first, adding a number at the start of each name, so the order is obvious: 01-cover, 02-introduction, 03-chapter-one, and so on. When you upload them to the merge tool, check the preview order before you press merge. Most tools show the files in a row and let you drag them into place, so take a moment to look before you commit.

For splitting, the same care applies in reverse. Write down your page ranges before you start, and keep the planner's output open while you work. After the split, open each new file and check its first and last page: the first page should be the one you expected, and the last page should be the one just before the next part starts. If a part is missing a page or has an extra one, the range was typed wrong, and it is quick to redo once you spot it.

A short checklist keeps both jobs safe:

  • Check the page count of the original file before you start.
  • Write down the ranges, or copy them from the planner above.
  • Merge in the order you listed, and check the preview before you press merge.
  • After splitting, open each part and check its first and last page.
  • Rename the finished parts clearly, so you can tell them apart later.
  • Keep the original files until you have checked the result.

There is no single best method: the right one depends on your files and your device. A quick job on ordinary files is fastest in the browser. A private file belongs in a desktop app. A job on the move belongs on your phone. The table below sums up the trade-offs, so you can choose in a few seconds instead of reading the whole guide again.

The only real rule is the privacy one: if you would not want a stranger to read the file, do not upload it.

What merging and splitting cannot fix

Merging and splitting only rearrange pages. They cannot fix a PDF that is broken, they cannot make a blurry scan sharp, and they cannot turn a scanned picture of text into real text. If a PDF will not open at all, no merger or splitter will save it: you need a fresh copy from whoever sent it. If the pages are upside down or sideways, that is a different job, and most free PDF tools have a separate rotate tool for it. If a page is missing from the original file, merging cannot bring it back.

Common mistakes

Merging in the wrong order. This is the number one mistake, and it happens because people upload files in the order their computer lists them, which is often alphabetical, not the order they want. Always arrange the files yourself, and always check the preview. A minute of checking saves a repeat of the whole job.

Splitting without noting the page numbers. If you split a file without writing down the ranges first, you end up guessing, and guessing leads to parts that start or end on the wrong page. Use the planner, copy the ranges, and keep them visible while you work.

Uploading private files to a random site. Not every free PDF site is careful with your files. Stick to well-known tools, and for anything private, use a desktop app so the file never leaves your computer. If a site looks rough, asks for payment, or shows you pages of adverts before it lets you work, close it and use a better-known one.

Deleting the originals too soon. Keep your original files until you have opened the merged or split result and checked every page. Storage is cheap, and a lost original is often gone for good. Once the result is checked and saved, then you can tidy up.

Splitting to dodge a size limit when compression would do. If a file is too big to upload, splitting works, but it leaves you with several files to manage. Try compressing first: a smaller single file is usually easier for the person receiving it. Splitting is the right answer when you genuinely need separate files, not just a smaller one.

Questions people ask

Can I merge PDF files for free without signing up?

Yes. Several free online tools let you merge PDFs in your browser without an account, and free desktop apps can do the same job offline. This guide shows both ways step by step, so you can pick the one that suits your device.

Is it safe to upload a PDF to a free online merger?

For ordinary files such as school notes or public forms, a well-known online tool is usually fine. For private files such as bank statements or medical records, use a desktop app instead, so the file never leaves your computer.

How do I split a PDF into single pages?

Open the file in a free splitting tool, choose the split by pages option, and tell it to make one file per page. A 20 page PDF becomes 20 one-page files, each named in order.

Can I merge PDFs on my phone?

Yes. Open a free online PDF tool in your phone browser, pick the files from your phone storage, arrange them in order, and download the merged file. No app download is needed for the online method.

Will merging change the quality of my PDFs?

No, merging only joins the pages in order. It does not change the text or the images inside them, so the merged file keeps the same quality as the originals.

What if my PDF is damaged and will not open?

Merging and splitting cannot repair a damaged file. If a PDF will not open at all, try opening it in a different reader, or ask the person who sent it for a fresh copy.
