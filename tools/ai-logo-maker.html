How to use it

Start with the name. Type your business or brand name exactly as you want it to appear — the maker shows it as you type it, capital letters and all. Short names work best: one or two words fit cleanly at a large size, while a long name shrinks to fit. If your full name is long, try the short version you actually use — "Rise" instead of "Rise Bakery and Cake Shop", for example.

Then add a tagline if you have one. A tagline is optional, and many good logos have none. If yours adds meaning — "Fresh every morning" for a bakery, "Learn by doing" for a tuition page — add it. If it only repeats the name, leave it out. The preview shows the tagline under the name in smaller text, so you can see at once whether it helps or clutters.

Next, pick a style. Modern is bold and clean, with a strong typeface. Classic uses a serif typeface — the kind with small feet on the letters — which suits a shop or a service that wants to look established. Playful is bright and friendly, with an italic tagline. Minimal drops the background entirely and shows just the name and shape, which suits a page or a channel that will sit on many different backgrounds.

Then pick colours and an icon shape. The colour choices are pairs that already work together — a main colour and an accent — so you do not have to guess. The icon is a simple shape: a circle, a star, a leaf, a bolt, a rounded square, or nothing at all. Text-only logos are perfectly good, and sometimes the name alone is the strongest mark. Try a few combinations, then download the one you like.

What makes a logo work

A good logo is readable. That sounds obvious, but it is the test most logos fail. If you cannot read the name at a glance — on a phone screen, on a small profile picture, on a sign across the street — the logo is not working, however pretty it looks up close. Big clear letters, strong contrast between the text and the background, and no more than a few words: those three things do most of the work.

A good logo has one idea. One name, one shape, one or two colours. Every extra element — a second icon, a third colour, a long tagline, a decorative border — dilutes the idea and makes the logo harder to remember. The strongest logos in the world are almost all simple: a name, a shape, a colour. This maker is built around that rule, which is why it offers simple shapes and paired colours instead of endless options.

A good logo works small. Most of the time, people will see your logo small — as a profile picture, a favicon, a label on a package. A logo that only works large is a poster, not a logo. Test yours by shrinking the preview: hold the phone at arm's length, or look at the small preview on the page. If the name is still readable and the shape is still clear, the logo passes. If the tagline vanishes into a grey line, drop the tagline.

A good logo also works in one colour. Imagine it printed in black on a white receipt, or stamped on a paper bag. If the logo depends on its colours to make sense, it will fail there. The text-and-shape logos from this maker pass that test easily, because the name and the shape carry the meaning and the colour only dresses it.

SVG or PNG: which file to use

The maker offers two file types, and the choice depends on where the logo will go. SVG is a vector file: it describes the logo as shapes and text, so it stays sharp at any size — from a tiny favicon to a shop sign three metres wide. Use SVG when you need to resize the logo later, when you are sending it to a printer or a sign maker, or when you want the sharpest possible result on a website.

PNG is a picture file: it stores the logo as pixels at a fixed size. The PNG from this maker is 1200 by 800 pixels, which is large enough for social media, documents, slides and most printing. Use PNG when you need to drop the logo straight into a post, a document or an app that does not accept SVG files — which is most of them. PNG is the practical, everyday choice; SVG is the quality choice for resizing and printing.

A simple habit covers both: download both files and keep them together in one folder. Use the PNG day to day, and keep the SVG for the day you need the logo bigger, sharper, or printed. The two files cost nothing, and having both means you never have to remake the logo.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is too many colours. Three or four colours fight each other, and the logo ends up looking busy instead of bold. Two colours — a main and an accent — are enough for almost every logo. The colour pairs on this page are chosen to work together, so pick one pair and stop there.

The second mistake is an unreadable typeface. Fancy, curly or ultra-thin lettering looks interesting up close and illegible everywhere else. The typefaces on this page are plain on purpose: bold sans-serif for modern and playful, serif for classic, light weight for minimal. Readability is the job; decoration is not.

The third mistake is copying a famous logo. Taking the shape, colours or lettering of a brand everyone knows does not make your business look established — it makes it look like a copy, and it can get you into real trouble if the brand's owners notice. Build your own mark from your own name. It will be smaller and simpler than a famous logo, and it will be yours.

The fourth mistake is a logo that only works at one size. If the tagline is essential to the logo, the logo fails everywhere small. If the icon only makes sense when it is large, it is not an icon. Design for the smallest use first — the profile picture — and the large uses take care of themselves.
