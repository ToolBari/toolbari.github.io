One page if you can

Early in your career, one page is enough — and it is what most hiring managers prefer. One page forces you to choose what matters. It also respects the reader's time. A two-page resume from someone with two years of experience usually means the writer padded it, and the reader can tell.

Two pages become reasonable when you have many years of experience and the second page holds real achievements: more roles, more results, more proof. Even then, the first page must stand on its own. Many readers never turn to page two, so the strongest material goes first: your summary, your most recent role, and your best numbers.

How do you fit a career onto one page? Cut duties and keep results. Cut the job from ten years ago that has nothing to do with this one. Cut the paragraph of adjectives in the summary and replace it with two sentences that say what you do and what you deliver. The builder's preview helps here — if your resume looks crowded, something needs to go.

Numbers in bullets

The difference between a weak bullet and a strong one is usually a number. Responsible for stock is a duty. Cut picking errors by 30 percent is a result. The first tells the reader what you were supposed to do. The second tells them what actually happened because of you.

You do not need dramatic numbers. Small, honest ones work just as well: served 40 customers a day, trained 3 new starters, kept the rota for a team of 8, cut costs 20 percent by switching suppliers. The number makes the claim checkable, and checkable claims are the ones a hiring manager believes.

If you genuinely cannot put a number on something, put a scale on it instead: how many people, how often, how big, how long. Handled the phones becomes answered 50 calls a day. Helped with training becomes trained every new starter in their first week. And never invent a number — if an interviewer asks how you measured it and you cannot answer, the whole resume is in doubt.

Start each bullet with a strong verb: cut, built, trained, fixed, organised, raised, saved. Avoid weak openings like responsible for and involved in — they describe a job, not a result. Three or four strong bullets under your most recent role are worth more than ten weak ones.

What to put in each section

Name and contact. Your name, the job title you want, a phone number, an email address and your city. That is all. No full home address, no date of birth, no photograph — none of those help, and some of them cause problems.

Professional summary. Two or three sentences: what you do, how long you have done it, and the result you are known for. Tailor it to each job — the same person writes a different summary for a logistics job and a team-lead job, because the reader cares about different things.

Work experience. Your most recent job first, with the company, the dates, and three or four achievement bullets. Older jobs get fewer bullets. If you have no paid work yet, use internships, volunteering, projects or part-time jobs — the builder does not care what the work was called, only that you can show what you did.

Education. Your highest qualification first, with the school and the year. Recent graduates can add a line about a project or a subject they did well in. Experienced workers can keep this section to one line.

Skills. The skills from the job description that you genuinely have, in the employer's own words. If the job asks for stock control and you ran a stock room, write stock control. Keep the list honest and short — ten real skills beat twenty claimed ones.

Mistakes to avoid

Copying the same resume everywhere. Tailor the summary and the skills to each job. The 2026 numbers are clear: tailored resumes reached interviews at 5.71 percent, against 3.09 percent for generic ones.

Leaving out contact details. Check the top of your resume every time. A great resume with a wrong phone number is a missed job.

Listing duties instead of results. Every bullet should carry a number or a scale. If it does not, ask yourself what actually changed because of you.

Claiming what you cannot prove. A skill you used once is not a skill you can claim. An interviewer will ask, and a shaky answer undoes the whole resume.

Forgetting to check spelling. Read the finished resume out loud before you send it. Your ear catches what your eye misses.

One page if you can, especially early in your career. Two pages are fine with many years of experience, as long as the second page holds real achievements. If your preview looks crowded, cut something.

What if I have no work experience?

Use internships, volunteering, projects, part-time jobs or coursework. The builder does not care what the work was called — it shows what you did and what happened because of you.

Should I include a photo or my date of birth?

No. Neither helps your application, and both can cause problems. Name, job title, phone, email and city are enough.
