What an ATS is, in plain words
An ATS — applicant tracking system — is the software most employers use to store and sort job applications. When you apply online, your resume goes into this system before any human sees it. The system reads your resume, pulls out the important parts — your job titles, your skills, your education, your dates — and stores them in a form the employer can search.
Many systems also rank or filter applications. They compare the words in your resume with the words in the job description, and they score the match. If your resume does not contain the skills the job asks for, the system may decide you are not a match — even when you are, and even when the words are there in a different form. If your resume is a fancy design with tables, columns or pictures, the system may read it as a jumble and store nothing useful at all.
This is not the system being unfair. It is the system doing the job it was built for: sorting hundreds of applications into a pile a human can actually read. Your job is to make the system's job easy. That means a plain format, the right words, and contact details it can find. This checker tells you, before you send anything, whether your resume does those three things.
How the checker works
Paste your resume into the first box and the full job description into the second, then press the button. The checker runs five tests, all in your browser — nothing is sent anywhere.
Keywords. It pulls the important words out of the job description — the skills, tools and duties, not the filler words — and checks which ones appear in your resume. It lists the missing ones so you can add the ones that are true for you.
Length. It counts your words and tells you whether your resume is very short, about one page, or long. One page (roughly 250 to 800 words) is the sweet spot for most applications.
Contact details. It checks whether your resume contains a phone number or an email address the employer can reach you on. A resume without contact details is a missed job.
Bullet points. It counts your bullet points. Short lines starting with a dash are the easiest thing for a system and a human to read, and a resume with almost no bullets is usually a wall of text.
Formatting flags. It looks for things that break applicant tracking systems: tabs, long runs of spaces, table borders made of box characters, and image references. Each flag comes with a plain explanation of what to fix.
Then it gives you a score out of 100 and a fix list, in plain words, starting with the fix that matters most. Copy the report and work through it before you send your application.
Keyword rules
Keywords are the words the employer used in the job description, and the single most useful thing you can do is to use the same words — where they are true for you. If the job asks for stock control and you ran a stock room, write stock control. If it asks for customer care and you handled fifty calls a day, write customer care. You are not inventing anything. You are naming your real experience in the employer's own words.
Three rules keep you honest. First, only add a keyword if it is true. Claiming a skill you do not have will cost you the job in the interview, when the interviewer asks about it. Second, use the keyword in context, not as a list of words at the bottom of the page. Systems and humans both notice a keyword dump, and it reads as dishonest. Third, match the exact form the employer used: if the advert says team rostering, write team rostering, not just rostering — small differences in wording can be the difference between a match and a miss.
Tailoring your keywords is the heart of tailoring your resume, and the numbers back it up. In the second quarter of 2026, tailored resumes reached interviews at 5.71 percent, against 3.09 percent for generic ones. The checker shows you exactly which keywords you are missing, so the tailoring takes minutes instead of guesswork.

What the score means — and what it does not
The score is a guide, not a verdict. A high score means your resume is easy for a system to read and matches the job description well. A low score means something specific needs fixing, and the fix list tells you what. But no score can tell you whether you will get the job, because different employers use different systems, and a human still reads the resume in the end.
Treat the score as a checklist, not a grade. If it says you are missing keywords, add the true ones. If it says your resume is too short, add detail about what you actually did. If it flags your formatting, clean it up. Then run the check again and watch the score rise. What matters is not the number — it is that every fix makes your application clearer to the next reader, human or machine.
One warning: do not chase a perfect score by stuffing keywords into your resume. A resume packed with the job's words, with no real experience behind them, reads as dishonest to a human — and the human is the one who decides. Use the keywords that are true for you, in sentences about work you really did, and stop there.
Fixing a low score
If your score is low, work through the fix list in order. Start with keywords: read the job description again, find the skills you genuinely have, and name them in your resume in the employer's own words. That one change usually lifts the score the most.
Next, check your length. A resume under 150 words is almost always too thin — add detail about what you did, with numbers where you can. A resume over 1,200 words is usually carrying jobs or duties that do not matter for this role — cut them.
Then check the basics: a phone number or email address at the top, bullet points for your achievements, and plain formatting with no tabs, no tables and no pictures. These are five-minute fixes, and they are the ones people forget most often.
Finally, read the resume out loud. The checker cannot hear your voice, but the hiring manager will — in the interview, and in the cover letter. If a sentence sounds wrong when you say it, rewrite it. Then run the check one more time, copy the report for your records, and send the application.


Questions people ask


What is a good ATS score?
There is no pass mark — different employers use different systems. As a rule, a score in the 80s means your resume is easy to read and matches the job well. Below 50, work through the fix list before you send it.
Does the checker store my resume?
No. Everything runs in your browser on your own computer. Nothing you paste is sent anywhere, and nothing is saved when you close the page.
Why does my resume score low when I am qualified?
Usually keywords or formatting. If your resume says the right things in different words, the system may not match them — use the job description's own words where they are true. If your formatting uses tabs or tables, the system may not read it at all.
Should I add every missing keyword?
Only the ones that are true for you. Claiming a skill you do not have will cost you the job in the interview. Add the true keywords, in sentences about work you really did.
How long should my resume be?
About one page — roughly 250 to 800 words — for most applications. Two pages are fine with many years of experience, as long as the second page holds real achievements.
Can a high score guarantee an interview?
No. The score measures how well your resume matches the job description and how readable it is to a system. The hiring decision still depends on the fit between you and the role.
