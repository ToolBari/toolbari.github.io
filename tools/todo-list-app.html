What this app does

A to-do list is a simple promise to yourself: these are the things I will do today. This page gives you a to-do list that lives in your browser, with no account, no signup and no app to install. Add a task, give it a due date and a priority if you like, and check it off when it is done. The list is saved on your own device, so it is still there when you come back tomorrow, and it never leaves your device at all.

The app is deliberately small. There are no folders, no tags, no projects and no settings to learn. There is a box to type a task into, a list of your tasks, and a count of how many you finished today. That is the whole app. A to-do list should take seconds to use, not minutes to manage, because the point of the list is to get things done, not to spend the morning organising the list itself.

Every task on the list can be checked off, edited or deleted. Checked-off tasks stay visible until you clear them, so you can see what you have done. The done-today count shows how many tasks you finished today, which is a small reward at the end of a long day. When the list is empty, the page simply says so, and you can enjoy the quiet.

How to use it

On a phone, tap the box at the top and type your task in plain words, starting with a verb: call the bank, buy bread, finish the report. Then pick a due date if the task has one, and a priority: low, medium or high. Tap the green Add task button and the task appears in the list below. To finish a task, tap the box beside it. The task is crossed out and the done-today count goes up by one. To change a task, tap Edit, change the words, and tap Save changes. To remove a task, tap Delete. To remove all the finished tasks at once, tap Clear completed tasks at the bottom of the page.

On a desktop computer the steps are the same, and you can press the Enter key after typing a task instead of clicking the Add button. The list is saved every time it changes, so you never need to press a save button. If you close the page and come back later, or reload it, or even restart your browser, your list is still there, because it is saved in your browser's own storage on your device.

The due date is optional. Many tasks do not have a real deadline, and giving every task a date just adds pressure. Use the date only when the task truly has one: a bill to pay by Friday, a form to send by Monday. The priority is optional too, in the same way. Most tasks are medium priority. Use high priority sparingly, for the one or two tasks that really matter today, and the list stays honest.

The three-task rule

Most to-do lists fail because they are too long. A list of forty tasks is not a plan, it is a source of guilt. You look at it in the morning, feel defeated, and do none of it. The fix is simple: never put more than three big tasks on a day's list. Three big tasks is a day you can actually finish. Everything else is a bonus.

The three-task rule works because it forces a choice. If you can only pick three, you pick the three that matter most, and the rest waits. That choice is the real work of a to-do list. Writing down forty tasks is easy. Choosing the three that matter is hard, and it is the part that makes the day work. Use the high priority setting for your three, and leave the rest at medium or low.

On a day when you finish your three big tasks early, you can add more. There is no rule against doing a fourth task. The rule is only about what you promise yourself in the morning. Promise three, do three, and the day is a success. Promise forty, do seven, and the day feels like a failure even though you did seven real things. The list should make you feel good at the end of the day, not bad.

What makes a task doable

A doable task has a verb and a clear finish. Call the bank is doable: you know what to do and you know when it is done. Sort out the bank is not doable, because it could mean ten different things and you will never know when it is finished. The same goes for work on the report, which has no finish line, versus finish the first draft of the report, which does. When you write a task, ask yourself one question: will I know when this is done. If the answer is no, rewrite it until the answer is yes.

Keep tasks small enough to finish in one sitting. A task that takes three hours will be put off all day. A task that takes twenty minutes gets done. If a job is big, split it into smaller tasks, each with its own verb and finish: write the outline, write the first page, check the numbers, send the draft. Four small tasks are easier to start than one huge one, and every check mark gives you a small push to do the next one.

Write tasks in your own words, the way you would say them to a friend. A list written in your own words is a list you will actually read. A list written in formal language feels like homework. The app does not care how you word your tasks. It only shows them back to you, in the order you added them, until you check them off.

Worked example: one day, done well

Suppose it is Monday morning and you have a busy day ahead. You open this page and add three big tasks: finish the monthly report, call the landlord about the leak, and buy food for the week. You mark the report as high priority with a due date of today, the landlord call as high priority with no date, and the food shopping as medium priority. Then you add two small tasks: water the plants and reply to the message from your sister. Five tasks, three big and two small, and the list feels manageable.

By midday you have finished the report and called the landlord. You check both off. The done-today count shows two, and the list shows three tasks left. After lunch you buy the food and check that off too. In the evening you water the plants and reply to the message. Five tasks, all checked off, and the done-today count shows five. You tap Clear completed tasks, and the list is empty for tomorrow.

If you had only finished three of the five, that would still be a good day, because the three big tasks were the ones that mattered. The two small tasks would simply wait until tomorrow, still on the list, because the list is saved on your device. Nothing is lost when a task rolls over to the next day. The list is patient. It waits for you.

Your list stays on your device

Your tasks are saved in your browser's own storage, on your device, under the name this page uses for its list. Nothing is sent to a server, nothing is sent to anyone, and there is no account to sign into. That is why the app is free and why it asks for nothing: there is no service behind it, just a page that remembers your list in your own browser.

This has two consequences worth knowing. The first is privacy. Nobody can see your tasks except someone holding your device, because the tasks never leave it. The second is that the list does not sync. A task you add on your phone will not appear on your computer, and a task you add on your computer will not appear on your phone. Each device has its own list. If you use the app on two devices, you will have two lists, and that is normal.

It also means that clearing your browser data will erase the list. If your browser is set to clear its data when you close it, or if you clear the data yourself, the tasks go with it. The warn box at the top of this page says so plainly. If your list holds something important, such as a task with a date you must not miss, keep a copy of it somewhere else too, such as a note on paper or a message to yourself.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is a forty-item list. A list that long is not a plan, it is a pile. Keep the day's list short, use the three-task rule for the big jobs, and let the small jobs fill the gaps. The second mistake is tasks with no verb. Bank, report, and shop are not tasks, they are topics. Call the bank, finish the report, and buy bread are tasks, because each one tells you what to do.

The third mistake is never checking anything off. Some people add tasks and never tap the box, because the task was never quite finished, or because they forgot. A task that is done should be checked off at once. The check mark is the reward, and the done-today count is the proof that the day was not wasted. If a task is truly finished, tap the box and enjoy it.

The fourth mistake is treating the list as a memory instead of a plan. A to-do list is not a place to store every idea you ever had. It is a place for today's work. If an idea is not for today, write it somewhere else and add it to the list on the day you plan to do it. A list full of someday tasks hides the tasks that matter today.

What it cannot do

This app is a simple list. It cannot remind you when a task is due, because a web page cannot send you a notification unless you give it permission, and this page never asks. It cannot sync between your devices, because it has no account and no server. It cannot sort your tasks by priority or by date on its own: the tasks stay in the order you added them, and you choose what to do next.

It also cannot make you do the tasks. A list is a tool, not a manager. It shows you what you promised yourself, it keeps the promise safe on your device, and it counts what you finished. The doing is still yours. But a short list, written in your own words, checked off one task at a time, is one of the simplest tools there is, and it works because it is simple.

Questions people ask

Is this to-do list app free?

Yes. The app is free to use, with no signup, no account and no limit on how many tasks you add. There is nothing to buy and nothing to subscribe to.

Do I need an account?

No. There is no account and no sign-in of any kind. Your list is saved in your browser on your own device, so the app does not need to know who you are.

Where are my tasks saved?

Your tasks are saved in your browser's own storage on your device. Nothing is sent to a server and nothing leaves your device. Only someone holding your device can see your list.

Will my tasks still be there tomorrow?

Yes, as long as you use the same browser on the same device and you do not clear your browser data. The list is saved every time it changes, so it survives closing the page and restarting the browser.

Can I use it on my phone?

Yes. The app works on a phone, on a tablet and on a desktop computer. On a phone, tap the boxes with your finger and tap the buttons to add, check off, edit and delete tasks.

What happens if I clear my browser data?

Clearing your browser data erases the list, because the tasks are stored in your browser. If your list holds something important, keep a copy of it somewhere else too, such as a note on paper.
