How the planner works

Add your stops one at a time, in the order you plan to visit them. For each stop, enter its name, the distance to it in kilometres, and how many minutes you expect to spend there unloading and doing paperwork. The first stop's distance is measured from your base; every later stop's distance is measured from the previous stop.

Enter your average driving speed as well. The planner divides the total distance by that speed to work out the driving time, adds the time at each stop, and gives you the total for the whole round. You can move stops up and down to change the order, remove a stop, and copy or download the finished checklist.

Your route is saved in this browser only, so it is still there when you come back to the page. Nothing is sent anywhere and there is no account. That also means the route lives on one device: if you plan on a computer and drive with a phone, copy or download the checklist and take it with you.

Ordering your stops

The order of the stops is the route, and a good order saves more time than a fast van. The simplest rule is geography: visit stops in the order they lie along the road, so the route flows in one direction instead of zigzagging back across ground you have already covered. A route that doubles back on itself wastes the very time the planner is trying to save.

Time windows come next. If a customer can only receive goods before noon, that stop goes early even if it is not the nearest. If another customer is only open in the afternoon, that stop goes late. Put the constrained stops in place first, then fill the gaps with the flexible ones.

Think about the load as well. Heavy goods are easier to unload if they are loaded last and therefore nearest the door, which usually means delivering them first. Fragile goods travel better when they are loaded last and delivered first, so they spend the least time on the move. The planner keeps the order you choose — it does not reorder for you, because only you know these constraints.

When two stops are close together, visit them one after the other even if that breaks a neat geographic line. A short hop between neighbours is almost always cheaper than returning to the same street later in the day. The planner's move buttons make this easy: add the stops in any order, then drag the neighbours together with Up and Down until the route reads sensibly from top to bottom.

Worked example: four stops, 31 kilometres

Take a round with four stops. The first is 5 kilometres from base with 10 minutes at the stop. The second is 8 kilometres further on with 15 minutes there. The third is 12 kilometres further with 10 minutes there. The fourth is 6 kilometres further with 20 minutes there. The average driving speed is 40 kilometres per hour.

The total distance is 5 plus 8 plus 12 plus 6, which is 31 kilometres. Driving time is 31 divided by 40, which is 0.775 hours, or about 47 minutes. Time at the stops is 10 plus 15 plus 10 plus 20, which is 55 minutes. The whole round is about 47 minutes of driving plus 55 minutes at stops, which is about 1 hour 42 minutes.

Notice that the time at the stops is longer than the driving time. That is normal for delivery rounds with several drops, and it is why the planner asks for stop time separately. A plan that counts only driving time will always run late, because the unloading, the signatures and the waiting are where the day actually goes.

How long does a stop really take?

Stop time is the number most people get wrong, and almost always in the same direction: they enter the time the unloading takes and forget everything around it. Finding parking, walking to the door, waiting for someone to answer, getting a signature and walking back all take minutes, and on a busy site they can take longer than the unloading itself.

The honest way to set the number is to time a few real stops. For a simple drop at a house, 10 minutes is often realistic. For a business delivery with paperwork, 15 to 20 minutes is common. For a site where you must wait for a forklift or a goods-in bay, allow 30 minutes or more. Enter the number your experience supports, not the number you wish were true.

Remember that stop time also varies by time of day. A delivery at opening time may wait for the shutters; a delivery at lunch may wait for staff to return. If you know a stop is slow at a certain hour, either allow extra minutes or move it to a better time in the order.

Planning for the day, not just the route

The route total is only part of the day. Loading the vehicle at the start takes time, and so does unloading anything that comes back, refuelling, and the drive back to base at the end — which the planner does not count unless you add it as a stop. Add those to the route total before you promise a finish time.

Build in a buffer. Traffic, roadworks, a customer who is not ready and a wrong turn all happen, and a plan with no slack turns the first delay into a late finish for every stop after it. A buffer of 10 to 15 percent on the whole round is a sensible habit: on a two-hour round, that is 12 to 18 minutes of room.

Finally, think about the driver's day as well as the route. Breaks, meals and the legal limits on driving time all belong in the plan. A route that fits the clock but leaves no time for a break is a route that will be driven tired, and tired driving is the most expensive delay of all.

It also helps to plan the start of the day, not just the route itself. Loading the vehicle in delivery order — last stop loaded first, first stop loaded last — means the driver never has to climb over parcels to reach the next drop. A few minutes spent loading in the right order saves many more minutes at every stop, and it is the cheapest time saving on the whole round.

Printing and sharing the checklist

The checklist the planner produces is deliberately plain text: one numbered line per stop, then the totals. Copy it to the clipboard and paste it into a message, a note or a document, or download it as a text file and print it. A printed checklist on the dashboard is still the most reliable navigation aid, because it never loses signal.

If someone else is driving, the checklist is also the handover. It shows the order, the distances and the stop times, so the driver can see the shape of the day before setting out. Add anything the checklist does not cover — gate codes, contact names, special instructions — in the message that carries it, because the planner only holds what you entered.

Keep a copy of the finished checklist after the round. Comparing the planned times with the actual times, stop by stop, is how your estimates get better. If one stop always takes twice as long as planned, the next route should say so.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is guessing distances instead of measuring them. A distance that feels like 5 kilometres is often 8, and the error multiplies across ten stops. Check distances on a map or from the vehicle's trip meter before you enter them.

The second mistake is forgetting stop time, or entering a hopeful number. Time the stops, use the real figures, and remember that the first stop of the day is usually the slowest while everything is being set up.

The third mistake is packing in too many stops. Ten stops is the planner's maximum, not a target. A round with fewer stops, each given enough time, finishes on schedule more often than a round that is full to the brim and late by the third stop.

Questions people ask

Does the planner find the shortest route?

No. It keeps the order you enter and adds up the distance and time for that order. It has no maps and no live traffic, so it cannot suggest a better order or check road conditions. You choose the order; the planner does the arithmetic.

How many stops can I add?

Up to 10 stops per route. If your round has more than 10 drops, split it into two routes — for example, a morning route and an afternoon route — and plan each one separately.

Does it include the drive back to base?

Only if you add it as a stop. The totals cover the stops you enter, starting from base and ending at the last stop. Add a final stop for the return leg if you want the drive home counted.

What average speed should I enter?

Your own typical speed for the area you drive in. City driving with traffic lights is much slower than open road driving, so a round through town needs a lower speed than a round between villages. Check a past round if you are unsure.

Is my route saved anywhere?

Only in your own browser, using its local storage. Nothing is sent to any server and there is no account. Clearing the browser's stored data will remove the route, so copy or download the checklist if you need it elsewhere.

Can I print the checklist?

Yes. Copy it to the clipboard and paste it into a document, or download it as a text file and print that. The checklist is plain text, one numbered line per stop, followed by the totals.
