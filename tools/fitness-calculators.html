What these numbers are

Fitness numbers look precise, and that is part of their appeal: you type in your height and weight, and a single number comes back, clean and certain. But every number on this page is an estimate from a formula built on averages, describing a typical body, not yours in particular. Used well, the numbers give you a starting point, a way to compare one month with the next, and a reason to ask better questions about your health. Used badly, they become a score you chase, and chasing a score is a poor way to look after yourself. Read the explanations before you trust the numbers: a result you understand is worth far more than one you only believe.

What BMI means in plain words

BMI stands for body mass index. It is your weight in kilograms divided by your height in metres, squared. The result is a single number, and the usual ranges are simple: below 18.5 is called underweight, from 18.5 to 24.9 is called the healthy range, from 25 to 29.9 is called overweight, and 30 and above is called obese. Those words sound like verdicts, but they are only labels for ranges drawn from large groups of people, not from any one person.

BMI is popular because it needs only two numbers and it works reasonably well across a whole population. Doctors and researchers use it to spot patterns and to decide who might need a closer look. But it has a well known blind spot: it cannot tell muscle from fat. A rugby player with a lot of muscle can score as overweight while carrying very little body fat, and an older person can score as healthy while carrying more fat than is good for them. Height, age, sex and build all shift what a number means, so treat your BMI as a rough guide and a prompt for a conversation, not as a grade. A number outside the healthy range is worth raising with a doctor, and a number inside it is reassuring but not a clean bill of health on its own.

What BMR means

BMR stands for basal metabolic rate. It is the number of calories your body burns in a day if you do nothing at all but lie still and stay alive. Breathing, keeping your heart beating, holding your body temperature steady and repairing your cells all cost energy, and BMR is the price of that quiet work. The calculator on this page uses the Mifflin-St Jeor formula, which takes your sex, weight, height and age and turns them into a daily figure. It is the formula most dietitians reach for first, because it matches measured results better than the older formulas it replaced. But it is still an estimate.

Two people of the same weight and height can burn quite different amounts at rest, because muscle burns more energy than fat and the formula cannot see how much of each you carry. BMR also drifts down with age, which is one reason the same meals can slowly add weight over the years. Your BMR is not a target to eat up to, and it is not the calories you need in a day. It is the floor beneath your daily needs, the part that stays roughly the same whatever you do. Everything else you burn comes from movement, which is where the next number comes in.

Daily calories

Your daily calorie needs start with your BMR and add everything else you do. The usual way to do this is to multiply your BMR by an activity factor: 1.2 if you are sedentary, 1.375 if you are lightly active, 1.55 if you are moderately active, 1.725 if you are very active, and 1.9 if you are extra active. The calculator on this page does exactly that. Pick the level that honestly describes your week, not the week you wish you had. Most desk workers who exercise a couple of times a week are lightly active, not moderately active, and choosing a level too high is the most common reason the answer comes out too generous.

The result is an estimate of the calories that would keep your weight steady. Eat more than that, day after day, and weight tends to rise. Eat less, and it tends to fall. But the body is not a simple ledger: appetite, sleep, stress and the kind of food you eat all change how the numbers play out. A calorie target is a guide for planning meals, not a promise. If you want to change your weight, use the estimate as a starting point, watch what actually happens over a few weeks, and adjust from there. And if you have a health condition, or you are aiming for a big change, talk to a doctor or a dietitian before you set a target.

Body fat

Body fat is the number most people actually want, and it is the hardest to get from a calculator. BMI tells you about weight for your height. It says nothing about how much of that weight is fat and how much is muscle, bone and water. Some websites offer a body fat calculator that asks for your waist, neck and hips and gives a percentage. Those formulas can give a rough idea, but they are much less reliable than the BMI and BMR formulas here: a tape measure reading can shift by a centimetre depending on where you place it, and a small error in the measurement turns into a large error in the answer.

The accurate ways to measure body fat need equipment: a DEXA scan, a Bod Pod, or weighing underwater. Smart scales that claim to measure body fat use a small electric current and a guess, and their readings can swing by several percentage points from one day to the next. That is why this page does not pretend to calculate your body fat from a few typed numbers. Instead, track the numbers you can measure reliably: your weight over weeks, your waist size, and how your clothes fit. For a real body fat figure, a gym or a clinic with proper equipment is the honest route. A rough guess dressed up as a precise percentage helps nobody.

Common mistakes

Three mistakes turn up again and again with fitness calculators. The first is chasing one number. A BMI of 24.9 is not meaningfully healthier than a BMI of 25.1, but the label changes, and people chase the label. Health is a wide picture — how you sleep, how you move, what you eat, how you feel — and no single number captures it. A calculator should never decide how you feel about yourself.

The second is weighing yourself five times a day. Your weight changes through the day as you eat, drink and move, and those swings say nothing about your health. Weigh yourself at the same time, in the same conditions, a few times a week at most, and look at the trend over weeks. The third is trusting a calculator over a doctor. A calculator knows four numbers about you; a doctor can see the rest — your history, your medicines, your family, the things a form cannot ask. If a calculator and a doctor disagree, the doctor is right. Calculators are good servants and bad masters: use them to learn and to track, and let a person who knows you make the decisions.

What it cannot do

These are estimates from standard formulas, not medical advice. See a doctor for health decisions. That line matters, so it is worth saying what sits behind it. No calculator on this page can diagnose anything, tell you what you should weigh, or set a safe target for you. The formulas were built from groups of people, and they do not know your history, your medicines, your build, or anything else that makes your body different from the average. They cannot tell whether a number is a problem for you, what to do about it, or replace the judgement of a doctor or a dietitian, who can look at the whole picture and give advice fitted to you. Use this page to understand the numbers, track them over time, and prepare better questions for a professional. That is what it is for, and that is enough.

Questions people ask

What is a healthy BMI?

A BMI between 18.5 and 24.9 is usually called the healthy range. But BMI is only a rough guide, because it cannot tell muscle from fat, so a number outside the range is a reason to talk to a doctor, not a verdict on its own.

Is my BMR the same as the calories I burn in a day?

No. Your BMR is the calories your body burns at rest, doing nothing at all. Your total daily needs are higher, because they add everything you do on top of rest, which is why the daily calorie calculator multiplies your BMR by an activity factor.

How many calories should I eat each day?

It depends on your age, sex, size and activity level, and the calculator on this page gives an estimate based on those numbers. Use it as a starting point, watch what happens over a few weeks, and talk to a doctor or a dietitian if you want a personal target.

Can a calculator measure my body fat?

Not well. Simple calculators can only guess from measurements like height, weight and waist size, and small errors in those measurements turn into large errors in the answer. Accurate body fat measurement needs proper equipment, such as a DEXA scan at a gym or clinic.

Why does my weight change from day to day?

Mostly water, food and the time of day. What you ate and drank, how much you moved, and even the weather can shift the number on the scale by a kilogram or more. Weigh yourself at the same time and in the same conditions, and judge the trend over weeks, not a single morning.

Are these calculators medical advice?

No. These are estimates from standard formulas, not medical advice. See a doctor for health decisions.
