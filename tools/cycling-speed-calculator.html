Cycling Speed Calculator

Work out your cycling speed from distance and time, or turn a speed into a time or a distance for any ride you plan. Answers come in km/h and mph, with pace per kilometre and per mile.

This calculator works with steady averages: it divides distance by moving time and knows nothing about hills, wind, traffic lights or stops. Real rides are slower than the number here whenever you stop, climb or ride into the wind — plan extra time for all three.

What this calculator does

This calculator answers three simple questions about cycling. How fast did you ride? How long will a ride take? How far will you get in the time you have? It works from the same relationship every time: speed equals distance divided by time. Give it any two of the three numbers and it works out the third, in kilometres per hour and miles per hour, plus your pace per kilometre and per mile.

Pace is the same information seen from the other side. Speed says how far you go in an hour; pace says how long one kilometre takes. Riders planning a long day often find pace easier to think with: if you know you ride at 3 minutes per kilometre, a 30 kilometre ride takes about 90 minutes, and you can feel straight away whether that fits the time you have. The calculator shows both, so you can use whichever makes more sense to you.

Everything here is an average over the whole ride. Nobody rides at exactly one speed from start to finish: you go faster on the flat, slower up hills, and you stop at junctions. The average smooths all of that into one number, which is exactly what you need for planning, and exactly what you should not confuse with the fastest speed your speedometer showed on a downhill stretch.

Worked examples: commute, weekend ride, race pace

A commute: Rina rides 8 kilometres to work in 30 minutes. Her speed is 8 divided by half an hour, which is 16 km/h, or about 9.9 mph. Her pace is 30 minutes divided by 8 kilometres, which is 3 minutes 45 seconds per kilometre. That is a very ordinary commuting speed, and it is a good number to plan with: if she moves house to 12 kilometres away, the same pace gives her a 45-minute ride.

A weekend ride: Tom plans a 40 kilometre ride and expects to average 20 km/h, a comfortable pace for a regular rider on fairly flat roads. The time is 40 divided by 20, which is 2 hours. His pace is 3 minutes per kilometre. If he wants to be home by noon and plans a 20-minute cafe stop, he should leave by 9:40 at the latest — the calculator gives moving time only, so stops always go on top.

A race pace: a strong rider covering 40 kilometres in exactly 1 hour averages 40 km/h, which is 24.9 mph, at a pace of 1 minute 30 seconds per kilometre. That is fast riding, the sort of speed seen in time trials on flat roads, and it shows how much speed matters: doubling your speed halves your time, but doubling your speed takes far more than double the effort, because air resistance grows quickly as you go faster.

What speed is realistic for you?

Beginners on ordinary bikes on flat ground typically average about 12 to 16 km/h over a whole ride, including the slow bits. That is a perfectly respectable speed: it is roughly three times walking pace, and it gets you across a city faster than a bus in traffic. A regular rider who cycles a few times a week typically averages 20 to 25 km/h on the flat, and a strong club rider may average 30 km/h or more on a fast group ride. These are broad typical ranges, not guarantees, and hills change everything.

Your bike matters too. A road bike with narrow tyres and a riding position that keeps you low will be several km/h faster than a heavy city bike with wide tyres at the same effort, simply because it wastes less energy on air resistance and rolling resistance. An electric bike changes the picture again, though its assistance usually stops above a set speed. What matters for planning is your own average on your own bike on your own roads, measured over real rides rather than guessed from what other people claim.

The honest way to find your number is to ride a known distance a few times and time yourself, then use the average of those rides. A single fast ride with a tailwind is not your average, and neither is a single slow ride into a headwind. Three or four rides give you a number you can trust, and this calculator turns that number into a time for any distance you care to plan.

Why your average is lower than your top speed

Many riders are puzzled the first time they compare their average speed with the fastest speed they saw on the ride. The gap is normal and it has a simple cause: you spend most of a ride below your top speed. You accelerate away from junctions, climb hills slowly, slow down for corners and traffic, and stop altogether at lights. The fast downhill minutes are real, but they are a small part of the ride, and they cannot make up for all the slow minutes.

Stops hurt more than people expect. Five minutes stopped in an hour-long ride costs about 8 percent of your average speed, because the clock keeps running while the wheels do not. That is why a ride through town with twenty traffic lights averages far less than the same distance on an open road, even when you ride just as hard. If you want a fair comparison between rides, compare moving time with moving time, or total time with total time, and do not mix the two.

Hills work the same way. You might climb at 8 km/h and descend at 45 km/h, and it feels as if the two should average out to about 26 km/h. They do not, because you spend far longer climbing than descending: the climb takes most of the time, so the slow speed dominates the average. On a hilly route the average speed is always closer to the climbing speed than to the descending speed, which is why hilly rides feel so much slower than their distance suggests.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is forgetting stops. People plan a 2-hour ride, then add a cafe stop, a photo stop and a stop to fix a puncture, and wonder why they are an hour late. Moving time is not total time: always add stops on top of the calculator's answer. The second mistake is mixing up units — entering miles as kilometres, or reading mph as km/h. A 20 mph average is 32 km/h, a big difference, and the calculator shows both units precisely so you can check which one you are looking at.

A third mistake is planning from a top speed instead of an average. If the fastest you have ever gone is 35 km/h, planning a 70 kilometre ride at that speed gives 2 hours, but your real average might be 22 km/h, which makes it over 3 hours. Plan from your average, measured over real rides. A fourth mistake is ignoring the wind: a headwind on the way out is not cancelled by a tailwind on the way back, because you spend longer riding slowly into the wind than you spend riding fast with it behind you.

Planning time for a long ride

For a long ride, start with your honest average speed on similar roads, work out the moving time, and then add everything else. Add 10 minutes for every hour of riding as a rough allowance for short stops, junctions and faffing about, and add the full length of any planned cafe or lunch stop. If the route is hilly, knock a few km/h off your flat-road average before you start, or the plan will fall apart at the first long climb.

It also helps to plan in chunks. A 100 kilometre ride at 20 km/h is 5 hours of moving time, which sounds manageable until you add stops and realise it is a 6-hour day. Breaking it into four 25 kilometre chunks, each about 75 minutes of riding with a short break between them, makes the day feel achievable and gives you natural points to check whether you are on schedule. If you are behind at the second checkpoint, you still have time to shorten the route; if you only discover it at 80 kilometres, you do not.

Finally, leave a margin. A plan that only works if everything goes perfectly is not a plan, it is a hope. Aim to finish with half an hour to spare, carry a little more food and water than you think you need, and remember that the last quarter of a long ride is almost always slower than the first three quarters. The calculator gives you the arithmetic; the margin is what turns arithmetic into a good day out.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator knows nothing about your route. It cannot see hills, wind, road surfaces, traffic or your fitness, and it treats every kilometre as equal, which no real road is. Two rides of the same distance at the same average speed can feel completely different if one is flat and the other climbs the whole way. Use the numbers as a planning aid, and adjust them with what you know about the route: slower for hills and headwinds, faster for flat roads and tailwinds.

It also cannot measure your speed for you. For that you need a bike computer, a sports watch or a phone app that records your ride. What this calculator does well is the arithmetic around a number you already have: turning a distance and a time into a speed, or a speed and a distance into a time, in both units, with pace included. That is all it claims to do, and for planning rides it is usually all you need.

Questions people ask

What is a good average cycling speed?

For a beginner on ordinary roads, 12 to 16 km/h is typical. A regular rider who cycles a few times a week often averages 20 to 25 km/h on the flat, and a strong club rider may average 30 km/h or more. Hills, wind and traffic all pull the average down, so compare your own rides on similar roads rather than chasing someone else's number.

How do I convert km/h to mph?

Multiply km/h by 0.621 to get mph, or divide mph by 0.621 to get km/h. So 20 km/h is about 12.4 mph, and 20 mph is about 32.2 km/h. This calculator shows both units in every answer, so you never have to convert by hand.

Why is my speed lower on hills?

Climbing takes far more effort than riding on the flat, so you go slower uphill, and you spend much longer climbing than descending, so the slow climbing speed dominates your average. A route with hills will always average less than the same distance on flat roads, even at the same effort.

Does wind change the result?

Yes, a lot. A headwind slows you down more than a tailwind of the same strength speeds you up, because you spend longer riding slowly into the wind. This calculator works with the average speed you enter, so if you expect wind, enter a lower speed than your calm-day average.

How do I plan time for a long ride?

Work out the moving time from your honest average speed, then add stops on top: about 10 minutes per hour of riding for short stops, plus the full length of any cafe or lunch stop. Break the ride into chunks with checkpoints, and leave half an hour of spare time so a puncture or a headwind does not ruin the day.

What speed should a beginner expect?

About 12 to 16 km/h over a whole ride on fairly flat ground, including the slow bits at junctions and on small rises. That is roughly three times walking pace and plenty fast enough to get across a city. Speed grows naturally as fitness and confidence grow; there is no need to chase a number in the first weeks.
