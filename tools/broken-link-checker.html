Why links break

Links break for ordinary reasons, and most of them come down to change. A page gets moved to a new address, and the old links still point at the old one. A file gets deleted, or renamed, and every link to it stops working. A page gets taken down after an event, a sale, or a season ends, and the links from older pages are forgotten. None of this is careless on its own. It becomes a problem when nobody checks.

Typing errors are the second cause. One wrong letter in an address is enough. A link typed by hand, copied from a message, or pasted with a missing character will fail every time, and it will keep failing until someone notices. Long addresses with many parts are the easiest to get wrong, and they are also the hardest to spot by reading.

The third cause is change on the other side. A site you link to moves its pages, closes down, or changes its addresses, and your link breaks even though you changed nothing. That is why links to other sites need checking too, even if this page cannot test them directly. A link is a promise that something is at the other end, and promises go stale.

A fourth cause is easy to miss: capital letters. On many servers, an address with a capital letter is a different address from the same words in small letters, so a link to Page.html will fail if the file is really called page.html. The same goes for a missing or extra slash at the end of an address, and for spaces that were never meant to be there. When a link fails and the page seems to exist, compare the address letter by letter. The difference is often a single capital, a missing slash, or a space that crept in when the link was copied.

Fixing a broken link

Fixing a broken link starts with one question: where should this link point to now? If the page moved, the answer is the new address. Fix the link to point there, and the job is done. If many links point to the same moved page, a redirect is the better fix. A redirect sends anyone who follows the old address to the new one automatically, so every old link keeps working without being edited one by one.

If the page is gone for good, you have two honest choices. Remove the link, so visitors never follow it to a dead end, or point it at the closest page that still exists and still helps the reader. Pointing a dead link at the homepage is a weak fix. It leaves the visitor on a page they did not ask for, with no sign of what they wanted. A link to the nearest real page, or no link at all, treats the reader better.

After a fix, test the link again. A fix that is not tested is a guess. Run this checker once more, or open the link yourself and see that the right page appears. If you run a site with many pages, keep a short list of the links you fixed and the dates you fixed them. The next time a link breaks, the list will show whether the same pages keep moving, which is a sign that the site structure needs a steadier plan.

How often to check

Check a small site every few months. That is often enough to catch the links that broke quietly while nobody was looking. Check again after any big change: a redesign, a move to a new host, a change of address structure, or a round of deleting old pages. Those are the moments when links break in groups, and a check right after the change catches them while the work is still fresh in your mind.

A site that changes often needs checking more often. If you publish new pages every week, a monthly check is a sensible habit. If your site rarely changes, twice a year may be enough. The right rhythm is the one you will actually keep. A check you do is worth more than a perfect schedule you ignore.

Pay extra attention after moving a site. Moves break links more than any other single event, because addresses change in bulk and some always get missed. After a move, check the pages that matter most first: the homepage, the most visited pages, and any page that other sites link to. Then work through the rest in order.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is checking only the homepage. The homepage is usually the best-kept page on a site, so it tells you the least. Broken links hide in old posts, old pages, and the pages nobody visits any more. Check the whole site, a section at a time, not just the front door.

The second mistake is ignoring image links. An image that fails to load leaves a gap in the page and a visitor who sees nothing where a picture should be. Images break the same way links break: the file moves, gets renamed, or gets deleted. When you check a page, look at the images too, and check that each one loads.

The third mistake is fixing the link but not the cause. If links keep breaking because pages keep moving, the fix is a steadier address plan, not another round of edits. Pick addresses that will not need to change, and keep them. The fourth mistake is never checking at all, because the site looks fine from the front. A site can look fine and still be full of dead ends that visitors hit every day.

What it cannot do

This tool checks the links on the page you are viewing and same-site links you paste. It cannot crawl a whole website or test other sites' links.

That is a real limit, not a missing feature. Browser rules stop a page like this one from reaching out to other sites, and a page that runs in your browser cannot walk through a whole website on its own. Any page that claims to crawl a full site from a typed domain is guessing or pretending. The honest way to check a whole site is to use a crawler that runs the job from its own software.

For a full crawl of a whole site, use the official Screaming Frog tool at screamingfrog.co.uk. It walks through every page on a site and reports every broken link it finds. Use it for the full-site check, and use this page for a quick check of the page you are on and the links you paste.

Questions people ask

Can this tool crawl my whole website?

No. This tool checks the links on the page you are viewing and same-site links you paste. It cannot crawl a whole website, and it never shows a fake crawl report for a domain you type in. For a full crawl, use a dedicated crawler such as Screaming Frog.

Can it test links to other websites?

No. Browser rules stop a page like this one from testing links that point to other sites. Those links are listed as could not test, and they are never claimed as working. Only links that belong to the same site as this page can be tested here.

What counts as a broken link?

A link is broken when the page it points to does not exist or cannot be reached. The usual sign is a 404 page not found error, but a page that never answers or a server error counts as broken too. This tool reports the status it sees for each link it tests.

Why do links break?

Pages get moved or deleted, files get renamed, and addresses get typed wrong. A link that worked for years can break in a day when a page moves without a redirect. Old posts and old pages are where broken links hide most often.

How do I fix a broken link?

First decide what the link should point to now. If the page moved, fix the link to the new address or set up a redirect from the old one. If the page is gone for good, remove the link or point it at the closest page that still exists.

How often should I check my links?

Check a small site every few months, and check again after any big change such as a redesign, a move to a new host, or deleting pages. A site that changes often needs checking more often. A quick check after every change catches problems while they are still easy to fix.
