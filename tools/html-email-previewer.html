    How to use this previewer

    Paste your email's full HTML into the box and tap Preview email. The rendered version appears in the sandboxed frame below, the plain-text version appears underneath it, and the checklist lists anything that needs fixing: images without alt text, images without a width, links with no text, and tables wider than most inboxes show well. Nothing is sent anywhere and nothing is uploaded — the whole preview runs in your browser, on your own device.

    The Load a sample email button fills the box with a small newsletter you can preview straight away: a heading, a short message, an image, a button and a footer. It is a good way to learn what the checklist looks for, because the sample is deliberately clean — it should pass every check. Try breaking it on purpose: delete the alt text from the image, or remove the width from the table, and watch the checklist catch it. That is how you learn to trust the checks before you run your own email through them.

    Use the plain-text version as a second test. Many readers open email with images off, and some read only the plain-text part, so the plain-text version should still make sense on its own: the heading, the message and the link text should all be there, in the right order. If the plain-text version is empty or garbled, the email's HTML structure needs work before the design does.

    Why email looks different in every inbox

    Email is the oldest part of the web still in daily use, and every inbox renders it with its own rules. Gmail strips some styles and keeps others. Outlook on Windows uses a word-processor engine to draw email, which is why it handles spacing and images differently from every other inbox. Apple Mail is the most forgiving, and webmail in a browser sits somewhere in between. A design that looks perfect in one inbox can look broken in another, and the only way to be sure is to test in the inboxes your readers actually use.

    That is why email is still built with tables, even though the rest of the web moved on years ago. Tables are the one layout method every inbox understands, including the oldest ones. A single-column table 600 pixels wide is the standard email width: it fits the preview pane of most desktop inboxes and scales down to a phone without breaking. Wider tables get cut off or squeezed, which is why this previewer warns you about any table wider than 600 pixels.

    Styles need the same care. The safest styles are written inline, on each element, because some inboxes strip the style block from the head of the email entirely. Web fonts, background images, animated images and fancy positioning all fail somewhere, so the reliable email uses system fonts, plain background colours, and images that carry their meaning in alt text in case they never load. Boring email is reliable email — and reliable email gets read.

    The checklist, explained

    Missing alt text is the most common problem the checklist finds. When images are blocked — which is the default in many inboxes — the reader sees the alt text instead of the picture. An image with no alt text shows as an empty box, and the reader never knows what was there. Every image that carries meaning needs alt text that says what it shows: a product photo needs the product name, and a button image needs the button's words. Decorative images that add nothing can carry empty alt text, but they should still have the attribute.

    Images without a width are the second check. An image with no width set will show at its full size, which can push the email wider than the inbox and break the layout — and in Outlook it can also leave a large gap while the image loads. Setting the width on the image tag itself, not just in a style rule, is the reliable fix, because some inboxes ignore the style and read only the attribute. The height should be set too, or left to scale with the width.

    Links without text are the third check. A link that wraps only an image, with no words inside it, is invisible when images are off — the reader sees nothing to click. Every link should contain words that make sense on their own: Shop the sale beats Click here, because it says where the link goes. Very wide tables are the fourth check: any table wider than 600 pixels will be cut off or squeezed in most inboxes, so the width should come down or the table should be rebuilt as a single column.

    Tables or divs, images, and width

    Use tables for layout in email, even if you would never use them on a web page. A table with one column and a width of 600 pixels is the standard email body, and nested tables make the columns inside it. Divs and modern layout methods fail in Outlook and in some webmail, while tables work everywhere. It feels old-fashioned because it is old-fashioned — email never got the layout upgrade the web did, and building for the lowest common inbox is what makes an email arrive looking right.

    Images are the part most senders get wrong. Keep them small in file size so the email loads fast on a phone, give every image a width and alt text, and never put the whole message in one big image — when images are blocked, the reader sees nothing at all. A good rule is that the email should still make sense with every image removed: the heading, the offer and the link should all be real text, with images adding to the message rather than carrying it.

    Six hundred pixels is the width to aim for. It fits the reading pane of desktop inboxes, it scales down cleanly to a phone, and it is the width most email templates use. Wider emails get cut off on the right or squeezed until the text is unreadable, and narrower emails waste space on desktop. On a phone the email will scale to the screen width, so text should be at least 14 pixels — smaller text becomes unreadable when the whole 600 pixel email is squeezed onto a 360 pixel screen.

    Common mistakes

    The most common mistake is sending without a plain-text version. Many senders write only the HTML and forget that some readers — and some spam filters — look at the plain text. This previewer builds a plain-text version from your HTML so you can check it, but a real send should include a proper plain-text part written for readers, not just the HTML with the tags stripped. If your sending tool builds one automatically, check it before you send.

    The second mistake is testing in only one inbox. An email that looks right in Gmail can fall apart in Outlook, and an email that looks right on desktop can be unreadable on a phone. Send a test to yourself and open it in at least two inboxes — one on a phone and one on a desktop — before the real send. The preview here catches the structural mistakes; only a real inbox shows you the rendering mistakes.

    The third mistake is forgetting the subject line and the preview text. The subject decides whether the email is opened at all, and the preview text — the first line of the email, shown next to the subject in the inbox list — decides it almost as much. Keep the subject short and honest, and make the first line of the email a real sentence, not a navigation row or an image description. A beautiful email that nobody opens is a wasted email.

    What this previewer cannot do

    This previewer shows a standard render of your email — it cannot show you exactly what Gmail, Outlook and Apple Mail will each do with it, because each inbox has its own rendering rules and its own quirks. It cannot send a test email, it cannot show you the email in dark mode, and it cannot check whether your email will land in the spam folder. Those checks need a real send to real inboxes, and there is no substitute for sending yourself a test before the real one.

    It also cannot check your links, your sending reputation, or whether your images will load from your server — it only reads the HTML you paste. Use it to catch the structural mistakes early, while the email is still easy to fix, and then finish with a real test send to the inboxes your readers use. The previewer is the first check, not the only one.

    Questions people ask

    Why does my email look different in Gmail and Outlook?

    Each inbox renders email with its own rules. Gmail strips some styles, Outlook on Windows uses a word-processor engine that handles spacing and images differently, and Apple Mail is the most forgiving. That is why email is built with tables and inline styles — they are the methods every inbox understands.

    Should I use tables or divs in email?

    Tables. Divs and modern layout methods fail in Outlook and some webmail, while tables work everywhere. A single-column table 600 pixels wide is the standard email body, with nested tables for columns inside it. It feels old-fashioned because email never got the layout upgrade the web did.

    Why do images sometimes not show?

    Most inboxes block images until the reader allows them, so the reader sees the alt text instead. That is why every image needs alt text and a width, and why the message should still make sense with the images removed. Never put the whole message in one big image.

    What is the plain-text version for?

    It is the version readers see when HTML and images are off, and some spam filters read it too. It should carry the heading, the message and the link text in the right order. This previewer builds one from your HTML so you can check it before you send.

    How wide should an email be?

    600 pixels is the standard. It fits the reading pane of desktop inboxes and scales down to a phone without breaking. Wider tables get cut off or squeezed, which is why this previewer warns about any table wider than 600 pixels.

    Can I test dark mode here?

    Not directly. This preview shows a standard light render, and dark mode is applied by each inbox in its own way — some invert colours, some do not. The safest approach is to use real text on a plain background and to check a test send in an inbox with dark mode on.
