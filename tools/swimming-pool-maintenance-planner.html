How to use the planner

Enter the length, width and average depth of your pool, pick the pool type, and say how often it is used. The planner works out roughly how much water your pool holds, then builds a schedule around it: what to do each week, what to do each month, and how often to test the water. The schedule changes with how much the pool is used, because a pool swum in daily by many people needs more attention than one used a few times a week.

The planner also gives chemical dose estimates, worked out from your pool volume. These are estimates built on a stated assumption — granular chlorine at 65% strength, a common product — and they are labelled as estimates everywhere they appear. Your own product may be a different strength, so always check its label, and always test the water before and after adding anything.

Use the copy button to keep your plan somewhere handy — on the fridge, in a notebook, or on your phone. A plan you can see is a plan you will follow, and a pool kept up week by week is far easier to look after than a pool rescued once a season.

The weekly routine

Skimming is the job you will do most often. Leaves, insects and dust land on the surface every day, and if they sink and rot, they feed algae and stain the pool floor. A few minutes with a skimmer net, every day or two in heavy use and twice a week in light use, keeps the surface clear and the water cleaner for everything else you do.

Brushing the walls and steps once a week stops algae getting a hold. Algae starts as a faint slippery film on the walls, usually in corners and on steps where the water moves least. A brush breaks it up before it becomes visible, and it costs nothing but a few minutes. Vacuum the floor once a week too, to pick up the dirt that skimming and brushing leave behind.

Testing the water is the weekly job that matters most. Test pH and chlorine at least once a week — twice a week if the pool is heavily used — and write the results down. A test takes a minute with strips or a simple kit, and it tells you whether the water is safe to swim in and whether your chemicals are doing their job. Guessing is not a test.

Check the filter every week as well. Most filters have a pressure gauge, and a rising pressure means the filter is clogged and needs cleaning or backwashing. A clean filter is what keeps the water clear between chemical doses, and a clogged one makes everything else you do less effective.

The monthly routine

Once a month, give the filter a deeper clean and check the pump, hoses and fittings for wear. Look for leaks around the pump, cracks in hoses, and any part that has gone brittle in the sun. Catching a worn part early is a small job; catching it after it fails is a flooded pump room and a pool out of use.

Shocking the pool — adding a large dose of chlorine to burn off built-up waste — is a monthly job for most pools, and an as-needed job after heavy use, a storm, or a spell of cloudy water. Shock in the evening, when the sun cannot burn the chlorine off before it works, and do not swim until the chlorine level has fallen back into range. The planner reminds you of this, but the test kit is what tells you when it is safe.

Check the water level weekly and top up when it is low, but also watch for a level that keeps falling faster than evaporation explains. A pool loses some water to evaporation and splashing, but a steady, unexplained fall can mean a leak — and a leak found early is far cheaper than one found late.

Reading the water (pH, chlorine in plain words)

pH measures how acidic or alkaline the water is, on a scale from 0 to 14, where 7 is neutral. Most pool guides put the ideal range at 7.2 to 7.6 — close to the pH of human tears, which is why water in that range does not sting the eyes. Below 7.2 the water is acidic: it stings eyes, corrodes metal fittings, and makes chlorine work too fast and burn off. Above 7.6 the water is alkaline: chlorine works slowly, the water can go cloudy, and scale forms on surfaces.

Free chlorine is the chlorine available to kill bacteria right now, and it is the number that matters for safety. Combined chlorine is chlorine that has already reacted with waste in the water — it smells strongly of "pool" and does little useful work. A strong chlorine smell usually means too much combined chlorine, not too much chlorine overall, and the fix is shocking the pool to break it down, not adding less chlorine.

Test strips and simple kits show both numbers in a minute. Test at the same time of day, away from the skimmer and away from where chemicals were just added, so the reading represents the pool as a whole. Write the results down: a notebook of readings over a season is the best teacher a pool owner can have.

How much chemical is enough

The honest answer is that it depends on your pool, your product, and your water — which is why this planner gives estimates, not orders. The estimate is built on a stated assumption: granular chlorine at 65% strength, a common product, where about 15 grams raises free chlorine by 1 ppm in 10,000 litres of water. Your pool volume scales that number up or down.

Always start from a test, not from a habit. If the test says chlorine is low, add the estimated dose for the rise you want, wait the time the product label says, and test again. Adding chemicals without testing is guessing, and guessing with pool chemicals wastes money at best and makes the water unsafe at worst.

pH adjusters work the same way: the label gives a dose per volume of water for a given change, and your pool volume tells you how much of that dose you need. Change the pH slowly, in small steps, testing between additions. A large swing in pH is worse than a slightly imperfect pH, because it shocks the water chemistry and takes days to settle.

Store chemicals safely, away from each other, in their original containers, somewhere dry and out of reach of children. Never mix pool chemicals together — mixing chlorine products with acid, or different chlorine types with each other, can release dangerous gas. Add one chemical at a time, and let the water circulate between additions.

Green water and other problems

Green water is almost always algae, and it means the chlorine ran out while the sun and warmth did their work. The fix is to brush the walls and floor to break the algae loose, shock the pool in the evening, and run the filter continuously until the water clears — which can take a day or two. Do not swim until the water is clear and the chlorine is back in range, and do not be tempted to add more and more chemicals at once: patience and filtration clear green water, not a cocktail.

Cloudy water has several causes: high pH, a clogged filter, or fine particles the filter cannot catch. Check the pH first, clean the filter, and give it a day of continuous running. If the cloudiness persists, the problem may be in the filter itself — old filter media, or a pump that no longer moves enough water.

A strong chlorine smell, as noted above, usually means combined chlorine, and the fix is shocking. Stains on the pool floor are usually metals or organic matter, and they need identifying before treating — a stain that is really a mineral deposit will not respond to more chlorine, and scrubbing it with the wrong product can damage the surface.

Common mistakes

The most dangerous mistake is shocking the pool and swimming the same day. After a shock dose, chlorine levels are far above the safe range for swimming, and the water can irritate skin, eyes and lungs. Wait until a test shows the level back in range — usually the next day — before anyone gets in.

The most common mistake is forgetting the filter. Chemicals get the attention, but the filter does the physical work of removing dirt, and a clogged filter makes the water cloudy no matter how much chlorine you add. Check the pressure gauge weekly, and clean or backwash when it rises.

The third is adding chemicals without testing. A dose added to water that did not need it is money wasted and chemistry disturbed. Test first, dose to the test, and test again. The test kit is the cheapest tool a pool owner owns.

The fourth is ignoring pH. Chlorine works best in a narrow pH range, and outside it, even a full dose of chlorine does little. If your chlorine seems to vanish no matter how much you add, check the pH before you add more.

The fifth is leaving the pool alone for weeks and expecting a quick rescue. A pool kept up weekly takes minutes a week; a pool neglected for a month takes days of work and far more chemicals to bring back. The schedule this planner builds is designed to keep the work small and regular, so the rescue is never needed.

Questions people ask

How often should I clean my pool?

Skim the surface every day or two in heavy use, twice a week in light use. Brush the walls and vacuum the floor once a week, and test the water at least once a week — twice a week if the pool is heavily used.

How much chlorine do I need?

It depends on your pool size and your product. As an ESTIMATE, about 15 g of granular chlorine at 65% strength raises free chlorine by 1 ppm in 10,000 litres — this planner works out your pool's number. Always test first and follow the product label.

What pH should pool water be?

Most pool guides put the ideal range at 7.2 to 7.6, close to the pH of human tears. Below that the water is acidic and stings eyes; above it, chlorine works slowly and the water can go cloudy.

Can I swim right after adding chemicals?

No. After a normal dose, wait the time the product label says and test the water first. After shocking the pool, wait until a test shows the chlorine level back in range — usually the next day.

How do I clear green pool water?

Green water is usually algae. Brush the walls and floor, shock the pool in the evening, and run the filter continuously until the water clears. Do not swim until the water is clear and a test shows chlorine back in range.

Do I need to test the water every day?

Not usually. Once a week is enough for a lightly used pool; twice a week is better for heavy use, hot weather, or after a storm. Test more often while you are learning your pool, and always test after adding chemicals.
