How to use this guide

Converting a PDF to Word means turning a fixed document back into an editable one. A PDF is built to look the same everywhere, which is wonderful for sharing and terrible for editing. A Word document is the opposite: it is built to be changed. Conversion bridges the two, and the quality of the result depends on what is inside your PDF and which method you use. This guide gives you three complete methods, a comparison table, a checklist to run before you convert, and advice for when the result looks wrong. Use the picker above if you want a quick recommendation, or read the methods and choose for yourself.

Start by checking what kind of PDF you have, because it decides everything else. Open the file and try to select a sentence. If the words highlight and copy into another app, you have a real-text PDF, and any of the three methods will work. If nothing selects, or the whole page selects as one picture, you have a scanned PDF, and you need OCR first. OCR is a program that reads the pictures of the words and turns them into real text. Some converters include OCR, and the picker above will point you to them. If you skip this check and convert a scan directly, you will get back an empty document or a document full of mistakes, and you will have wasted the attempt.

Method 1: Use a free online converter

An online converter is the fastest way to convert a PDF to Word. You open the converter in your browser, choose your PDF file, wait while the service converts it, and download the Word file it makes. There is nothing to install, and it works on any computer or phone with a browser. Well-known free converters include iLovePDF, Smallpdf, Sejda and the online tools from Adobe Acrobat online. All of them offer a free plan, and all of them convert a normal text PDF to Word in a few minutes.

Here are the steps, which are nearly the same on every converter. First, open the converter and find the PDF to Word tool on its site. Second, choose your PDF file from your device, or drag it onto the page if you are on a computer. Third, if the converter offers a choice, pick the option that keeps the layout, sometimes called Word with formatting or similar wording. Fourth, wait for the conversion to finish, then download the Word file and save it somewhere you can find it. Fifth, and most important, open the Word file and check it before you use it. Read the first page, look at any tables, and scroll to the last page to make sure nothing is missing.

Online converters are best for ordinary documents: school handouts, public leaflets, articles, forms you found online, and anything that is not private. Their free plans have limits, such as a number of files per day or a maximum file size, and the exact limits differ from one service to another. If one converter refuses your file or gives a poor result, try another one, because converters handle the same PDF differently. For scanned PDFs, choose a converter that offers OCR, and expect the result to need a proofread, because OCR misreads words, especially in tables and in poor-quality scans.

Method 2: Use a word processor on your computer

If the document is private, or if you convert PDFs often, a word processor on your own computer is the better method. Nothing leaves your device, there is no daily limit, and the result is often cleaner for documents with tables. The free word processor LibreOffice can open a PDF and let you edit it as a document, and you can then save it in Word format. Google Docs can also open a PDF: you upload the file to Google Drive, open it with Google Docs, and then download it as a Word file. Both methods are free, and both keep your file under your own control, with Google Docs being the one that involves uploading to a service.

To convert with LibreOffice, install it once from its official site, then open the program and use its Open command to choose your PDF. LibreOffice reads the PDF and shows it as an editable document. You can fix the text, adjust the tables, and then save the file in Word format using Save As. The first install takes a few minutes, but after that each conversion is quick, and there are no limits on how many files you convert. This method is the one to choose for private documents, because the file never leaves your computer at any point.

To convert with Google Docs, open Google Drive in your browser, upload the PDF, then right-click the file and open it with Google Docs. Google Docs reads the PDF and turns it into an editable document, and you can then download it as a Word file from the File menu. This method is convenient if you already use a Google account, and it works on any computer without installing anything. It does involve uploading the file to Google, so it is not the right choice for private papers. For scanned PDFs, Google Docs also runs OCR when it opens the file, which makes it a useful free way to get text out of a scan, though you should still proofread the result carefully.

Method 3: Convert on your phone

Converting on a phone is straightforward, and you do not need a computer for it. The simplest way is to open a free online converter in your phone browser and follow the same steps as Method 1. iLovePDF, Smallpdf and PDF24 Tools all work in a mobile browser, and they let you choose a PDF from your phone storage, convert it, and download the Word file. The pages are designed for touch, and the whole job takes a few minutes on a decent connection.

If you convert files on your phone often, a dedicated app is more comfortable than a browser. The makers of the converters above offer free apps for Android and iPhone, and the steps inside the app are the same: choose the PDF, convert, and save the Word file to your phone. A phone app can also scan a paper page with your camera and turn it into a PDF first, which is handy when the document only exists on paper. Whichever route you take, the advice is the same as on a computer: check the result before you use it, and do not upload private documents to an online service when an offline method is available.

One practical tip for phone conversion is to mind your connection. A large PDF takes a while to upload on a slow mobile connection, and if the upload is interrupted, you may need to start again. If the file is large, convert it on Wi-Fi rather than mobile data. Also check where the downloaded Word file goes on your phone, because different browsers save downloads in different places. Open the file once from your downloads folder to confirm it is really there before you close the converter page.

Before you convert: a short checklist

Run through this checklist before you start, and most conversion problems disappear. First, confirm the PDF is real text or a scan, using the selection test. Second, make a copy of the original PDF and keep it safe, so you can start again if a step goes wrong. Third, note what matters most in the document: the exact wording, the tables, the images, or the page layout. That tells you which method to pick and what to check afterwards. Fourth, if the document is private, choose an offline method and do not upload it anywhere. Fifth, check the file size against the free plan limit of the converter you plan to use, so you are not surprised halfway through. Sixth, close other heavy apps or browser tabs if your device is slow, because conversion pages work better with a little free memory.

It also helps to know what you will do with the Word file afterwards. If you plan to edit it heavily, a clean conversion matters more than a fast one, so take the word processor route. If you only need the text to quote a paragraph or two, a quick online conversion is enough, and you can copy the words you need and ignore the rest. If the document must look identical to the original, be ready to fix the layout by hand afterwards, because no free converter reproduces a complex PDF perfectly. Knowing the goal before you start keeps the job quick and stops you from redoing work that was already good enough.

What conversion loses

Conversion is not perfect, and knowing what it loses helps you judge the result fairly. Fonts are the first casualty: a PDF can carry a font that your computer does not have, and the converter will replace it with the closest match it knows, which can change the look of headings and the spacing of lines. Tables are the second: a simple table with clear lines usually survives, but a complex table with merged cells, narrow columns or tiny text often comes out with shifted columns or joined cells. Images usually survive, though their position on the page can shift, and a picture that sat neatly beside a paragraph may end up above or below it.

Page breaks and headers are the third thing to check. A PDF has fixed pages, while a Word document reflows its text, so a heading that sat at the top of page three in the PDF may land at the bottom of page two in Word. Page numbers, running headers and footers sometimes turn into ordinary text, or disappear entirely. None of this means the conversion failed. It means the two formats think about documents differently, and a few minutes of tidying in Word will fix most of it. The text itself, which is usually what matters most, converts well in almost every case, and that is the part worth checking first.

When the result looks wrong

If the converted file looks wrong, do not give up on the first attempt. Start by identifying the problem. If the document is empty or nearly empty, the PDF is a scan, and you need OCR first. The PDF OCR and Text Extractor guide on ToolBari explains how to tell a scan from a text PDF and shows three free ways to get the text out. If the text is there but the layout is a mess, try a different method, because converters handle the same file differently, and a desktop word processor often does better with complex layouts. If only the tables are broken, convert again and then rebuild the broken table by hand in Word, with the PDF open beside it as a reference. Rebuilding one table takes a few minutes and is usually faster than fighting the converter.

If the converted file has strange characters, the problem is usually the font or the language of the original document. Try a different converter, or open the PDF in a word processor and save it from there. If the file is very large and the converter refuses it, split the PDF into smaller parts first, convert each part, and join the Word files afterwards. And if the same file fails everywhere, check whether the PDF itself is damaged by opening it in a different reader. A damaged PDF converts badly in every tool, and no converter can fix a file that is broken at the source.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is uploading a scanned PDF to a converter and expecting editable text back. A scan is a set of pictures, and a converter cannot find text in a picture. The fix is OCR first, then conversion. The second mistake is trusting a converter with private documents. Free online tools are convenient, but your file travels to their server, and for bank papers, medical letters or work contracts, an offline method is the safer choice. The third mistake is accepting the first result without checking it. Always open the Word file, read the first page, check the tables, and scroll to the end. A two-minute check catches the broken table or the missing page while there is still time to fix it.

Another common mistake is choosing a method for speed when the document needs care. A quick online conversion is fine for a simple handout, but a contract or a report with tables deserves the slower word processor method, which keeps more of the layout. People also forget to keep the original PDF, and then cannot start again when a step goes wrong. Keep the original, work on a copy, and give the converted file a clear name. Finally, many people convert the whole document when they only need a paragraph or two. If you only need a few sentences, open the PDF, copy the text directly, and paste it where you need it. Conversion is a tool for whole documents, not for a single quote.

Questions people ask

Can I convert a PDF to Word for free?

Yes. A free online converter, a free word processor on your computer, or a free app on your phone can all do it. This guide shows all three methods step by step, and says plainly what each one keeps and loses.

Will the Word file look exactly like the PDF?

Usually not exactly. Simple text converts well, but unusual fonts, complex tables and crowded layouts often shift a little. Always open the Word file after converting and check the pages, the tables and the headings before you use it.

Why does my converted file have no text at all?

Your PDF is probably a scan, which means each page is a picture of a page rather than real text. A converter cannot find text in a picture. Run OCR on the file first, which reads the pictures and turns them into real text, then convert the result.

Is it safe to upload my PDF to a free converter?

For ordinary documents, such as a school handout or a public leaflet, a well-known free converter is fine. For private documents, such as bank papers, medical letters or work contracts, use an offline method on your own computer instead, so the file never leaves your device.

Can I convert a PDF to Word on my phone?

Yes. Open a free online converter in your phone browser, choose the PDF from your phone, and download the Word file it makes. The steps are the same as on a computer, and the method works on both Android and iPhone.

What should I do if the tables come out broken?

Try a different method, because converters handle tables differently, and a desktop word processor often keeps tables better than a quick online tool. If a table is still broken, rebuild that one table by hand in Word, using the PDF open beside it as a reference.
