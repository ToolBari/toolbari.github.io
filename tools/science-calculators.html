The five calculators, with a worked example each

Each calculator below is described in plain words, with one worked example using the same numbers the calculator itself uses. Work through the example on paper first if you like, then check it with the tool.

Speed. Speed tells you how fast something is moving, and it comes from dividing the distance travelled by the time taken. The formula is speed equals distance divided by time. Take a car that travels 100 metres in 10 seconds. Put the numbers into the formula and you get 100 divided by 10, which equals 10, and the unit is metres per second, so the car is moving at 10 metres per second. Speed is one of the first formulas most students meet, and it is worth learning well, because the same pattern of dividing one quantity by another turns up in density and pressure too. Remember that the answer is an average speed over the whole journey. The car might have gone faster at some points and slower at others, but the formula only sees the total distance and the total time.

Force. Force tells you how hard something is being pushed or pulled, and it comes from multiplying mass by acceleration. The formula is force equals mass multiplied by acceleration. Take a mass of 2 kilograms accelerating at 3 metres per second squared. Put the numbers in and you get 2 multiplied by 3, which equals 6, and the unit is the newton, so the force is 6 newtons. The newton is named after Isaac Newton, whose laws of motion describe how forces change the way things move. A bigger mass needs a bigger force to accelerate at the same rate, and that is why a loaded trolley is harder to push than an empty one. Keep the mass in kilograms, because the newton is defined with kilograms, and grams will give you an answer a thousand times too small.

Density. Density tells you how much mass is packed into a given volume, and it comes from dividing mass by volume. The formula is density equals mass divided by volume. Take 10 grams of a material that fills 2 cubic centimetres. Put the numbers in and you get 10 divided by 2, which equals 5, and the unit is grams per cubic centimetre, so the density is 5 grams per cubic centimetre. Density explains why some things float and others sink. A material with a lower density than water floats on water, and a material with a higher density sinks. It also explains why a small block of metal can weigh more than a large block of wood. The two blocks have different densities, so the same volume holds a different mass.

Pressure. Pressure tells you how spread out a force is, and it comes from dividing force by the area it acts on. The formula is pressure equals force divided by area. Take a force of 20 newtons spread over an area of 4 square metres. Put the numbers in and you get 20 divided by 4, which equals 5, and the unit is the pascal, so the pressure is 5 pascals. The same force gives a much higher pressure when it acts on a smaller area, which is why a sharp knife cuts and a blunt one does not, and why snowshoes stop you sinking into snow. The pascal is a small unit, so everyday pressures are often large numbers of pascals, but the formula stays the same whatever the size of the numbers.

Molarity. Molarity tells you how strong a solution is, and it comes from dividing the number of moles of a substance by the volume of the solution in litres. The formula is molarity equals moles divided by volume. Take 2 moles of a substance dissolved to make 4 litres of solution. Put the numbers in and you get 2 divided by 4, which equals 0.5, and the unit is moles per litre, so the molarity is 0.5 moles per litre. Chemists use molarity whenever they need to describe a solution exactly, because it says how much of the substance is in each litre. If you add more water to a solution without adding more of the substance, the volume grows, the number of moles stays the same, and the molarity falls. That is dilution, and it follows straight from the formula.

Units and why they matter

A number without a unit is only half an answer. Ten metres per second is a speed. Ten on its own could be anything: ten metres, ten seconds, ten kilograms, or ten of anything else. That is why every answer on this page comes with its unit attached, and why the steps show the unit at the end. In school science, an answer without a unit often loses the mark even when the number is right, so get into the habit of writing the unit every time.

Units matter even more before the calculation starts, because the formulas only work when the numbers are in the right units. The speed formula expects metres and seconds. If the distance is given in kilometres, you must turn it into metres first, by multiplying by one thousand. If the time is given in minutes, you must turn it into seconds first, by multiplying by sixty. Skip that step and the answer will be wrong, sometimes wildly wrong. A car travelling 100 kilometres in 1 hour is not moving at 100 metres per second. It is moving at about 28 metres per second, because 100 kilometres is 100,000 metres and 1 hour is 3,600 seconds. The formula is the same, but the numbers going in have to be in metres and seconds.

The same trap waits in the other calculators. The force calculator expects kilograms, so a mass given in grams must be divided by one thousand first. The density calculator expects grams and cubic centimetres, so a volume given in litres or in cubic metres must be converted before it goes in. The pressure calculator expects square metres, so an area given in square centimetres must be divided by ten thousand first, because there are ten thousand square centimetres in one square metre. The molarity calculator expects litres, so a volume given in millilitres must be divided by one thousand first. Each of these conversions is simple on its own, but skipping one is the single most common reason for a wrong answer in school science.

There is a quick habit that catches most unit mistakes. Before you tap calculate, read the label above each box and say the unit out loud. Then look at the number you are about to type and ask whether it is already in that unit. If it is not, convert it first. After the answer appears, ask whether it is sensible. A walking speed of 400 metres per second is not sensible, and it usually means a kilometre was typed in as metres. A density of 0.005 grams per cubic centimetre for a metal is not sensible either, and it usually means grams and kilograms were mixed up. The calculator will happily divide whatever numbers you give it, so the checking is your job.

Common mistakes to avoid

The first mistake is the one described above: wrong units. It is the most common, and it is the easiest to avoid once you build the habit of checking the label before you type. The second mistake is rounding in the middle of the working. If a division gives a long decimal, keep the full number while you work and round only the final answer. Rounding early can shift the final answer by enough to matter, especially when the rounded number is used in another step. These calculators keep the full number through the calculation and show the answer rounded to four decimal places, which is more precision than school work usually needs.

The third mistake is forgetting to square a unit, or squaring a number that should not be squared. Acceleration is measured in metres per second squared, which means the seconds are squared, not the whole number. Area is measured in square metres, which means the length and the width are both in metres before they are multiplied. If an area is given as a length and a width, multiply them first and then use the result in the pressure formula. Putting a single length into the area box is a common slip, and the answer will be wrong even though the arithmetic is right.

The fourth mistake is mixing up which number goes on top. Speed is distance divided by time, not time divided by distance. Density is mass divided by volume, not volume divided by mass. Pressure is force divided by area, not area divided by force. If the answer looks upside down, check that the numbers went into the right boxes. The steps shown under each answer make this easy to check, because they show the numbers in the order they were divided.

The fifth mistake is trusting an answer without asking whether it makes sense. A calculator never knows whether your numbers were sensible. It divides what you give it and shows the result. You are the one who knows that a person cannot run at 90 metres per second, that water cannot have a density of 90 grams per cubic centimetre, and that a school experiment is unlikely to produce a pressure of a million pascals. When an answer surprises you, the first thing to check is the numbers you typed in, and the second is the units. Nine times out of ten, the mistake is there.

What these calculators cannot do

These calculators work with the standard school formulas, and they do not go beyond them. The speed calculator gives an average speed, not the speed at any single moment, and it cannot tell you anything about the direction of travel. The force calculator assumes the mass stays the same and the acceleration is steady, which is true for most school problems but not for every real situation. The density calculator assumes the material is uniform all the way through, which is not true for a hollow object or a mixture. The pressure calculator assumes the force is spread evenly over the whole area. The molarity calculator assumes the substance is fully dissolved and the volume is measured after dissolving, not before. None of these limits matters for ordinary school work, but it is honest to name them, so you know where the formulas stop.

The calculators also cannot convert units for you, and they cannot tell you whether the numbers you typed are right. They cannot read a question and decide which formula to use. That part is yours, and it is the part that matters most in an exam. If you can look at a question, pick the right formula, get the units right, and check that the answer makes sense, you have the skill. The calculator is only a fast way to do the arithmetic.

Questions people ask

What is the formula for speed?

Speed equals distance divided by time. If a car travels 100 metres in 10 seconds, its speed is 10 metres per second, because 100 divided by 10 equals 10.

How do I calculate force?

Force equals mass multiplied by acceleration. A 2 kilogram mass accelerating at 3 metres per second squared gives a force of 6 newtons, because 2 multiplied by 3 equals 6.

What units should I use in these calculators?

Use metres for distance, seconds for time, kilograms for mass in the force calculator, grams and cubic centimetres in the density calculator, newtons and square metres for pressure, and moles and litres for molarity. If your numbers are in other units, convert them first, because mixing units gives a wrong answer.

Why does my answer look wrong?

The most common reason is a unit mistake, such as entering kilometres where the calculator expects metres. Check that every number is in the unit shown on the label, then check that you have not rounded a number in the middle of the working.

What is molarity, and when do I use it?

Molarity tells you how many moles of a substance are dissolved in each litre of solution. Chemists use it to describe how strong a solution is, and this calculator divides the number of moles by the volume in litres.

Can I use these calculators for homework?

Yes. Each calculator shows the formula and the working step by step, so you can copy the steps into your homework and check that you understand them. The answers are only as good as the numbers you enter, so always check your units and your working before you hand anything in.
