How to pick a girl name that ages well

A girl name has to work for a baby, a schoolgirl, a teenager and a grown woman. The names that age well are usually the ones that are simple to say and hard to mock. Short names age well: Mia, Ava, Lily. So do names with a long history: Elizabeth, Anna, Catherine. A name that sounds sweet at five and steady at fifty is a name that will serve the child for life.

Sound matters as much as meaning. Say the name with the surname and listen for the rhythm. A one-syllable first name often pairs well with a longer surname, and a long first name often pairs well with a short surname. If the first name and the surname end in the same sound, the name can blur together — say it out loud a few times and you will know. And check the nickname: a girl named Penelope will be called Penny by someone, sooner or later, so make sure you like the short form too.

Meaning is worth a check, but it should not decide the name on its own. A name that means light or grace is a nice story, but a girl named Grace is not obliged to be graceful, and a girl named Felicity is not guaranteed happiness. Pick a name whose meaning you would be happy to explain, and then let the child give the name its own meaning.

If this is not your first child, think about how the names sound together. Siblings do not need matching names — in fact, names that rhyme or start with the same letter can cause confusion for years. Two girls called Ella and Emma will spend their lives answering to each other's name. A little contrast is kinder: different starting sounds, different lengths, and a different feel. The names should sit well side by side without blending into one. And if an older child has strong feelings about the new baby's name, listen to them — a name the whole family likes is worth more than a name only the parents love.

Finally, give the name the playground test and the job-interview test. Imagine the name called out across a playground, and imagine it on a business card. A name that passes both tests is a name that will serve the child at every age. If a name only passes one — lovely for a baby, awkward for an adult, or the other way round — think about whether the nickname can carry the other half. A long formal name with a friendly nickname, like Penelope and Penny, passes both tests at once.

Classic, modern or unique: how to choose

Classic girl names are the safest choice. Elizabeth, Anna, Catherine and Mary have been used for centuries, and they will still sound right in fifty years. A classic name is easy to spell, easy to pronounce, and never sounds dated. The only cost is that the name is common — but as the popular names page explains, even the most popular name today belongs to roughly one baby in a hundred.

Modern girl names feel current. Luna, Aria, Harper and Isla are modern: short, bright, and easy to say. Modern names suit parents who want a name that sounds like today. The risk is that a very modern name can date faster than a classic one, so pick a modern name you would still like in twenty years, not just one that sounds good this year.

Unique girl names are the ones few other children will share. Astrid, Aurelia, Wren and Persephone are unique: names that stand out in a class list. A unique name is a gift if the child likes standing out, and a burden if they do not. If you choose a unique name, pick one that is easy to say and spell — a name that is unusual but not difficult is the best of both worlds.

Names from around the world

The girl names on this page come from many languages, and each one carries a piece of its home. Hebrew gives us Elizabeth, Anna and Mary. Greek gives us Catherine, Sophia and Persephone. Latin gives us Olivia, Aurora and Luna. French gives us Charlotte, Margot and Anais. Irish gives us Maeve, Nora and Keira. Norse gives us Astrid, Freya and Ingrid. Italian gives us Aria, Mia and Bella. A name from your family's language is a way of carrying that language forward, even if the child grows up speaking English every day.

If your family speaks more than one language, test the name in both. Say it with each accent, check that it is easy to pronounce for both sides of the family, and check that it does not mean something odd in the other language. A name that travels well — easy in English, easy in the other language, with a meaning that works in both — is a quiet gift to a child who will grow up between two worlds.

Some of the most interesting girl names on the list are the ones that work in several languages at once. Anna, Sofia, Elena and Clara are used across Europe and beyond, with the same spelling or nearly the same. A name like that gives a child a home in more than one place, and it means the name will be recognised wherever the child goes.

Honour names are another reason to look around the world. Many families name a child after a grandmother or a great-grandmother, and the name that honours her may come from a language the child does not speak yet. A girl named after a grandmother called Giuseppina or Fatima carries that name into a new country and a new generation. If the original name is hard to pronounce where you live, a shorter form can carry the honour without the difficulty — Giuseppina becomes Josie, and the link stays.
