Alhamdulillah — All Praise Is for Allah

Alhamdulillah means "All praise is for Allah" — every good thing, every blessing, every breath, belongs to Him, and the praise for it belongs to Him too. Say it 33 times after every prayer, and after eating and drinking: "Alhamdulillah fills the Scale" (Sahih Muslim 223).

What Alhamdulillah means

Alhamdulillah is hamd: praise. The word covers every kind of praise at once — praise for who Allah is, and praise for what He has given. When a Muslim says Alhamdulillah, he is saying that all praise belongs to Allah: not some of it, not the praise for the big things, but all of it. The food on the table, the health in the body, the roof overhead, the breath just taken — all of it is from Allah, and the praise for all of it goes back to Him.

That is what makes Alhamdulillah different from a simple thank-you. A thank-you is for something received. Alhamdulillah is for something received and for the Giver Himself. Allah is praised because He is worthy of praise, whether a particular blessing has just arrived or not. A Muslim says it in good times and in hard times, because the One being praised does not change when circumstances change. The praise is not a reaction to the day; it is a statement about Allah.

Alhamdulillah is also one of the four most beloved words to Allah, along with SubhanAllah, La ilaha illallah and Allahu Akbar (Sahih Muslim 2137). Four short phrases, and they are the dearest speech to Allah. That ranking matters for a beginner: it means the simplest dhikr is also the most beloved. You do not need long formulas or difficult words to say what Allah loves most. You need four phrases, said often, said with the heart present.

The four phrases work together. SubhanAllah declares Allah perfect; Alhamdulillah praises Him; La ilaha illallah declares Him alone worthy of worship; Allahu Akbar declares Him greater than everything. A Muslim who keeps these four on his tongue through the day has covered the heart of the faith in a handful of words. This guide is about the second of the four — but the other three have their own guides on this hub, and the after-salah routine joins three of them together.

Arabic, transliteration and meaning

الْحَمْدُ لِلَّهِ

Transliteration: Alhamdulillah. Meaning: "All praise is for Allah."

Say it slowly at first: al-ham-du-lil-lah. The first part, "al-hamd", is the praise — all of it, every kind; the second part names Allah as the One the praise belongs to. The phrase is short enough to say in a single breath, which is part of its design: it is meant to be repeated, and repetition is easier when the words are light on the tongue.

If you are learning the Arabic, read the phrase from the page above and listen to a trusted reciter saying it. The harakat — the small vowel marks above and below the letters — are shown on the Arabic here so the pronunciation is clear. Take a day or two to get the sound right, then build the habit. A correct phrase said slowly is better than a rushed phrase said wrong.

How many times, and when

The fixed number for Alhamdulillah is thirty-three after every prayer. After every prayer, say SubhanAllah thirty-three times, then Alhamdulillah thirty-three times, then Allahu Akbar thirty-three times, and complete the hundred with the words of tawhid (Sahih Muslim 597). That is the after-salah routine, and it takes about two minutes. Five prayers a day means one hundred and sixty-five Alhamdulillah a day from this routine alone — before you have added anything else.

Beyond the after-salah count, Alhamdulillah is the Sunnah after eating and drinking. The meal ends, the cup is put down, and the tongue says Alhamdulillah — the blessing is named and its Giver is praised before the moment passes. It is a small act, and it is easy to forget, but it is the habit that turns eating from a routine into an act of worship. Three meals a day, plus drinks between them, add up to many Alhamdulillah a week from this Sunnah alone.

Beyond the fixed numbers, Alhamdulillah has no limit. It is said on waking, on hearing good news, when something goes right, when something goes wrong and could have been worse, and whenever the heart turns to Allah. There is no wrong time for it and no wrong place, and it can be said sitting, standing or walking, with or without wudu. The fixed numbers give the day a backbone; the free repetitions fill the rest of the day.

If you are starting from nothing, start with the after-salah thirty-three. Tie the new habit to a habit you already have — prayer — and let it grow from there. After a week or two of keeping the thirty-three after every prayer, add the after-eating Alhamdulillah: one phrase, said with meaning, at the end of every meal. Small numbers kept every day beat big numbers kept once.

The reward

The reward for Alhamdulillah is stated in a single powerful sentence: "Alhamdulillah fills the Scale" (Sahih Muslim 223). The Scale is the balance on which deeds are weighed on the Day of Judgment, and one short phrase — five syllables, one breath — fills it. It is worth stopping on that image. A word you can say in two seconds carries that much weight. The exchange is so generous that it is easy to read past it — but it is the point of the whole practice. Allah rewards a small, sincere act far beyond what the act itself seems worth.

The reward is not only for the Scale. Alhamdulillah is one of the four most beloved words to Allah (Sahih Muslim 2137), and beloved speech is rewarded as beloved speech is. A Muslim who says it often is saying, many times a day, the words Allah loves most. That is a different kind of reward from the weighed deeds: it is the reward of being occupied with what Allah loves, instead of being occupied with what earns nothing.

There is a third reward, quieter than the first two, and it is the one people notice first in their own lives: the heart changes. A tongue that keeps saying Alhamdulillah through the day keeps the heart grateful through the day. Gratitude is a habit before it is a feeling, and the phrase is the habit. A person who says Alhamdulillah after every meal starts to notice the meal. A person who says it after every prayer starts to notice the prayer. The phrase trains the eye to see blessings instead of passing them by — and that is the rest the Quran promises: "Verily, in the remembrance of Allah do hearts find rest" (Quran 13:28). The weighed reward is for the Hereafter; the rest is for today.

None of this makes Alhamdulillah a magic formula. The reward is tied to the saying, but the saying is meant to be said with the heart present — knowing who Allah is, and meaning the words. A hundred said while thinking about the words is worth more than a hundred said while thinking about lunch. The numbers are a target, not a trick: they exist so the tongue has something to aim at while the heart learns to be present.

How to count

Count on the fingers of your right hand (Abu Dawud 1502). The simplest method is to press each fingertip in turn with your thumb: three joints on each of four fingers gives twelve, and three rounds gives thirty-six — so thirty-three is just under three full rounds. With a little practice the hands learn the count and the tongue is free to think about the words.

Prayer beads are allowed, and many people find them helpful for a long count. But the fingers are better: they are always with you, they cost nothing, and they keep the counting quiet and private. The Tasbih Counter tool is a third option for people who like a visible target on a screen. Whichever you use, the rule is the same — count to stay present, not to race. A slow thirty-three said with the heart is worth more than a hurried hundred said on autopilot.

Two practical tips help the count stay honest. First, say the full phrase every time — Alhamdulillah, not a shortened mumble. The tongue speeds up on its own after a few repetitions, and the phrase shrinks without you noticing. Second, keep the same counting method every day. A method that changes daily is a method that gets abandoned; a method that stays the same becomes a habit, and a habit is what carries you through the days when you do not feel like it.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is rushing. The tongue learns the phrase quickly, and after a few days it can run through thirty-three in under a minute — but speed is not the goal. If you finish and cannot remember saying it, you went too fast. Slow down until each repetition is a real word, said on purpose. The routine takes two minutes at a calm pace; there is no prize for finishing in one.

The second mistake is counting on the left hand. The Sunnah way is the fingers of the right hand (Abu Dawud 1502). It is a small detail, and it is easy to get right once you know it: the right hand counts, the left hand rests. If you use beads, hold them in the right hand too.

The third mistake is saying Alhamdulillah only when things go well. The phrase is praise for Allah, not a report on the day — and Allah is worthy of praise in hard times too. The Muslim who says Alhamdulillah only in good times has made the praise depend on the weather. The habit is to say it after every prayer and every meal, whatever the day has brought, and to let the praise shape the mood instead of waiting for the mood to shape the praise.

The fourth mistake is stopping after a few days because the habit feels small. A small habit is the point. Thirty-three after each prayer, kept for a year, is more than sixty thousand Alhamdulillah — and it took two minutes a day. The people who keep dhikr for life are not the people who started with the biggest numbers; they are the people who started with a small number and never stopped.

Frequently asked questions

What does Alhamdulillah mean?

Alhamdulillah means All praise is for Allah. It says that every blessing is from Allah and all praise for it belongs to Him.

How many times should I say Alhamdulillah after prayer?

Thirty-three times after every prayer, between 33 SubhanAllah and 33 Allahu Akbar, completing one hundred with the words of tawhid (Sahih Muslim 597).

What is the reward for saying Alhamdulillah?

Alhamdulillah fills the Scale on which deeds are weighed (Sahih Muslim 223). It is also one of the four most beloved words to Allah (Sahih Muslim 2137).

Should I say Alhamdulillah after eating?

Yes. Saying Alhamdulillah after eating and drinking is the Sunnah — the blessing is named and its Giver is praised before the moment passes.

How do I count Alhamdulillah?

Count on the fingers of your right hand (Abu Dawud 1502). Prayer beads are allowed and the Tasbih Counter tool works too, but the fingers are better.

Can I say Alhamdulillah without wudu?

Yes. Dhikr can be said in any state, with or without wudu, sitting, standing or walking, at any time of day.
