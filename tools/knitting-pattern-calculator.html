Knitting Pattern Calculator

Turn your gauge swatch into stitch counts, row counts and a yarn estimate for any size. Enter your stitches and rows per 10 centimetres and your finished measurements, and the calculator works out the pattern numbers with the working shown.

The numbers are exact arithmetic on the gauge you enter, so they are only as good as your swatch: measure a washed, blocked swatch of at least 10 by 10 centimetres, and count carefully. A small error in gauge becomes a large error in a finished garment.

How to use this calculator

Enter your gauge — the stitches and rows you get per 10 centimetres, measured from a washed swatch — and the finished width and length you want, in centimetres. The calculator works out how many stitches to cast on and how many rows to knit. If you weighed your 10 by 10 centimetre swatch, enter its weight in grams and the calculator estimates the total yarn you need; if you also enter the weight of one ball, it tells you how many balls to buy. The working is shown underneath, so you can check every step.

The gauge is the number that matters most, and it must come from your own knitting, not from the ball band. Two knitters using the same yarn and the same needles get different gauges, because tension is personal. The ball band gauge is a starting point for choosing needles; your swatch gauge is the number this calculator needs. If you have not made a swatch yet, make one first — the section below explains how.

The swatch weight is optional, but it is what makes the yarn estimate real. Without it, the calculator can count stitches and rows but cannot estimate yarn, because yarn use depends on the yarn and the stitch pattern as well as the size. Weighing a 10 by 10 centimetre swatch takes a minute with kitchen scales, and it turns a guess into arithmetic.

What gauge is and why it matters

Gauge is the number of stitches and rows in a fixed size — usually 10 by 10 centimetres — knitted in the yarn, needles and stitch pattern you plan to use. A gauge of 20 stitches and 28 rows per 10 centimetres means that every 10 centimetres of width needs 20 stitches, and every 10 centimetres of length needs 28 rows. It is the conversion rate between your knitting and your measurements, and every number in a pattern depends on it.

Gauge matters because small differences grow. One extra stitch per 10 centimetres sounds like nothing, but over a 50 centimetre wide sweater it is five extra stitches — and over a 100 centimetre blanket it is ten. A garment knitted at the wrong gauge comes out the wrong size, and the error is proportional: a gauge that is 10 percent out gives a garment 10 percent too big or too small. That is why patterns say "check your gauge" and mean it.

To measure gauge, knit a swatch larger than 10 by 10 centimetres — 15 by 15 is a good size — in the stitch pattern you will use, wash and dry it the way you will wash the finished piece, then lay it flat and count the stitches across 10 centimetres in the middle (not at the edges, where stitches distort) and the rows up 10 centimetres. Count twice. The washed swatch is the honest one: yarn changes size when it is washed, and an unwashed swatch lies.

The stitch and row maths

The stitch count is the finished width divided by 10, times the stitches per 10 centimetres. A 50 centimetre wide piece at 20 stitches per 10 centimetres needs 50 divided by 10 times 20, which is 100 stitches. The row count is the finished length divided by 10, times the rows per 10 centimetres: a 60 centimetre long piece at 28 rows per 10 centimetres needs 60 divided by 10 times 28, which is 168 rows.

The calculator rounds the stitch count to a whole number — you cannot cast on half a stitch. It rounds to the nearest whole stitch, and the steps show the unrounded number so you can see how close the rounding is. If your stitch pattern needs a multiple — a repeat of 4 stitches plus 2, for example — round to the nearest number that fits the repeat, and adjust the width by a fraction of a centimetre. The calculator gives the plain count; the pattern repeat is your adjustment.

Rows work the same way, with one difference: rows are easier to adjust as you go, because you can stop when the piece measures the right length. Stitches are fixed at the cast-on; rows are counted as you knit. If your row gauge is slightly out, the piece will reach the right length in a slightly different number of rows, and measuring as you go catches it.

Worked example

Take a gauge of 20 stitches and 28 rows per 10 centimetres, a finished width of 50 centimetres and a finished length of 60 centimetres, a swatch weight of 10 grams for a 10 by 10 centimetre swatch, and balls of 50 grams. The cast-on is 50 divided by 10 times 20, which is 100 stitches. The rows are 60 divided by 10 times 28, which is 168 rows.

The area of the finished piece is 50 times 60, which is 3000 square centimetres. A 10 by 10 centimetre swatch covers 100 square centimetres, so the piece is 30 swatches in area. At 10 grams per swatch, the yarn needed is 30 times 10, which is 300 grams. At 50 grams per ball, that is 300 divided by 50, which is 6 balls exactly.

Now change one thing: make the piece 55 centimetres wide instead of 50. The cast-on becomes 55 divided by 10 times 20, which is 110 stitches. The area becomes 55 times 60, which is 3300 square centimetres, or 33 swatches, so the yarn becomes 330 grams — 6.6 balls, rounded up to 7. A 5 centimetre change in width costs a whole extra ball, which is why measuring before you buy matters.

Yarn estimates and why they vary

The yarn estimate on this page uses the swatch-weight method, which is the most reliable way to estimate yarn without a pattern. It works because yarn use is proportional to area: a piece twice the area of your swatch uses twice the yarn, if the stitch pattern and tension are the same. Weighing the swatch captures everything about your yarn and your tension in one number, which is why it beats any table of averages.

The estimate still varies, and the honest reasons are worth knowing. A textured stitch pattern — cables, bobbles, heavy ribbing — uses more yarn than plain stocking stitch, because the stitches are longer and denser. A loose tension uses more yarn than a tight one for the same size. And the finishing — seams, borders, tassels, a collar — uses yarn the area calculation does not count. The estimate is for the main piece; the finishing is extra.

The practical habit is to buy one ball more than the estimate. Yarn from a different dye lot can differ slightly in colour, and running out mid-project means either a visible change or a hunt for the same lot. An extra ball is insurance, and leftover yarn is useful for repairs, swatches and small projects. The calculator rounds up to whole balls already; the extra ball is your own decision on top.

Adjusting a pattern to your size

Patterns are written for a set of sizes at a stated gauge, and your body or your gauge may not match any of them. The calculator helps in two ways. First, if your gauge differs from the pattern's gauge, enter your gauge and your finished measurements to get the stitch and row counts for your knitting — the pattern's instructions stay the same, but the numbers change. Second, if you want a size between two pattern sizes, enter your finished measurements directly and use the counts the calculator gives.

Ease is the other half of sizing: the difference between your body measurement and the finished garment measurement. A sweater with no ease fits skin-tight; a sweater with 5 centimetres of ease fits comfortably; a sweater with 15 centimetres of ease is oversized. Decide the ease you want, add it to your body measurement, and enter the total as the finished width. The calculator does not choose ease for you — it is a matter of taste and style — but it will count the stitches for whatever width you enter.

When you adjust a pattern, adjust the shaping too. A pattern that decreases for armholes every sixth row needs its decreases recalculated if your row gauge differs, because the same number of rows covers a different length. The calculator gives the total rows; the shaping intervals are your arithmetic, using the same rows-per-10-centimetres figure. Keep a notebook: the counts, the intervals and the measurements, written down, are worth more than a good memory.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is skipping the swatch. Knitting without a gauge is guessing, and the guess is usually wrong — not because knitters are careless, but because tension varies by yarn, needles, mood and time of day. A swatch takes an hour; a sweater takes weeks. The hour is worth it.

The second mistake is measuring an unwashed swatch. Yarn changes when it is washed — it relaxes, it blooms, it can grow or shrink by a noticeable amount — and a garment is worn washed, not fresh off the needles. Wash and dry the swatch the way you will wash the finished piece, then measure. The washed number is the honest one.

The third mistake is counting stitches at the edge of the swatch. Edge stitches distort: they are pulled by the selvedge, stretched by handling, and unrepresentative of the middle. Count in the centre of the swatch, over 10 centimetres, and count twice. If the two counts differ, count a third time and take the middle value.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot design a garment. It counts stitches and rows and estimates yarn for a rectangle of a given size at a given gauge — it does not shape armholes, necks or sleeves, it does not choose a stitch pattern, and it does not know how a yarn behaves. A pattern does those things; the calculator does the arithmetic inside the pattern.

It also cannot know your tension on the day you knit the garment. Tension changes with mood, with tiredness and with practice, and a gauge measured on a calm Sunday may not match the knitting done on a rushed evening. Measure a swatch from the garment itself after a few centimetres, compare it with your original gauge, and adjust if it has drifted. The calculator is exact arithmetic on the gauge you enter — and the gauge is yours to keep honest.

Questions people ask

What is gauge?

Gauge is the number of stitches and rows in a fixed size — usually 10 by 10 centimetres — knitted in the yarn, needles and stitch pattern you plan to use. Why does my swatch matter?

Because small differences grow. One extra stitch per 10 centimetres is five extra stitches over a 50 centimetre sweater, and a gauge 10 percent out gives a garment 10 percent too big or too small. The swatch is the only way to know your own gauge, and your gauge is personal — two knitters using the same yarn get different numbers.

How much yarn do I need?

Weigh a 10 by 10 centimetre swatch, then multiply by the area of your piece in swatches: a 50 by 60 centimetre piece is 30 swatches, so at 10 grams per swatch it needs 300 grams. Enter your numbers in the calculator for the exact figure, and buy one ball more than the estimate as insurance.

What if my gauge does not match the pattern?

Change your needle size, not your tension: go up a needle size if you have too many stitches per 10 centimetres, and down a size if you have too few. Make a new swatch for each needle size and measure again. Or enter your own gauge and finished measurements in this calculator to get counts for your knitting.

Should I round stitch counts up or down?
