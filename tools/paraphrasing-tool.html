What this paraphrasing tool is

Paraphrasing means saying the same idea in different words. You keep the meaning, but you change the sentences so they sound like you wrote them. Students paraphrase when they explain a textbook idea in an essay. Writers paraphrase when they turn a long report into a short summary. Anyone who writes emails, homework, or social posts paraphrases without even noticing it.

This page is a free paraphrasing helper that runs in your browser. You type or paste a sentence, and it shows you two things: synonyms for the key words in your sentence, and a version of your sentence with the parts in a different order. You tap a synonym to swap it into your sentence, or tap a button to use the reordered version.

It is important to be clear about what this page does. It is a helper, not a machine that rewrites for you. It suggests words and orders, and you do the rewriting. That is a strength, not a weakness. When you rewrite a sentence by hand, you actually understand it, and the result sounds natural instead of strange. A page that claims to rewrite whole essays for you is either guessing or copying, and this page does neither.

The helper works on one sentence at a time. You read a sentence, understand it, look away, and say it again in your own words. This page supports that process instead of replacing it.

How to use the helper

Using the helper takes less than a minute, on a phone or a computer.

First, type or paste one sentence into the box. One sentence works best, because the synonym suggestions and the reordering are built around a single sentence. If you paste a whole paragraph, the helper still works, but the suggestions will be harder to follow. Start with the sentence you find hardest to rewrite.

Next, tap the Suggest button. The helper looks at your sentence and finds the key words, the words that carry the meaning. For each key word it shows two or three synonyms as buttons. Tap any synonym and it swaps into your sentence straight away, replacing the first time that word appears.

Below the synonyms you will see a reordered version of your sentence. If your sentence has a comma, the part after the last comma moves to the front. If your sentence has the word and joining two parts, the two parts swap places. Tap the Use button if you like the new order, and it replaces the text in the box. If your sentence has no comma and no and, the helper says so and suggests splitting it in two.

When you are happy with your sentence, tap Copy to copy it. Then paste it wherever you need it. Read it once aloud before you use it. If it sounds awkward, change one more word by hand. The helper gives you a starting point, and your own judgement finishes the job.

On a phone, the buttons are large enough to tap easily. On a computer, use the keyboard and mouse as normal. Nothing you type leaves your device, because everything runs in your browser.

Five manual paraphrase methods

The helper gives you words and orders. The real skill is knowing what to change. These five methods cover almost every paraphrase you will ever need. You can use one method at a time, or combine two or three for a bigger change. Each method below shows the same idea before and after, so you can see exactly what changed.

Method 1 — Change the order. Move a part of the sentence to a different place. English sentences often start with the subject, so moving a time phrase or a place phrase to the front changes the shape at once. Before: The students finished the project on Friday after working for three weeks. After: After working for three weeks, the students finished the project on Friday. The meaning is the same, but the sentence starts differently and reads differently. The helper does this for you when your sentence has a comma or the word and.

Method 2 — Change the words. Swap some words for synonyms, but only words you understand. A synonym that sounds right in one sentence can sound wrong in another, so read the new sentence aloud and ask whether it still means the same thing. Before: The weather was very bad, so the match was cancelled. After: The weather was terrible, so the game was called off. Notice that only two words changed, and both are ordinary words. Swapping every word at once usually makes a mess, so change a few words and leave the rest alone.

Method 3 — Change active to passive, or passive to active. In an active sentence, the doer comes first. In a passive sentence, the thing that was done comes first. Switching between them changes the shape of the sentence completely. Before: The teacher explained the new rule to the class. After: The new rule was explained to the class by the teacher. Passive sentences are useful when the doer matters less than the result, but too many passive sentences make writing feel heavy. Use this method when it fits, and keep most of your sentences active.

Method 4 — Split or join sentences. A long sentence can become two short ones, and two short sentences can become one longer one. Splitting helps when a sentence tries to say too much at once. Joining helps when short sentences sound choppy. Before: The river flooded the fields, and the farmers lost most of their crops that year. After: The river flooded the fields. The farmers lost most of their crops that year. Both versions say the same thing. The split version is easier to read, and it gives each idea its own space.

Method 5 — Change the example. When a sentence explains an idea with an example, you can keep the idea and use a different example of your own. An example you chose yourself is easier to remember. Before: Learning a language is like learning to swim, because you only improve by practising. After: Learning a language is like learning to cook, because you only improve by trying it yourself. The idea is unchanged, but the sentence is now yours. Be careful that your new example really fits the idea, or the sentence will stop making sense.

When paraphrasing is fine and when it is not

Paraphrasing is fine whenever you are explaining an idea in your own words and you are honest about where the idea came from. In school and university work, paraphrasing a source and naming it is normal and expected. In a summary, paraphrasing is the whole job.

Paraphrasing is not fine when it is used to hide copying. Copying a sentence and changing a few words is still copying. Swapping three words in a paragraph you did not write does not make it yours. Neither does rearranging the same words into a slightly different order. Teachers and readers can tell, and plagiarism checks can tell too.

The honest way to paraphrase is simple. Read the source until you understand it. Close it or look away. Write the idea in your own words without looking. Then compare your version with the source. If whole phrases match, rewrite those parts again. If the idea is not yours, name the source, even when the words are yours. The idea still belongs to the person who thought of it first.

This matters most for students, because schools treat copied work seriously. A genuine paraphrase, written from understanding and credited properly, is good work. If you are unsure whether your version is different enough, rewrite it once more.

Common mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is swapping words until the sentence makes no sense. Synonyms are not exact copies of each other. A strong coffee and a powerful coffee are not the same thing, and a big mistake is not a huge error in every context. Change words you understand, and read the result aloud. If it sounds strange, put the old word back.

The second mistake is changing words but keeping the sentence shape exactly the same. If your paraphrase has the same order and the same rhythm as the original, it is still too close. Use the order method or the split method as well as word swaps.

The third mistake is paraphrasing without understanding. If you do not know what a sentence means, you cannot rewrite it. Read it again, look up the hard words, and explain it to yourself in plain words first. The paraphrase comes after the understanding, not before it.

The fourth mistake is trusting a tool blindly. Any helper can suggest a wrong word, and any reordered sentence can sound awkward. You are the writer. Check every suggestion, keep the good ones, and ignore the rest.

What it cannot do

This is a helper, not an AI paraphraser. It suggests words and orders — you do the rewriting.

It cannot rewrite a whole essay, and it does not try. It works on one sentence at a time. It cannot check whether your paraphrase is close to a source, or tell you whether your writing counts as plagiarism. For that, compare your work with the source yourself.

It does not understand meaning. Its synonym table holds real words with correct meanings, but it does not know which synonym fits your sentence. Only you know that. A word that is correct in one sentence can be wrong in another, so every suggestion needs your judgement.

It also cannot fix grammar, spelling, or style. If your sentence has a grammar mistake, the helper will carry that mistake into the reordered version. Read your final sentence aloud, check it once, and fix anything that sounds wrong. The helper is a starting point. The writing is yours.

Questions people ask

Is this paraphrasing tool really free?

Yes. It runs entirely in your browser, there is no signup, no download and no limit on how many sentences you can check.

Does this tool rewrite my sentence for me?

No. It suggests synonyms and a different word order, but you do the rewriting yourself. That is the point: changing a sentence by hand is how you learn to write in your own words.

Will paraphrasing help me avoid plagiarism?

Paraphrasing helps only when you truly rewrite the idea in your own words and still name the source. Changing a few words in a copied sentence is still copying, so always write from your own notes and give credit where it is due.

Can I use this tool on my phone?

Yes. The page works in any modern phone browser. Type or paste your sentence, tap Suggest, and tap any synonym to swap it into your text.

Why does the tool sometimes find no words to suggest?

The helper only suggests words that are in its synonym table, and it skips very short words. If your sentence is short or uses unusual words, try a longer sentence with common words.

Is my text sent to a server?

No. Everything happens on your own device in your browser. Your sentences never leave your phone or computer.
