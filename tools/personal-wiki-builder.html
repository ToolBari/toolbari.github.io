How to use the wiki

A wiki is a set of pages joined by links, and this builder keeps the idea as simple as it sounds. Type a page title, write the page, and save it. To link to another page, write its title inside double square brackets, like this: [[Garden jobs]]. When you open the page, that link is clickable — tap it and the wiki jumps to the linked page, or offers to create it if it does not exist yet.

The page list shows every page you have made, and the search box narrows it to pages whose title or text contains your words. Open a page to read it, edit it in the same editor, and save it again — the wiki always keeps the latest version. Delete a page with the delete button when you no longer need it.

Everything stays in this browser only, on this device. There is no account, no server, and no sharing — your pages are yours alone, and nobody else can see them. That also means there is no backup unless you make one: use the export button to copy your whole wiki as plain text, and paste it somewhere safe.

Linking pages so knowledge connects

The link is what turns a pile of notes into a wiki. A note about rose pruning that links to [[Garden jobs]] and [[March tasks]] is connected to your plans, not floating alone. When you open [[Garden jobs]], you can see the pruning note waiting there, and the knowledge finds you instead of waiting to be searched for.

Link generously, but link with meaning. A link should mean "this page helps you understand that page". Linking every word to something is noise; linking the ideas that genuinely relate is knowledge. A good rule is to link the first time you mention a topic that has — or deserves — its own page, and to leave the rest as plain text.

Links that point nowhere are not mistakes — they are invitations. When you write [[Composting]] and the page does not exist yet, the wiki shows the link in a different style, and tapping it opens the editor with that title ready to write. Some of the best pages in a wiki start as a link someone wrote before the page existed.

Over time, the links show you the shape of what you know. Pages with many links coming in are your central ideas. Pages with no links at all are orphans — worth linking in, or worth asking whether they belong in the wiki at all. The page list and the search box are your tools for finding orphans and giving them a home.

A starter structure (topics, projects, people)

If you are starting from nothing, three kinds of page will carry most of what you want to keep: topics, projects and people. A topic page holds what you know about a subject — "Rose pruning", "Bread baking", "Local history". A project page holds a thing you are doing — "Kitchen renovation", "Learning Spanish" — with its plans, decisions and next steps. A people page holds what you want to remember about a person — their interests, what you owe them, what you promised.

Start with one page of each kind, and link them. A project page that links to the topic pages it needs is already a small wiki, and it will grow naturally as you add what you learn. Do not plan the whole structure in advance: a wiki grows by writing, not by planning, and the structure that emerges from real pages is always better than one drawn up in the abstract.

Give every page a clear, plain title. "Rose pruning" is a good title; "Notes 3" is not. Titles are how you find pages and how you link to them, so a title should say what the page is about in the fewest words that still make sense. If you cannot title a page clearly, the page probably does not know what it is about yet.

Keeping it tidy over time

A wiki stays useful only if someone tends it, and that someone is you. Every few weeks, read through your page list and ask three questions: which pages are out of date, which pages say the same thing twice, and which pages have no links. Update the first, merge the second, and link in the third.

Merging is the most valuable tidy-up. When two pages grow to cover the same ground — "Rose pruning" and "Pruning roses" — pick the better title, move the good content into it, and delete the other. A wiki with one good page on a topic beats a wiki with three half-pages, because you always know where to look.

Do not be afraid to delete. A page that no longer matters is clutter, and clutter makes the pages that do matter harder to find. Delete with confidence: if the knowledge was worth keeping, it will come back, and if it does not come back, it was not worth keeping. The export button is there so you can keep a copy of everything before a big tidy-up, if that helps you be brave.

Writing pages that link well

Small pages link better than long ones. A page should cover one idea, one project or one person — not a whole subject. When a page grows past a few paragraphs, look for a section that deserves its own page, split it out, and link to it. Small pages are easier to find, easier to link to, and easier to keep up to date.

Write the first line as a summary. A reader — usually future you — should know what the page is about from its first sentence, without reading further. "Rose pruning: cut back in late winter, remove dead wood first, and always cut just above an outward-facing bud." That first line is the page in miniature, and it makes the whole wiki faster to use.

Write in your own words. A wiki is not a filing cabinet for other people's text; it is a record of what you know and think. Copying a paragraph from somewhere else teaches you nothing, and a page written in your own words is a page you will actually remember. Quote sparingly, and only when the exact words matter.

What this wiki cannot do

This wiki is a simple tool in your browser, and it is honest about its limits. It cannot sync between your phone and your computer — a page written on your phone stays on your phone. It cannot be shared with anyone, and it cannot be published. If you need a wiki other people can read, you need a different tool, and this page will not pretend otherwise.

It cannot search the internet, suggest links, or organise your pages for you. The links are yours to make, and the structure is yours to keep. That is deliberate: a personal wiki is a thinking tool, and the thinking is the point. A tool that organised everything for you would save you the work — and cost you the understanding.

It cannot keep your pages safe from a cleared browser. Everything lives in this browser's storage, and clearing your browser data removes it. Export your wiki regularly — the export button copies the whole wiki as plain text, which you can paste into a document or a note and keep anywhere you like.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is one giant page instead of small linked pages. A single page called "Everything about the garden" is a notebook, not a wiki — it cannot be linked to in parts, it is hard to search, and it grows until nobody reads it. Split it into pages, link them, and let the wiki do what it is for.

The second is links that point nowhere and stay nowhere. A missing page is fine when you first write the link, but if months pass and the page is still missing, the link is a promise you never kept. Either write the page, or remove the link. A wiki full of dead links is a wiki that teaches you not to trust its links.

The third is titles that mean nothing. "Notes", "Misc", "Stuff" — these are not titles, they are shrugs, and a page with a shrug for a title will never be found or linked to. Give every page a title that says what it is, in plain words.

The fourth is collecting without reviewing. A wiki grows by writing, but it only becomes knowledge when you read it back. Set aside time now and then to read through your pages, follow the links, and see what connects. That is when a wiki pays for itself — not while you are writing it, but while you are reading it.

The fifth is never exporting. A wiki that lives only in one browser is one cleared cache away from nothing. Export it, paste it somewhere safe, and do it often enough that losing the browser would be an inconvenience, not a disaster.

Questions people ask

Is my wiki uploaded anywhere?

No. Your pages stay in this browser only, on your own device, and this page does not connect to any server. If you clear your browser data, your wiki will be removed, so use the export button to keep a backup.

How do links between pages work?

Write a page title inside double square brackets, like [[Garden jobs]], and it becomes a clickable link. Tapping it opens that page — or offers to create it if it does not exist yet. Links to missing pages look different, so you can spot them.

Can I use this on my phone?

Yes. The wiki is a single web page that works in your phone's browser, with big buttons and no tiny controls. Your pages stay on the phone you wrote them on — they do not sync to your computer.

How many pages can I make?

There is no fixed limit on this page — your browser's storage is the only constraint, and for an ordinary personal wiki you will not reach it. Hundreds of small pages are comfortable; thousands would be slow to search.

Can I export my wiki?

Yes. The export button copies your whole wiki as plain text, with each page under its title, which you can paste into a document or a note. Export regularly, so a cleared browser never costs you your pages.

What should I put in a personal wiki?

Anything you want to remember and connect: topics you are learning, projects you are running, people you know, decisions you made and why. Start with one page of each kind, link them together, and let it grow from there.
