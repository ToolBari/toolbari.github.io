How to use this guide

Background noise is any sound in a recording that is not the voice you want: hiss, hum, a fan, wind, the echo of a bare room, or traffic outside a window. Each kind of noise has a fix that works for it, and the wrong fix either does nothing or makes the voice sound worse. This guide explains each noise in plain words, gives you three complete methods for removing noise, and ends with the best advice of all: how to record clean audio in the first place.

The picker at the top of this page asks what kind of noise you hear and recommends the fix that works for it. If you already know your noise, jump straight to the method that fits your device. If you are not sure what the noise is, read the noises section first, because naming the noise correctly is half the job.

The noises explained

Hiss is the soft shhh sound you hear in a quiet recording, like air escaping from a tyre. It comes from the microphone and the electronics in the phone or computer, and it gets louder when the recording is quiet, because turning the volume up turns the hiss up too. Hiss is the easiest noise to reduce, because it is steady, and a noise reduction tool can learn it from a quiet second and remove it.

Hum is a low steady buzz, usually from mains electricity or a nearby appliance. It sounds like a single low note that never changes, and it is most common near a power adapter, a fridge, or a fluorescent light. Hum is easy to remove, because it sits at one steady frequency, and a filter can cut that frequency while leaving the voice alone.

Wind noise is a low rumbling that comes and goes as air moves across the microphone, and it is the hardest noise to fix afterwards, because it changes all the time. Room echo is the hollow sound of a voice bouncing off bare walls. Echo is also hard to fix afterwards, and the best cure is a smaller, softer room, or a microphone closer to the speaker.

Fan noise is a steady whir, usually from a ceiling fan, an air conditioner, or a computer fan, and it behaves like hiss: steady and easy to learn and remove. Street noise is everything from outside: traffic, horns, dogs, and people talking. It changes all the time, so record at a quiet time of day and close the windows.

Method 1: Clean audio on your phone

Cleaning audio on a phone is the quickest method, and it is good enough for voice notes, short recordings, and clips for social media. Free audio editing apps for Android and iPhone include a noise reduction feature: open the recording, find the noise reduction or denoise option, and let the app clean it. Start with the lightest setting and listen before you go stronger.

  1. Open the recording in a free audio editing app on your phone.
  2. Find the noise reduction or denoise option in the effects or tools menu.
  3. If the app asks for a noise sample, select a second of the recording where nobody is speaking.
  4. Apply a light amount of noise reduction first, then listen to the result.
  5. If noise remains, apply a little more, but stop as soon as the voice starts to sound strange.
  6. Save the cleaned recording as a new file and keep the original.

Method 2: Use a free desktop app

If the recording matters, or if the noise is heavy, a free desktop app gives the best result. The best-known free choice is Audacity, a free and open-source audio editor for Windows, Mac and Linux.

Audacity noise reduction works in two steps. First, you select a short part of the recording where nobody is speaking, and Audacity learns what the noise sounds like. This is called the noise profile, and it is the key to the whole method. Second, you select the whole recording and apply noise reduction, and Audacity removes the sound it learned while leaving the voice alone. A quiet second at the start makes the best sample, so leave a short pause before you start speaking.

  1. Install Audacity from its official website and open your recording in it.
  2. Select a second or two where nobody is speaking, then open Effect, Noise Removal and Repair, Noise Reduction, and choose Get Noise Profile.
  3. Select the whole recording, open the same Noise Reduction effect again, and press OK with the default settings.
  4. Listen to the result. If noise remains, undo and try slightly stronger settings, one step at a time.
  5. Export the cleaned recording as a new file and keep the original safe.

The default settings in Audacity are a good starting point, and for most recordings they are enough. The strength setting decides how much noise is removed, and raising it too far eats into the voice, so change one setting at a time and listen after each change. Audacity also has a notch filter for hum and a high-pass filter for wind rumble.

Method 3: Use a free online tool

An online noise removal tool is a good choice when you need to clean one recording quickly. You open the tool in your browser, choose your audio file, wait while the service cleans it, and download the result. Free online tools handle hiss, hum and fan noise well, and most of them work in a phone browser too. Their free plans have limits, such as a maximum file length or a number of files per day.

The steps are nearly the same on every online tool. First, open the tool and choose your audio file. Second, pick the kind of noise if the tool asks, or let it detect the noise itself. Third, choose the cleaning strength, starting with the lightest option. Fourth, wait for the cleaning to finish and download the file. Fifth, keep the original recording.

Online tools have one weakness: your recording travels to their server, so they are not the right choice for private recordings, such as a personal voice note or a confidential interview. For those, use a phone app or a desktop app that works offline. Online tools are also less flexible, because you cannot choose a noise sample yourself.

Recording clean audio in the first place

The best noise reduction is a quiet recording. Every minute spent on a clean recording saves ten minutes of cleaning afterwards. Record in the smallest, softest room you can find: a bedroom with curtains and a bed absorbs sound, while a bare room with hard walls echoes. Close the windows and the door, turn off the fan and the air conditioner, and move away from the fridge and the computer fan.

Move the microphone closer to the speaker. The voice gets louder when the microphone is close, while the background noise stays the same, so the voice stands out more clearly. A handspan of distance is a good starting point for a phone microphone. Hold the phone steady, or rest it on a soft cloth. Leave a second of silence at the start, because that quiet second is the noise sample a cleaning tool needs later.

Check the recording as you go. Record ten seconds, play it back with headphones, and listen for noise before you record the whole thing. Headphones reveal hiss and hum that a phone speaker hides, and a ten-second test catches a noisy fan while there is still time to fix it.

How much cleaning is too much

Noise reduction is easy to overdo, and the sign is unmistakable: the voice starts to sound underwater, as if the speaker is talking through a pipe, with a warbling, robotic quality that is worse than the original noise. This happens when the tool eats into the voice itself. If you hear that sound, you have cleaned too hard. Undo the last step and try again with lighter settings.

The rule is to clean in small steps and listen after each one. Apply a light amount of noise reduction, listen to the result with headphones, and only then decide whether more is needed. Two light passes are usually better than one heavy pass. Stop when the noise is quiet enough that a listener would not notice it. A faint trace of noise and a natural-sounding voice is better than a perfectly silent recording with a voice that sounds like a robot.

Some recordings cannot be saved. A recording made in strong wind, or a whisper recorded from far away, will not sound clean no matter how hard you clean it. In those cases, record again in a quieter place, with the microphone closer to the speaker.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is cleaning the same file five times. Each pass removes a little more of the voice, and after several passes the voice sounds thin and robotic. Clean once, lightly, from the original, and keep it safe. The second mistake is expecting a windy phone recording to sound like a studio. Wind and echo change all the time, and no tool can make a windy recording sound like a studio recording. Record again in a quiet place instead of fighting the file.

Another common mistake is cleaning before listening. Always play the original recording with headphones first, so you know what the noise actually is. Cleaning hiss with a hum filter does nothing, and cleaning a recording that was already clean only damages the voice. People also forget to keep the original, and then cannot start again. Keep the original, work on a copy, and give the cleaned file a clear name.

Questions people ask

Can I remove noise from a voice note?

Yes. A free audio editing app on your phone can remove hiss and fan noise from a voice note in a few taps. Open the voice note in the app, use the noise reduction option on a light setting, and listen to the result before you save it. Keep the original voice note in case you want to try again.

Why does my voice sound strange after cleaning?

You cleaned too hard. Heavy noise reduction eats into the voice itself, and the result sounds underwater or robotic. Undo the last step and clean again with lighter settings, in small steps, listening after each one. Two light passes sound better than one heavy pass.

What is the best free noise reducer?

For most recordings, Audacity on a computer gives the best free result, because you can choose a noise sample and control the strength. For a quick clean on a phone, a free audio editing app is enough. The best tool depends on the noise, so the picker at the top of this page recommends a fix for each kind.

Can I clean audio on my phone?

Yes. Free audio editing apps for Android and iPhone include noise reduction, and free online tools work in a phone browser too. Phone methods handle hiss and fan noise well. For heavy noise or a long recording, a desktop app gives a better result.

How do I stop fan noise while recording?

Turn the fan off for the few minutes of the recording, which is the simplest fix. If you cannot turn it off, move away from the fan, point the microphone away from it, and move the microphone closer to the speaker so the voice stands out over the noise.

Will noise reduction fix a very quiet recording?

No, not really. Turning a quiet recording up makes the noise louder too, and cleaning then eats into the voice. If the voice is very quiet, record again with the microphone closer to the speaker. Cleaning can rescue a good recording with a little noise, but it cannot rescue a recording where the voice is barely there.
