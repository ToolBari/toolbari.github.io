Property Rental Yield Calculator

Work out whether a rental property pays its way. Enter the price, the monthly rent and the yearly costs, and the calculator shows the gross yield, the net yield after costs, and how many years the rent would take to pay the price back.

Gross yield = yearly rent / price x 100. Net yield = (yearly rent - yearly costs) / price x 100. Payback years = price / net yearly income. All amounts are plain numbers in your own currency.

A yield is arithmetic, not a promise — rents can fall, properties can sit empty, and costs can rise. This page does not give investment advice, and a high yield on paper can hide a property that is hard to let or expensive to keep.

How to use this calculator

Enter the price of the property, the monthly rent you expect, and the yearly costs of owning and letting it — tax, insurance, maintenance, management fees and anything else you pay each year. The calculator shows the gross yield, the net yield after costs, and the payback years: how long the net rent would take to repay the price.

Use realistic numbers, not hopeful ones. The rent is what the property would actually let for in its area, not what you wish it would let for — check similar properties nearby before you enter a figure. The costs are what you actually pay each year, including the irregular ones: a roof, a boiler and a vacant month all belong in the yearly average, even if they do not happen every year.

Run the calculator more than once. Try a lower rent, a higher cost figure, and a month of vacancy, and see how the yield moves. A property that only works at the most optimistic numbers is a property that fails at the first surprise.

Gross yield vs net yield

Gross yield is the simple figure: yearly rent divided by the price, times one hundred. A property costing 200,000 that rents for 1,200 a month brings in 14,400 a year, so the gross yield is 7.2 percent. Gross yield is useful for a first comparison between properties, because it needs only two numbers.

Net yield is the honest figure: yearly rent minus yearly costs, divided by the price. The same property with 2,400 a year of costs keeps 12,000, so the net yield is 6 percent. The gap between gross and net is the cost of owning the property, and it is where cheap properties often disappoint — a low price with high maintenance can have a fine gross yield and a poor net one.

Always compare net yields when you compare properties, and make sure the costs are counted the same way in each. A net yield that leaves out maintenance is not a net yield, and a comparison between two such figures is a comparison between two guesses.

Worked example: a small flat

Suppose a small flat costs 200,000, rents for 1,200 a month, and costs 2,400 a year to own and let. The yearly rent is 1,200 times 12, which is 14,400. The gross yield is 14,400 divided by 200,000 times 100, which is 7.2 percent.

The net yearly income is 14,400 minus 2,400, which is 12,000. The net yield is 12,000 divided by 200,000 times 100, which is 6 percent. The payback years are 200,000 divided by 12,000, which is 16.7 years — the time the net rent would take to repay the price, if nothing changed.

Now suppose the flat sits empty for one month a year. The rent falls to 13,200, the net income to 10,800, the net yield to 5.4 percent, and the payback stretches to 18.5 years. One empty month costs more than it looks, which is why voids belong in the calculation from the start.

What counts as a cost

Yearly costs are everything you pay to own and let the property, averaged over a year. The regular ones are easy: property tax, insurance, management fees, ground rent or service charges, and the cost of finding tenants. The irregular ones are the ones people forget: repairs, replacing appliances, repainting between tenants, and the legal costs of a tenancy.

Maintenance deserves a line of its own in your thinking, even if it shares a box here. Roofs, boilers, windows and kitchens all wear out on their own schedules, and a year with no repairs is not a year with no maintenance cost — it is a year the bill is deferred. Setting aside a fixed amount each year for maintenance, even when nothing breaks, is how the cost stays honest.

A mortgage is a financing cost, not an ownership cost, and this calculator leaves it out on purpose. The yield measures the property; the mortgage measures how you paid for it. If you want to know whether the rent covers the mortgage as well, compare the net yearly income with the yearly mortgage payments separately.

What payback years mean

Payback years answer a simple question: if the net rent stayed exactly as it is, how long would it take to add up to the price? A payback of 16.7 years means sixteen and a half years of net rent equals the purchase price. It is a way of feeling the size of the investment, not a promise — rents change, costs change, and the property itself changes value.

A shorter payback means the rent repays the price faster, which usually means a higher yield. A longer payback means the rent is a smaller return on the price, and the investment depends more on the property gaining value over time. Neither is automatically better: a long payback in a growing area can beat a short payback in a declining one.

If the costs are higher than the rent, there is no payback at all — the calculator says so plainly. A property that loses money every year needs a reason beyond the rent to be worth holding, and that reason should be examined with care, not assumed.

Yield and the mortgage

This calculator leaves the mortgage out on purpose, because the yield measures the property and the mortgage measures how you paid for it. The same flat has the same yield whether you buy it with cash or with a loan — what changes is how much of the rent you keep after the mortgage is paid.

To check the mortgage, compare the net yearly income with the yearly mortgage payments. In the worked example the net income is 12,000 a year, or 1,000 a month. If the mortgage costs 700 a month, the property covers it with 300 a month to spare; if the mortgage costs 1,100 a month, the rent does not cover it, and you pay the difference out of your own pocket every month.

A property that does not cover its mortgage is not automatically a bad investment — it may gain in value, or the rent may rise — but it is a property you subsidise, and you should know that before you buy, not after. Run the yield first, then the mortgage comparison, and keep the two questions separate: is this a good property, and is this a good way to pay for it.

What a good yield looks like

There is no single good yield. Yields vary widely by city, by country and by year: a yield that is ordinary in one place is remarkable in another, and a yield that was good five years ago may be poor today. Anyone who quotes a single number as the threshold for a good property is selling a rule of thumb, not a fact.

What you can do is compare. Look at similar properties in the same area, worked out the same way, and see where yours sits. A property whose net yield is well below its neighbours needs an explanation — a better tenant, a rising area, a lower price than the asking figure — and a property well above its neighbours needs one too, because a yield that looks too good usually hides a cost or a risk.

Remember that yield is only half the return. The other half is what happens to the property's value, and this calculator says nothing about that, because nobody can predict it. A low yield with rising values and a high yield with falling values can end in the same place, and the yield alone cannot tell you which you are buying.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is using gross yield to decide. Gross yield ignores costs, and costs are where properties differ most. Compare net yields, with the same costs counted in each.

The second mistake is forgetting voids. A property that is empty for a month a year earns eleven months of rent, not twelve, and the costs continue while it is empty. Build the vacancy into the rent or the costs before you trust the yield.

The third mistake is entering the asking rent instead of the achievable rent. Check what similar properties actually let for, not what the listing hopes for. A yield built on an optimistic rent is a story, not a calculation.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you whether to buy a property. It divides the numbers you enter and shows the yield and the payback — it cannot judge the area, the tenants, the condition of the building, or the direction of prices. Those are the things that decide whether a rental succeeds, and they are not numbers in a box.

It also cannot predict the future. Rents rise and fall, costs rise and fall, and a yield calculated today is a snapshot, not a guarantee. Use it to compare properties honestly, then make the decision with everything else you know.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between gross and net yield?

Gross yield is yearly rent divided by price, with no costs taken off. Net yield subtracts the yearly costs first, so it shows what the property actually keeps. Net yield is the figure to compare between properties, because costs differ so much.

What costs should I include?

Everything you pay each year to own and let the property: tax, insurance, maintenance, management fees, service charges, and the cost of finding tenants. Average the irregular costs, such as repairs and repainting, across the years. A mortgage is a financing cost and is left out on purpose.

Should I allow for empty months?

Yes. A property that is empty for a month a year earns eleven months of rent, not twelve, while the costs continue. Either reduce the rent you enter, or add the lost rent to the yearly costs — the calculator works either way, as long as the void is counted once.

Should I include the mortgage in the costs?

No — this calculator measures the property, not how you paid for it. The yield is the same whether you buy with cash or with a loan. To check whether the rent covers the mortgage, compare the net yearly income with your yearly mortgage payments separately.

What is a good rental yield?

There is no single good number — yields vary widely by place and by year. Compare similar properties in the same area, worked out the same way, and see where a property sits among its neighbours. A yield far above or far below the local range deserves a closer look.

Does the calculator include house price growth?

No. It measures the rent against the price, and says nothing about what the property will be worth later, because nobody can predict that. The total return on a property is the rent plus any change in value, and this page covers only the rent half.
