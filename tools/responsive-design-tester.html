How to use this tester

There are two ways to preview a page. If the page is already online, type its web address into the box and tap Preview this address — the page loads into all three frames at once, at 360, 768 and 1280 pixels wide. If the page is still on your computer, or if the live site blocks framing, paste the full HTML into the second box and tap Preview pasted HTML instead: the code runs on your own device, so it always works.

The Load a sample page button fills the paste box with a small sample page that has a heading, a picture area, two columns and a button — a page that looks fine on desktop and breaks in the usual ways on phone. Use it to learn what to look for: the columns should stack on the phone frame, the button should stay big enough to tap, and nothing should stick out past the edge of the frame. Once you know the pattern, test your own pages the same way.

Work through the frames from narrow to wide. The phone frame is the strictest test: if the page works at 360 pixels, it usually works everywhere else. Check that text is readable without zooming, that buttons are big enough to tap with a thumb, and that nothing forces sideways scrolling. Then check the tablet frame for the in-between sizes, where layouts often look stretched or cramped, and finish with the desktop frame to make sure the wide version still looks deliberate rather than empty.

How to read the three widths

The 360 pixel frame stands for the common Android phone held upright. It is the width most of your visitors will use if your audience is mostly on phones, and it is the hardest width to get right, because there is so little room. A page that works at 360 pixels works on almost every phone, because most phones are at least that wide. If you only test one width, test this one.

The 768 pixel frame stands for a tablet held upright, and for the narrow half of a laptop screen when the browser window is not full width. It catches the mistakes that only show up in the middle: text columns that stretch too wide to read comfortably, images that sit awkwardly beside text, and navigation bars that wrap onto two lines. Many pages are built for phone and desktop and forgotten in the middle, so this frame often shows the most surprises.

The 1280 pixel frame stands for a normal laptop or desktop screen. Here the question is not whether things fit, but whether they use the space well: lines of text that run the full width of the screen are hard to read, and a single narrow column floating in a wide empty page looks unfinished. Good desktop layouts limit the text width, use the extra space for side content or breathing room, and keep the main action visible without scrolling.

Common responsive mistakes

The most common mistake is a fixed width somewhere in the page. A box set to 800 pixels wide, an image with a fixed size, or a table that cannot shrink will all push past the edge of a 360 pixel screen and force sideways scrolling. The phone frame shows this at once: if you can scroll sideways inside the frame, something on the page has a fixed width that is too big. The fix is to let widths be flexible — a maximum width instead of a fixed one, and images that scale down to fit their container.

The second mistake is tap targets that are too small. A link or button that is easy to click with a mouse can be almost impossible to tap with a thumb, especially if several small links sit close together. On the phone frame, ask whether you could tap each button with one thumb while walking: if not, make the buttons taller and give them space. A good rule is at least 44 pixels tall for a tap target, and 72 pixels is comfortable for the main actions.

The third mistake is text that is too small or too wide. Body text below about 16 pixels is hard to read on a phone, and visitors will not pinch to zoom — they will leave. Text that runs the full width of a desktop screen is hard to read for the opposite reason: the eye loses its place on the way back to the start of the next line. The three frames show both problems at once, which is why testing at three widths beats testing at one.

How to fix each mistake

Fix sideways scrolling first, because it breaks everything else. Find the element that sticks out — in a browser's developer tools, or by removing parts of the page until the scrolling stops — and give it a flexible width. Images need a maximum width of 100 percent of their container. Tables need a wrapper that scrolls on its own, so the table can be wide while the page stays put. Long words and links need a rule that lets them break, or they will push their container wider than the screen.

Fix tap targets next. Make buttons at least 44 pixels tall, with clear space around them, and make the whole button tappable, not just the text inside it. If a row of small links is hard to tap, turn it into a stacked list on phone widths, with each link on its own line. Check the result in the phone frame with your thumb: if you can tap each action without hitting its neighbour, it is big enough.

Fix the text last, because it is the easiest to judge. Set body text to at least 16 pixels, and keep the line length comfortable — around 60 to 75 characters per line on desktop, and whatever fits naturally on phone. Headings should shrink on small screens instead of wrapping onto five lines, and the space between lines should be generous enough that the text breathes. Read the page in the phone frame yourself: if it feels like work, your visitors will feel it too.

Why some sites refuse to load

Some sites send a header that tells the browser never to show them inside a frame on another site. The header is called X-Frame-Options, and sites use it to protect themselves against a trick called clickjacking, where a bad page frames a real site and tricks visitors into clicking things they cannot see. When a site sends that header, the browser shows a blank frame instead of the page — and there is no setting in this tool that can override it, because the browser is following the site's own security rule.

A blank frame is therefore not a bug in this tester, and it does not mean the site is broken. It means the site has chosen not to be framed, and the honest thing to do is respect that choice. The sites that block framing are usually the big ones — banks, social networks, search engines — while smaller sites and your own pages usually load fine. If you run the site yourself and want it to load here, the setting lives in your own server's headers, not in this tool.

The paste-HTML mode exists for exactly this situation. If you have the page's HTML — your own page, a page you are building, or a page you saved — paste it into the box and it will preview at all three widths, because the code runs on your own device and no framing rule applies. That is also why the paste mode is the best way to test a page that is not published yet: you do not need to put it online to see how it behaves at phone width.

What this tester cannot do

This tester shows widths, not phones. It cannot show you how a page feels on a real phone in your hand, how it behaves when the keyboard opens, or how it loads on a slow mobile connection. It cannot test touch gestures, the way a phone's browser hides its address bar, or how the page looks in a specific phone's browser with its own quirks. For those questions there is no substitute for opening the page on a real phone — this tool is for the quick check that catches most mistakes before that step.

It also cannot test pages that need a login, pages that load their content from other services after the page opens, or pages that behave differently depending on who is visiting. A preview frame is a plain view of the page as a first-time visitor sees it, and that is its limit as well as its strength. Use it to check layout at three widths, to catch sideways scrolling and tiny tap targets, and to compare your page with itself at different sizes — then finish the job on a real device.

Questions people ask

Why does a site look fine on desktop but broken on phone?

Because a desktop screen is three or four times wider than a phone, so a layout with fixed widths, side-by-side columns and small text fits on desktop and overflows on phone. The phone frame in this tester shows the same page at 360 pixels wide, where those problems become obvious.

What width should I design for first?

Design for the phone first — 360 pixels wide — then add space as the screen grows. It is easier to stretch a good narrow layout than to squeeze a wide one, and most visitors arrive on phones, so the narrow layout is the one most people will actually see.

Why do some sites refuse to load in the preview?

They send a security header called X-Frame-Options that tells the browser never to show them inside a frame on another site. It protects them against clickjacking. The blank frame is the site's own rule, not a bug — use the paste-HTML mode to test pages you own.

Is 360px the right phone width to test?

Yes, for most audiences. 360 pixels is the common width of an Android phone held upright, and most phones are at least that wide. A page that works at 360 pixels works on almost every phone; a page that only works at 400 pixels will break for a large share of visitors.

How do I fix sideways scrolling?

Find the element that is wider than the screen — usually a fixed-width box, an image, or a table — and give it a flexible width. Images need a maximum width of 100 percent of their container, and tables need a wrapper that scrolls on its own so the page stays put.

Does this test real phones or only widths?

Only widths. It shows how the layout behaves at three screen widths, which catches most responsive mistakes. It cannot show touch feel, slow connections, or a specific phone browser's quirks — for those, open the page on a real phone after the quick check here.
