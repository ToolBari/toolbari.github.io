Pixels, DPI and print size

The whole subject comes down to one formula: print size in inches equals pixels divided by DPI. A photo 6000 pixels wide printed at 300 DPI is 6000 divided by 300, which is 20 inches wide. The same photo at 150 DPI is 6000 divided by 150, which is 40 inches wide. The pixels do not change; the DPI decides how tightly they are packed, and the packing decides the size.

Centimetres follow from inches: one inch is exactly 2.54 centimetres, so 20 inches is 50.8 centimetres. The calculator shows both, because paper sizes are sold in inches in some countries and in centimetres or millimetres in others. A4 paper, for example, is 210 by 297 millimetres, which is 8.27 by 11.69 inches — and knowing both forms saves a conversion every time you order a print.

DPI is often confused with the quality of the photo, but it is really a printing instruction: it says how many pixels the printer should squeeze into each inch. A high DPI packs the pixels tightly and gives a sharp, small print; a low DPI spreads them out and gives a softer, larger print. The photo's own sharpness is separate — a blurry photo stays blurry at any DPI, and a sharp photo can look good at a lower DPI than the textbooks suggest.

Home prints vs lab prints

For a lab print, 300 DPI is the usual guidance: it is the resolution most labs print at, and it gives a sharp result at normal viewing distance. A photo that meets the 300 DPI pixel figures in the table above will print well at that size in a lab. Below 300 DPI, a lab can still print the photo — the printer simply spreads the pixels further — but the result softens as the effective DPI falls.

For a home print, the same guidance applies, with one difference: home printers and home viewing are more forgiving than people expect. A large print hung on a wall is viewed from a metre or more away, and at that distance 150 DPI looks sharp to the eye, because the eye cannot resolve the individual dots from that far. The old rule is that the bigger the print, the farther you stand, and the lower the DPI you need — which is why billboards print at a DPI that would look terrible on a postcard.

So the practical advice is: use 300 DPI for prints you will hold in your hands (4 by 6, 5 by 7, 8 by 10), and accept 150 to 200 DPI for large prints you will view from a distance (A3 and above). Those are typical figures, not rules — a sharp photo at 150 DPI often looks better than a soft one at 300, because the photo matters more than the number.

Worked example

Take a photo 6000 by 4000 pixels — a 24 megapixel camera — and print it at 300 DPI. The width is 6000 divided by 300, which is 20 inches, or 50.8 centimetres. The height is 4000 divided by 300, which is 13.33 inches, or 33.9 centimetres. The aspect ratio is 6000 to 4000, which simplifies to 3 to 2. The megapixels are 6000 times 4000 divided by one million, which is 24.

At 300 DPI, that photo fills every size in the table: 4 by 6, 5 by 7, 8 by 10, A4 and A3 all fit, because 6000 by 4000 covers the largest requirement (3507 by 4962 for A3) in both directions — 6000 is more than 4962, and 4000 is more than 3507. In fact it could print larger still: at 300 DPI it reaches 20 by 13.3 inches, which is bigger than A3.

Now print the same photo at 150 DPI. The width becomes 6000 divided by 150, which is 40 inches, and the height becomes 26.67 inches — a poster-sized print, perfectly reasonable for a wall. The pixels are the same; only the packing has changed. That is the whole lesson of this calculator in one example: DPI is a choice about size and sharpness, not a property of the photo.

Aspect ratio and cropping

Aspect ratio is the shape of your photo: the width divided by the height. Most cameras shoot at 3 to 2 (6000 by 4000, for example), phones often shoot at 4 to 3, and some cameras offer 16 to 9. The print sizes have their own shapes, and when the shapes do not match, something has to give: either the photo is cropped to fit the paper, or the paper shows white borders around the photo.

A 3 to 2 photo fits a 4 by 6 print exactly, because 4 by 6 is also 2 to 3. It does not fit an 8 by 10 print, which is 4 to 5: printing it there crops about a sixth of the width from the sides, or leaves borders top and bottom. An A4 print crops a 3 to 2 photo by a smaller amount. The calculator shows your aspect ratio so you can spot these mismatches before you order, and the fits list only counts a size as fitting if the pixels cover it — cropping is a separate decision, and it is yours.

If cropping matters — a group photo where someone stands at the edge, for example — pick a print size that matches your photo's shape, or ask the lab for a border rather than a crop. If it does not matter, any size that fits will do, and the crop will take a little from the edges that nobody misses.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is confusing DPI with quality. A photo saved at 72 DPI and a photo saved at 300 DPI can be the same photo: the DPI number stored in the file is only a printing hint, and it does not change the pixels. What matters is the pixel count. A 6000 by 4000 photo is a 6000 by 4000 photo whatever DPI is written in its file, and this calculator works from the pixels, not from the stored hint.

The second mistake is ordering a print bigger than the pixels support and expecting sharpness. A small photo printed very large spreads its pixels thin, and no printer can invent detail that is not there. The fits list exists to prevent this: if a size does not fit at your chosen DPI, either pick a smaller size or accept a lower effective DPI with your eyes open.

The third mistake is forgetting the crop. An 8 by 10 print of a 3 to 2 photo loses part of the sides, and the lost part is often the part you wanted — a face at the edge of a group, or a branch that balanced the composition. Check the aspect ratio before you order, and if the shapes do not match, decide in advance whether you want a crop or a border.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you how good a print will look — it can only tell you the size and whether the pixels cover it. Sharpness depends on the photo itself (was it in focus, was the lens clean, was the light good), on the paper (glossy shows more detail than matte), and on the printer. A sharp photo at 150 DPI can look better than a soft one at 300, and no calculator can judge that for you.

It also cannot account for the printer's own limits. Some printers add a border, some trim the edges slightly, and some labs crop automatically to fit the paper. The numbers here are the arithmetic of pixels and DPI; the finished print depends on the printer too. For an important print, order a small test first — it is cheaper than a large disappointment.

Questions people ask

What DPI should I use for printing?

300 DPI is the usual choice for a lab print and for prints you will hold in your hands, such as 4 by 6 or 8 by 10. For a large print viewed from a distance, such as A3 on a wall, 150 to 200 DPI is often fine, because the eye cannot resolve the dots from that far. These are typical figures, not rules.

How big can I print my phone photo?

Divide the pixel size by the DPI. A phone photo 4000 by 3000 pixels prints at 13.3 by 10 inches at 300 DPI, or 26.7 by 20 inches at 150 DPI. Enter your photo's pixel size in the calculator to see its exact print size and which common sizes it can fill.

What is the difference between DPI and PPI?

DPI (dots per inch) is about printing: how many ink dots the printer puts in each inch. PPI (pixels per inch) is about screens and files: how many pixels sit in each inch of an image. People use the two words interchangeably, and for print-size arithmetic they behave the same way: size in inches equals pixels divided by the number.

Why does my 4 by 6 print crop my photo?

Because the shapes do not match. A 4 by 6 print is 2 to 3, and most cameras shoot 3 to 2, so they match — but a phone photo at 4 to 3 does not match 4 by 6, so part of it must be cropped or bordered. The calculator shows your photo's aspect ratio so you can spot the mismatch before you order.

Is 150 DPI good enough?

For a large print viewed from a distance, usually yes. The bigger the print, the farther you stand, and the lower the DPI you need. For a small print held in your hands, 300 DPI is the safer choice. A sharp photo at 150 DPI often looks better than a soft one at 300, because the photo matters more than the number.

How many megapixels do I need for A3?

A3 is 11.69 by 16.54 inches, so at 300 DPI it needs about 3507 by 4962 pixels — about 17.4 megapixels. At 150 DPI it needs about 1754 by 2481 pixels — about 4.4 megapixels. Most modern cameras and phones clear the 150 DPI figure easily; the 300 DPI figure needs a 17 megapixel photo or larger.
