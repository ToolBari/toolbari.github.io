What a keyword is, in plain words

A keyword is the word or phrase a person types into a search box when they are looking for something. The word coffee is a keyword, and so is the phrase how to make coffee at home. The second one is longer and more specific, and it tells you much more about what the person wants. They are not looking for a definition of coffee; they want steps they can follow in their own kitchen.

Search engines match pages to keywords. When someone searches, the search engine looks through its index for pages that answer that search, and shows the ones it believes are the best match. If your page answers the search clearly and completely, the search engine may show your page to that person. If your page does not match what the person asked for, it will not be shown, no matter how well it is written.

Keywords are not magic words you sprinkle on a page. They are a way of understanding what people are looking for, so you can write the page they actually need. The research part is the thinking: which searches can I answer well, which ones match my page, and which ones are realistic for a site like mine. This tool gives you the raw ideas; the thinking is yours, and it is the part that matters.

The idea groups, explained

The tool sorts its ideas into six groups, and each group matches a different kind of search. Knowing the groups helps you pick ideas that fit the page you plan to write, instead of picking at random.

  • Questions. Ideas that start with what, how, why, where, when, or is. People searching with a question want an answer, and they usually want it quickly. A page built around a question keyword should answer that question near the top, in plain words, and then explain it in more detail below. Question pages are a good place to start, because the reader's need is clear.
  • Comparisons. Ideas that compare one thing with another. People making a comparison are close to a decision: they want to know which option fits them better. A comparison page should be fair, list the real differences, and say plainly who each option suits. A page that pretends one option wins on everything is not a comparison, and readers can tell.
  • Best. Ideas that ask for the best option. People typing best want a recommendation they can trust. A page for a best keyword needs a real opinion, backed by clear reasons, and it should say who the recommendation is for. Never call something the best unless you can explain why, in words a reader can check.
  • How to. Ideas that ask for steps. People typing how to want to do something, and they want instructions they can follow. A how-to page should give the steps in order, keep each step short, and say what the reader needs before they start. These pages are some of the most useful pages a site can have.
  • Near me. Ideas from people looking for something close by: a shop, a service, a class, a place to visit. Near me searches are for local businesses and local pages, and the page should say clearly where the thing is and when it is open. If your site is not tied to a place, skip this group.
  • Long-tail. Longer, more specific phrases, usually four words or more. Each one is searched less often than a short keyword, but the people who type them know exactly what they want, and a page that matches them exactly is very likely to be the page they open. For a new site, long-tail phrases are usually the best place to begin.

A good page usually matches one group. A how-to page fits a how-to keyword, and a comparison page fits a comparison keyword. When the page and the keyword match, the reader finds what they expected, stays on the page, and the search engine notices. When they do not match, the reader leaves quickly, and that is a signal the page did not answer the search.

Picking keywords a new site can actually rank for

A new site cannot win the short, popular words. Big sites have held them for years, with many pages and many links, and a new page will not push them aside in a week. That is not a reason to give up; it is a reason to pick your battles. The short words are the hardest fight on the internet, and the long specific phrases are the fights a new site can actually win.

Pick long and specific phrases, three words or more, that match your page exactly. A page about making cold coffee at home in summer has a far better chance with a phrase like that than with the single word coffee. The specific phrase has less competition, and the people who type it want exactly the page you wrote. A hundred readers who wanted your page are worth more than a thousand who arrived by accident and left.

Match the intent behind the search. Intent is a simple idea: what does the person actually want. If they want a guide, write a guide. If they want to buy something, show the product. If they want an answer, give the answer first. A page that matches the intent keeps its readers; a page that does not match it loses them in seconds, and no amount of keyword repetition will save it.

Give each page one main keyword. Trying to rank one page for ten keywords makes it weak for all of them, because the page ends up saying a little about everything and enough about nothing. One page, one main keyword, and a page that answers that search properly from the first paragraph to the last. If you have ten keywords, you have the start of ten pages, and that is a good problem to have.

After you have your ideas, check the numbers with a free tool that shows real data. Google Keyword Planner and Google Search Console are the official free tools for this, and they are named here as plain text so you can find them yourself. Use them to check which of your ideas people actually search for, and how much competition each one has, before you spend a day writing the page.

What it cannot do

This tool generates keyword IDEAS from patterns. It does not show live search volume or competition.

A static page cannot pull live search data, because that data lives inside the search engine's own systems, and no page like this one can reach it. Any page that claims to show real search volume from a simple form is either guessing or pretending, and this page does neither. The ideas here are built from the patterns real searches follow, which makes them useful for planning, but they are a starting point, not a measurement.

To check volume and competition, use the free official tools: Google Keyword Planner shows search volume ranges for keywords, and Google Search Console shows the searches that already bring people to your site. Both are free, both come from the search engine itself, and both are worth learning if you plan to write for search more than once.

The tool also cannot tell you which keyword to choose. That choice depends on your page, your readers, and what you can write well. An idea that fits another site may not fit yours, and the best keyword for your page is the one you can answer honestly and completely. Pick ideas you can stand behind, and skip the ones that would force you to write a page you do not believe in.

Common mistakes

  • Chasing only big keywords. The short, popular words look tempting because many people search for them, but a new site will not win them, and a page written for a word it cannot win is a page written for nobody. Start with the long, specific phrases, win those first, and let the site grow from there.
  • Stuffing keywords into a page. Repeating the same phrase until the page reads badly does not help. Search engines notice it, readers notice it, and both of them leave. Use the keyword in the title, once near the top, and then write naturally. If the page is really about that keyword, the words will appear on their own.
  • Picking a keyword that does not match the page. If the keyword promises a guide and the page is a sales pitch, the reader leaves, and the search engine learns that the page did not answer the search. Match the page to the keyword, or pick a keyword that matches the page you want to write.
  • Copying a competitor's keywords without understanding them. A keyword that works for a big site may not work for yours, because their readers, their pages and their history are different. Study what works for others, but choose keywords that fit your own pages and your own readers.
  • Never checking what people actually search for. The words you would use are not always the words your readers use. Run your seed words through this tool, check the ideas against real search data, and let the evidence change your mind. A page built on a real search is a page with a real audience waiting for it.
