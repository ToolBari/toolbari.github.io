How to use the EXIF reader

Tap the file box, pick a photo from your device, then tap Read EXIF data. The page reads the tags stored inside the file — the camera that took it, the exposure settings, the date and time, and sometimes the location — and shows them in a table. Everything happens on your device: the file is never uploaded, never sent anywhere and never changed. The reader is view only, which means it shows you what is there and nothing more.

If the photo has no EXIF data, the reader says so plainly: "No EXIF data found in this file." That is common. Photos downloaded from social media usually have their EXIF data stripped, screenshots have none, and some cameras and apps never write it. A photo with no tags is not broken — it simply carries no hidden information, and the reader will not invent any.

The reader works with JPEG and TIFF files, which are the formats that carry EXIF data. PNG files and WebP files use different metadata systems, so the reader will usually find nothing in them. If you pick a file that is not a photo at all, the reader tells you it cannot read it, rather than guessing.

What EXIF data is

EXIF stands for Exchangeable Image File Format, and it is the hidden information your camera writes into every photo it takes. When you press the shutter, the camera records not just the picture but a set of tags: which camera and lens took it, the aperture, shutter speed and ISO, the focal length, the date and time, and — if location is switched on — the GPS coordinates of where you stood. All of it travels inside the photo file, invisible until something reads it.

That hidden information is genuinely useful. It tells you how a photo was taken, which is how photographers learn from their own work and from other people's. It tells your gallery app when a photo was taken, so your photos sort themselves by date. It tells printing software the orientation, so a portrait photo prints upright instead of sideways. And it tells mapping software where a photo was taken, so your memories can be plotted on a map.

But the same information can surprise you. A photo shared online can carry the exact location where it was taken, the date and time, and the camera you own — details you may not want strangers to have. That is why it is worth knowing what EXIF data is, how to read it, and how to remove it before sharing a photo in public. This page teaches all three, and the reader at the top shows you exactly what your own photos carry.

What each field means

The most common EXIF fields are the camera fields: Make (the brand, such as Canon or Nikon), Model (the camera body), and sometimes the lens. Then come the exposure fields: ExposureTime (how long the shutter was open, such as 1/125 second), FNumber (the aperture, such as f/2.8), ISOSpeedRatings (the sensitivity, such as 200) and FocalLength (the lens length in millimetres, such as 50). Together they describe the shot completely — a photographer can look at them and know exactly how the photo was made.

Then come the date fields: DateTime (when the file was last changed), DateTimeOriginal (when the photo was taken) and DateTimeDigitized (when it was turned into a digital file). For most photos all three are the same moment, but a photo that has been edited may have a newer DateTime than its DateTimeOriginal — a small clue that the file has been worked on.

Then come the technical fields: Orientation (which way up the photo should be displayed — a number from 1 to 8, where 1 means the right way up), PixelXDimension and PixelYDimension (the size in pixels), and the GPS fields (latitude, longitude and sometimes altitude). The reader decodes the ones it understands into plain words: an orientation of 6 becomes "rotate 90 degrees clockwise", and GPS coordinates become a readable latitude and longitude. Fields it does not recognise are shown by their tag number, so nothing is hidden from you.

How to edit EXIF on your computer

This page only reads EXIF data — it cannot edit it, because a browser cannot reliably rewrite the data inside a photo file. To actually change EXIF tags, use a free desktop tool. The best-known is ExifTool, a free program that reads and writes EXIF data for almost every camera format, and it is the tool professionals use. You can find it at exiftool.org.

With ExifTool installed, the usual steps are: open a terminal or command window, go to the folder with your photos, and run a command to change the tag you want. To change the date on a photo, the command names the tag and the new value. To remove all EXIF data from a copy of a photo, the command strips every tag at once. Always work on a copy, never on your only version of a photo — EXIF editing changes the file itself, and there is no undo.

If a command-line tool feels too technical, many photo programs offer the same thing with buttons. Free photo managers and editors usually let you view and edit the date, the title and the location of a photo through their menus, and they let you export a copy with the metadata stripped. The exact menu names differ by program, but the idea is the same: open the photo's properties or information panel, change what you want, and save a copy.

How to edit or remove EXIF on your phone

On a phone, the simplest honest answer is that most gallery apps let you view some EXIF data (usually the date and location) but do not let you edit it. What they do let you do is remove it when you share: many phones have a sharing option that strips the location from a photo before it is sent, and that option is worth switching on if you share photos in public places.

To change the date or location on a phone, you generally need a dedicated metadata app from your app store — search for one that edits EXIF data, check its reviews, and try it on a copy of a photo first. The good ones show you the same fields this page shows, let you change them one by one, and save a new copy. The bad ones add watermarks or charge for basic edits, so read before you install.

The most reliable phone method is still the computer: send the photo to your computer, edit it with a desktop tool such as ExifTool, and send it back. It is an extra step, but it is the method that works for every field, on every photo, without surprises.

Why you might want to remove EXIF

The main reason is privacy. A photo's GPS tags can give away your home address, your children's school, or the place you are right now — and a photo posted in public carries those tags with it unless something strips them. Social media sites usually strip EXIF data when you upload, but not all of them do, and a photo sent by message or email keeps its tags. Before sharing a photo of your home, your children or your travels, check what it carries with the reader at the top of this page.

The second reason is tidiness. A camera with the wrong date writes the wrong date into every photo, and a photo scanned from film may carry the scanning date instead of the day it was taken. Fixing the date makes your gallery sort properly and your memories land in the right year. And a photo whose orientation tag is wrong shows sideways in some programs and upright in others — fixing the tag once saves confusion everywhere.

The third reason is honesty in the other direction: a photographer selling or exhibiting work may want the EXIF data to stay, because it proves how the photo was made. Removing EXIF data is a choice, not a rule — the point is to know what your photos carry, and to decide for yourself.

What this page cannot do

This page cannot edit or save EXIF data. Browsers can read a file you pick, but they cannot reliably rewrite the metadata inside a photo file and save the result — that needs a desktop program such as ExifTool. Any page that claims to edit your EXIF data in the browser and download the result is either using a limited trick that only works for some files, or it is not doing what it claims. This page does not pretend: it reads, it shows, and it stops there.

It also cannot recover EXIF data that is already gone. Once a photo's tags have been stripped — by a social media upload, by a screenshot, or by an export that discards metadata — the information is gone for good, and no reader can bring it back. And it cannot read tags it does not understand: camera makers add their own private tags, and this reader shows those by number rather than guessing what they mean. What it shows is real; what it cannot show, it says so.

How do I remove location data from a photo?

What does the orientation tag do?

The orientation tag tells viewing software which way up the photo should be displayed. It is a number from 1 to 8: 1 means the photo is already the right way up, and the other values mean rotate or flip it. A wrong orientation tag is why a photo sometimes shows sideways in one program and upright in another.
