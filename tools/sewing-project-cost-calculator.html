Sewing Project Cost Calculator

Add up the true cost of a sewing project before you cut the fabric. Enter what you pay for fabric, notions, thread and the pattern, plus the hours you will spend, and the calculator shows an itemised breakdown and a total in US dollars.

Fabric cost = metres x price per metre. Time cost = hours x hourly rate. The total is the sum of every line — nothing is hidden.

Prices change and fabric shops differ — enter the prices you actually pay, not the ones you remember. The hourly rate is your choice: it can be what you would pay someone else, or simply a way to see what your time is worth.

How to use this calculator

Enter the fabric first: how many metres the pattern needs, and what one metre costs in US dollars. Then add the smaller items — notions, thread and the pattern — and finally the hours you expect to spend and what an hour of your time is worth. The calculator shows an itemised breakdown and a total, so you can see exactly where the money goes.

Every box accepts zero. If you already own the thread, enter zero for thread. If the pattern was free, enter zero for the pattern. If you sew for pleasure and do not want to price your time, enter zero for the hourly rate and the total will cover materials only. The calculator does not judge the numbers — it just adds them up honestly.

Use the breakdown, not just the total. The line that surprises most people is the time: five hours at 15 dollars an hour is 75 dollars, which is often more than the fabric. Seeing the lines separately is what makes the total useful, because it shows you which part of the project actually costs the most.

Worked example: a simple cotton dress

Suppose a dress needs 2 metres of cotton at 12 dollars a metre, notions of 8 dollars, thread of 3 dollars and a pattern of 10 dollars, and takes 5 hours at an hourly rate of 15 dollars. The fabric costs 2 times 12, which is 24 dollars. The materials add up to 24 plus 8 plus 3 plus 10, which is 45 dollars. The time costs 5 times 15, which is 75 dollars. The total is 45 plus 75, which is 120 dollars.

That total surprises people who only counted the fabric. The fabric is 24 dollars of a 120 dollar project — a fifth of the real cost. The rest is the small items and, above all, the time. None of that makes the dress a bad idea: a handmade dress that fits well is worth more than a shop dress that does not. But the number should be honest, and now it is.

Change any line and the total follows. A cheaper fabric at 8 dollars a metre drops the fabric to 16 dollars and the total to 112 dollars. A faster sew at 3 hours drops the time to 45 dollars and the total to 90 dollars. The calculator lets you test those choices before you buy anything.

Pricing fabric honestly

Fabric is usually the biggest materials cost, and the easiest to misjudge. The price on the bolt is per metre, but the pattern's metre requirement assumes a certain fabric width — usually 110 or 150 centimetres. If your fabric is narrower than the pattern assumes, you need more metres; if it is wider, you may need fewer. Check the width before you buy, not after.

Printed and directional fabrics need extra. A one-way print, a nap, or a large repeat means the pattern pieces must all run the same way, and that wastes fabric between the pieces. A striped fabric that must match at the seams needs extra too. If your fabric has a direction or a match, buy an extra quarter to half a metre beyond the pattern's requirement and enter the real amount you buy.

Fabric from your stash still has a cost, even if you paid for it long ago. If you want the total to reflect the real value of the project, enter what the same fabric would cost today. If you want the total to reflect only the money leaving your pocket now, enter zero. Both answers are honest — they just answer different questions, so be clear which one you are asking.

The small costs that add up

Notions are the small items that hold a garment together: buttons, zips, elastic, interfacing, bias binding, hooks, snaps and labels. Each one costs little, but a dress can easily need 8 to 15 dollars of them, and a coat much more. They are the items most often forgotten in a mental total, and the ones most likely to force a second trip to the shop.

Thread is cheap per spool but not free, and a project in a matching colour may need a new spool even if you own twenty others. Interfacing, often bought by the metre, is another quiet cost — a jacket front, collar and cuffs can use a full metre. Enter what you actually buy for this project, not what you wish you already had.

The pattern itself belongs in the total too. A printed pattern, a PDF pattern, or the printing cost of a PDF all count. A pattern you will reuse many times can be spread across those projects — enter a share of its price if that feels fairer — but a pattern bought for one dress belongs to that dress in full.

Valuing your time

The hardest line to fill in is the value of your time, because sewing is both a hobby and a skill. There is no single right rate. Some people use the wage they earn at work; some use what they would pay a seamstress; some use a round number that feels fair for skilled handwork. Any of those is defensible, because the point is to see the time, not to argue about the rate.

What matters is that the time is counted at all. A project that takes 10 hours is a real commitment, and pretending it is free makes handmade work look cheaper than it is. That matters most when you sew for other people: a friend who asks for a dress and offers to pay for the fabric is offering to cover a fifth of the cost, and the calculator shows why.

Be honest about the hours too. Count cutting, fitting, pressing and finishing, not just the time at the machine. A project that takes 2 hours of sewing often takes 5 hours in total, and the fitting and pressing are where the quality comes from. If you do not know how long a project will take, time the first one and use that number next time.

Costing a project you plan to sell

If you sell what you sew, the calculator gives you the floor price: the total is what the project costs you, and selling below it means paying the buyer to take your work. A selling price needs more than the floor — it needs room for the time you spend photographing, listing and packing, for the fees a marketplace charges, and for a margin that makes the work sustainable.

Start with the calculator's total, then add the selling costs you know: postage materials, listing fees, and the percentage the platform takes. Then decide on a margin. There is no universal percentage, and this calculator does not invent one — but a price that covers only the materials is not a business, it is a hobby subsidised by your own pocket.

Keep records. Note the fabric, the notions, the hours and the total for every project you sell, and compare them with the price you charged. Over time the records tell you which projects are worth repeating and which ones quietly lose money, and that is information no guess can replace.

Common mistakes

The first mistake is counting only the fabric. Notions, thread, pattern and time are real costs, and leaving them out gives a total that flatters the project and misleads the budget. Enter every line, even the small ones.

The second mistake is using the price you remember instead of the price you pay. Fabric prices change, and the bolt you bought last year may cost more now. Check the current price before you cost the project, especially if you are costing it for someone else.

The third mistake is underestimating the hours. Sewing time includes cutting, pinning, fitting, pressing and finishing — not just the machine time. If you guess the hours, guess generously; the calculator will still be honest about the total.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you what to charge for your work, or whether a project is worth making. It adds up the costs you enter — it cannot judge the value of a garment that fits perfectly, or the pleasure of making something with your own hands. Those are worth something the calculator cannot see.

It also cannot track the costs it does not ask about: the wear on your machine, the electricity, the pattern paper, or the trip to the fabric shop. If those matter to you, add them to the notions line. The calculator is a frame for honest numbers, and the numbers are only as complete as what you put in.

Questions people ask

Should I include the cost of tools I already own?

Only if you want the total to reflect the full value of the project. Tools such as scissors and a sewing machine last for many projects, so most people leave them out of a single project's cost. If a project needs a tool you must buy specially, add its price to the notions line.

How do I price fabric from my stash?

Either way is honest, as long as you know which question you are answering. Enter the price of the same fabric today to see the project's true value, or enter zero to see only the money leaving your pocket now. The calculator shows both if you run it twice.

What hourly rate should I use?

Use a rate that feels fair for skilled handwork — your wage at work, what you would pay a seamstress, or a round number you are comfortable with. The point is to make the time visible, not to argue about the exact rate. If you sew purely for pleasure, enter zero.

Why does the total seem higher than a shop price?

Because a shop price hides the time and buys fabric in bulk. Your total counts every hour and every small item at retail prices, which is honest — and a handmade garment that fits well is worth more than a shop garment that does not. The comparison is not between two prices for the same thing.

Can I cost a quilt or a bag with this calculator?

Yes. Enter the total fabric in metres, the notions and thread, and the hours. For a quilt, add the batting and backing fabric to the fabric line or the notions line — whichever you find clearer — and note it in your own records.

Does the calculator include tax or postage?

No. Enter prices that already include tax if you want the total to include it, and add postage for anything you order to the notions line. The calculator adds exactly the numbers you enter, so include everything you want counted.
