What this calculator does and who it is for

Legal work runs on deadlines: days to file, days to respond, days to appeal. The numbers sound simple — thirty days from service, fourteen days from the order — but the arithmetic is where mistakes happen. Weekends, month ends, and the question of whether the first day counts all change the answer, and a date worked out wrong can mean a filing that is a day late. A day late, in legal work, can mean a case lost.

This calculator does the date maths. Enter a start date and a number of days, choose calendar days or working days, and it works out the deadline date, shows the day of the week, and tells you how many days are left. The result always carries the same warning: verify the date with a lawyer or the court, because counting rules differ and this page does not know the rules of any particular court or case.

It is built for anyone who needs a quick, careful answer to a date question — a lawyer checking a filing date, a small firm diarising a time limit, or a person keeping track of a deadline in their own matter. It is a calculator, not a legal adviser, and it is honest about that on every result. The maths is exact; the rules behind the maths are yours to check.

The inputs, explained one by one

The start date is the date the clock runs from — usually the date of the event that starts the time limit, such as the date a document was served or an order was made. Enter it exactly, from the document itself, not from memory. A start date that is a day out makes the deadline a day out, and there is no way to spot that from the result alone.

The number of days is the time limit itself: the figure in the rule, the order, or the letter that sets the deadline. Enter it as a whole number. If the limit is given in weeks or months rather than days, convert it first — and check whether the rule means calendar weeks or something else, because a month is not always thirty days.

The counting mode is the choice that changes the answer most. Calendar days count every day, including weekends: thirty calendar days from a Monday is a Wednesday five weeks later, weekends included. Working days count only Monday to Friday, skipping Saturdays and Sundays: thirty working days from the same Monday is six working weeks later. The calculator does not know public holidays, which is one more reason every result must be verified — a deadline that falls on a holiday is usually moved, and the rule for moving it differs by place.

The formula and how the result is worked out

The calculator counts from the day after the start date. That is the usual convention: the start date is day zero, and the first day of the period is the next day. So a ten-day period starting on a Monday ends on the Thursday of the following week in calendar days — Tuesday is day one, Wednesday day two, and so on until the tenth day.

In calendar mode, the calculator simply adds the number of days to the start date. In working mode, it steps forward one day at a time, counting only Mondays to Fridays and skipping Saturdays and Sundays, until it has counted the number of working days you entered. The result is the last day counted — the deadline itself, not the day after it.

The result also shows how many days are left until the deadline, counted from today. That figure is a planning aid, not a legal fact: it tells you how much time you have, not what the court will accept. And the weekday is shown with the date because a deadline that falls on a Saturday or Sunday is a warning sign — courts are usually closed, and the rule for what happens next differs by place.

A worked example with real numbers

Imagine a time limit of thirty days starting on Monday 1 February 2027. In calendar mode, the calculator counts from Tuesday 2 February — day one — and adds thirty days. February 2027 has twenty-eight days, so the count runs through February and into March: the thirtieth day is Wednesday 3 March 2027. That is the deadline in calendar days.

In working mode, the same thirty days are counted differently. Starting from Tuesday 2 February and counting only Monday to Friday, the first week gives four working days, and each full week after that gives five. The thirtieth working day falls on Monday 15 March 2027 — twelve days later than the calendar-day answer. The difference between the two answers is the weekends: thirty calendar days contain four weekends, and working-day counting skips them all.

The example shows why the mode matters more than the arithmetic. Both answers are correct maths; only one of them is the right deadline, and which one depends on the rule that sets the time limit. If the rule says calendar days and you count working days, you file twelve days late. If it says working days and you count calendar days, you file early — which is safe, but only by luck. Check the rule, then count.

Common mistakes that give a wrong answer

The first mistake is counting the start date as day one. Most counting rules start the clock the day after the event, so a period that starts on a Monday ends a day later than it would if Monday counted. This calculator counts from the day after the start date, but the rule for your case may differ — check it, and if it differs, adjust the start date you enter.

The second mistake is mixing up calendar days and working days. A limit written as days usually means calendar days unless the rule says working days, business days, or court days — but usually is not always, and the words vary by place. Read the rule itself, not someone's summary of it.

The third mistake is forgetting holidays. This calculator skips weekends in working mode but knows nothing about public holidays, court closures, or local holidays. A deadline that falls on a holiday is usually moved to the next working day, but the rule for moving it — and whether it applies at all — differs by place. The fourth mistake is trusting a single calculation: always work the date out twice, by two different methods, and check it against the court or a lawyer before you rely on it.

How to use the result in a real decision

Use the result as the starting point for a diary entry, not as the final word. Work the date out here, then verify it — with the rule itself, with a lawyer, or with the court — and only then put it in the diary. Diary the deadline a day or two early if you can: a deadline met a day early is safe, and a deadline met on the last day depends on everything going right.

Set a reminder well before the deadline, not on the day itself. A reminder a week before a filing deadline gives you time to finish the work; a reminder on the day gives you time to panic. Copy the summary from this page into your notes so the start date, the counting mode, and the result are all recorded together — if anyone questions the date later, the record shows how it was worked out.

And if the deadline is close, or the counting is unclear, or the consequences of missing it are serious, do not rely on a calculator at all. Ask a lawyer, or ask the court. A five-minute question is cheaper than a missed deadline, and no calculator — including this one — can replace the person who knows the rules of your case.

Keep the record. Note the start date, the number of days, the counting mode, and the verified deadline together, so anyone who picks up the file can see how the date was reached. A deadline without its workings is a deadline nobody can check.

Questions people ask

How are legal deadlines usually counted?

Usually from the day after the event that starts the time limit, with the start date as day zero. Rules differ by court and by case, so check the rule that sets your deadline.

Do weekends count in a deadline?

In calendar days, yes — every day counts. In working days, no — only Monday to Friday count. Which one applies depends on the rule that sets the time limit.

What happens if a deadline falls on a holiday?

It is usually moved to the next working day, but the rule differs by place. This calculator does not know public holidays, so check with a lawyer or the court.

Can I rely on this calculator for a court filing?

No — not on its own. It does date maths only. Verify every deadline with a lawyer or the court before you rely on it, and work the date out twice by two different methods.

What is the difference between calendar days and working days?

Calendar days count every day, including weekends. Working days count only Monday to Friday. Thirty calendar days and thirty working days from the same start date end twelve days apart.
