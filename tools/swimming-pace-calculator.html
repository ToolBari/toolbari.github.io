What is swimming pace?

Swimming pace is the time it takes you to swim a fixed distance, usually 100 metres. A pace of 2 minutes per 100 metres means you cover each 100 metres in 2 minutes, so a kilometre takes 20 minutes. Pace is the number swimmers talk about because it works for any distance: once you know your pace, you can predict how long any swim will take and compare swims of different lengths fairly.

Pace is different from speed, though the two describe the same swimming. Speed says how far you go in an hour; pace says how long 100 metres takes. Swimmers prefer pace because pools are measured in lengths, and thinking in 100-metre chunks matches how a swim actually feels: you count lengths, you turn at the wall, and every four lengths of a 25-metre pool is another 100 metres done.

This calculator works out your pace from a swim you have already done. Those predictions are useful for planning training sessions and for setting a target for your next swim.

Worked examples

Amara swims 1,000 metres in 25 minutes. Her pace is 25 minutes divided by 10 hundreds of metres, which is 2 minutes 30 seconds per 100 metres. Her pace per 100 yards is 2 minutes 17 seconds, because a yard is slightly shorter than a metre. Her speed is 2.4 km/h, or 40 metres per minute. At the same pace, 400 metres would take 10 minutes, 1 kilometre would take 25 minutes, and 1.5 kilometres would take 37 minutes 30 seconds.

Ben swims 1,500 metres in 30 minutes. His pace is 30 minutes divided by 15 hundreds, which is 2 minutes per 100 metres — a solid pace for a regular swimmer. His speed is 3 km/h, or 50 metres per minute. At the same pace, 400 metres would take 8 minutes and 1 kilometre would take 20 minutes. The difference between Amara and Ben is 30 seconds per 100 metres, which sounds small but adds up to 5 minutes over a kilometre: pace differences that look tiny are large over distance.

A faster example: a club swimmer covering 400 metres in 6 minutes holds 1 minute 30 seconds per 100 metres, a speed of 4 km/h. That pace predicts a 15-minute kilometre and a 22 minute 30 second 1.5 kilometres. Holding that pace for the full 1.5 kilometres is much harder than holding it for 400 metres, which is why predictions for longer distances should be treated as targets rather than promises.

What does your pace mean?

Beginners who can swim steadily but are still building technique typically hold about 2 minutes 30 seconds to 3 minutes per 100 metres. That is a fine place to start: it means you can swim a kilometre in 25 to 30 minutes without stopping, which is a real achievement. Regular swimmers who train once or twice a week often hold 1 minute 45 seconds to 2 minutes 15 seconds per 100 metres, and strong club swimmers go under 1 minute 30 seconds. These are broad typical ranges, not grades, and your pace depends on technique far more than on fitness.

Technique matters so much because water is dense: small improvements in body position, breathing and stroke timing cut drag and add speed without any extra effort. A swimmer who fixes a sinking leg position or learns to breathe without lifting the head can gain 10 seconds per 100 metres in a few weeks, while months of fitness training alone might gain half that. If your pace has stopped improving, a lesson or two focused on technique is usually worth more than swimming further.

Pace also changes with the stroke. Front crawl is the fastest stroke for most people; breaststroke is typically 30 to 60 seconds per 100 metres slower at the same effort, and backstroke sits between the two. Compare paces within one stroke, and do not be surprised if your breaststroke pace is much slower than your front crawl pace — that is normal, and it is true for good swimmers too.

How to count lengths correctly

Counting lengths is the easiest part of swimming to get wrong, because it is boring and the mind wanders. The simplest reliable method is to count in groups: count every four lengths as 100 metres in a 25-metre pool, and keep count of the hundreds rather than the lengths. Some swimmers move a small object along the poolside every 100 metres, or count laps on their fingers against the pool edge at each turn.

If your pool is 50 metres long, each length is 50 metres, so two lengths make 100 metres and 20 lengths make a kilometre. If you are unsure of your pool's length, ask at reception or check the pool's website before you swim; guessing wrong doubles or halves your pace and makes the result meaningless. A sports watch that counts lengths automatically is the most reliable option, but it needs to be set to the right pool length first, and it can miscount if you stop mid-length or change stroke.

Whatever method you use, count the lengths you actually swam. Lengths swum slowly as a warm-up count just as much as fast lengths when you are working out an average pace, and lengths you skipped do not count at all. An honest count gives an honest pace, and an honest pace is the only one worth tracking.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is counting rest time. If you swim 1,000 metres in 20 minutes of swimming but rest for 5 minutes at the wall in the middle, your swimming time is 20 minutes and your pace is 2 minutes per 100 metres; counting the rest gives 25 minutes and a pace of 2 minutes 30 seconds, which understates how fast you actually swam. Decide in advance whether you are measuring swimming pace or total session time, and be consistent.

The second mistake is miscounting lengths, usually by losing track halfway through. The third is comparing paces across different pools without adjusting: a 100-metre pace in a 25-metre pool is slightly faster than the same pace in a 50-metre pool, because you push off the wall twice as often and each push-off gives you free speed. The fourth is treating a single fast swim as your pace. One good day with fresh arms is not your pace; the average of several ordinary swims is.

How to get faster

The fastest gains usually come from technique, not from swimming more. Three things help most swimmers: keeping the body flat and level in the water instead of letting the legs sink, breathing to the side without lifting the head, and reaching fully forward on each stroke instead of rushing the arms. A few lessons with a coach who can watch you swim will find the biggest problem quickly, and fixing one big problem is worth more than months of unfocused laps.

Once technique is reasonable, structured sessions help more than steady laps. Short intervals — for example, ten 100-metre swims with 20 seconds rest between them, holding the same pace for each — teach your body to sustain a pace under tiredness, which is exactly what a longer swim demands. A weekly session of intervals plus one longer steady swim is a simple pattern that works for most people. Warm up gently, swim the main set, and finish with a few easy lengths to cool down.

Finally, be patient with pace. Improvements of 5 seconds per 100 metres over a month are good progress, and they add up: 5 seconds per 100 metres is 50 seconds over a kilometre. Track your pace over weeks, not days, celebrate the small gains, and remember that the swimmer who keeps turning up is the one who gets faster.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot measure your swim. It cannot count your lengths, time your laps, or tell whether you rested at the wall. It trusts the numbers you enter, so an honest count and an honest time are the whole foundation of the result. It also cannot predict how you will swim a longer distance: holding a pace for 400 metres is much easier than holding it for 1.5 kilometres, and fatigue, technique breakdown and turns all cost time as distance grows.

It also knows nothing about your stroke, your pool or the water. Open water is slower than a pool for most people, because there are no walls to push off and no lane ropes to follow, and a wetsuit changes the picture again. Use the predictions as targets for pool training, adjust them with experience when you swim outdoors, and treat the pace itself — measured honestly over several swims — as the number that matters.

Questions people ask

What is swimming pace?

Swimming pace is the time it takes to swim a fixed distance, usually 100 metres. A pace of 2 minutes per 100 metres means each 100 metres takes 2 minutes, so a kilometre takes 20 minutes. Pace lets you compare swims of different lengths and predict how long any swim will take.

What is a good pace per 100m?

Beginners typically hold about 2 minutes 30 seconds to 3 minutes per 100 metres. Regular swimmers who train once or twice a week often hold 1 minute 45 seconds to 2 minutes 15 seconds, and strong club swimmers go under 1 minute 30 seconds. Technique matters more than fitness, so a few lessons can move your pace a lot.

How do I count lengths correctly?

Should rest time count?

It depends what you are measuring. For swimming pace, count only your swimming time and leave out rests at the wall. For total session time, count everything. Mixing the two gives a pace that understates how fast you actually swam, so decide in advance and be consistent.

How do I get faster?

Work on technique first: a flat body position, breathing without lifting your head, and a full reach on each stroke. Then add structured sessions, such as ten 100-metre swims with short rests, holding the same pace each time. Improvements of 5 seconds per 100 metres in a month are good progress.

What distance should I train for first?

Start with the distance you can already swim without stopping, then build up gradually. If you can swim 400 metres comfortably, aim for 800 metres, then a kilometre. Add about 10 percent to your longest swim each week, keep most swims easy, and let pace follow distance rather than chasing both at once.
