How the date planner works

This planner answers a simple question that is easy to get wrong: if it is a certain date in one city, what date is it in another? You pick two cities and a date, and the planner shows the date on both sides, the time difference in hours, and whether the date line is crossed — meaning the calendar date is different on the two sides.

The planner also checks a small table of fixed-date public holidays for both places. If the date you picked is a public holiday in either city, it tells you, so you can avoid booking a meeting or a delivery on a day when offices are closed. The holiday table holds fixed dates only, such as New Year's Day on 1 January and Christmas Day on 25 December.

All offsets use standard time. Some cities observe daylight saving for part of the year, which shifts the difference by one hour. The planner warns you when one or both of your cities do this, and the date result is worked out from midday in the first city, so a one-hour shift only changes the answer near midnight.

Time zones and the date line

The world is divided into time zones, each a fixed number of hours ahead of or behind Coordinated Universal Time, known as UTC. London is on UTC in winter. Tokyo is nine hours ahead of UTC. New York is five hours behind UTC in winter. The difference between two cities is the difference between their offsets: Tokyo is fourteen hours ahead of New York.

The international date line runs through the Pacific Ocean. Crossing it westward moves the calendar forward by one day; crossing it eastward moves it back by one day. That is why noon on 15 March in New York is 2 in the morning on 16 March in Tokyo — the date has already changed on the far side of the line.

Half-hour and 45-minute offsets exist too. Mumbai is five and a half hours ahead of UTC, which surprises people who expect whole hours. The planner handles these offsets exactly, so the date it shows for Mumbai is correct even though the difference is not a whole number of hours.

Daylight saving, explained honestly

Daylight saving means clocks move forward by one hour for part of the year, usually in the warmer months, so that evenings have more daylight. London, Paris, Berlin, New York, Chicago, Los Angeles, Toronto, Sydney and Auckland all observe it, among others. The dates when clocks change differ by country and sometimes by region.

This planner uses standard-time offsets for every city, and warns you when daylight saving applies to one of your cities. That means the time difference it shows can be off by one hour during the daylight saving period. The date it shows is worked out from midday, so the one-hour shift only matters if your meeting or flight is close to midnight.

Some places have stopped observing daylight saving in recent years — Brazil ended it in 2019 and most of Mexico ended it in 2022 — and rules change from time to time. For anything important, check an official clock for both cities on the day itself. The planner is a planning aid, not a live clock.

Who should use this planner

This planner is for anyone who works, studies or keeps in touch across time zones: remote teams scheduling meetings, families calling relatives abroad, students applying to universities overseas, and travellers planning flights and hotel check-ins. It answers the date question before you book anything.

It is also useful for teachers and event organisers. If you run an online class or a webinar for people in several countries, the planner shows you at a glance which of your participants will be joining on a different calendar date — and whether any of them has a public holiday that day.

The planner does not show live times, flight schedules or meeting links. It works out dates and differences from fixed offsets and a small holiday table. For the exact local time right now, use a world clock. For booking, always verify with an official calendar and the airline or venue.

How to use it step by step

First, pick the city where your date starts — usually the city you are in, or the city where an event is happening. Then pick the second city you want to compare it with. The list covers 21 cities across every continent, chosen to cover the most common pairs.

Next, pick a date from the calendar. The date is read as a calendar date in the first city. Press the button to plan. The result shows the date in both cities, the time difference in hours, whether the date line is crossed, and any fixed-date holiday on that date in either place.

Copy the plan if you want to keep it or send it to someone. Nothing is stored or sent anywhere — the planner works entirely in your browser, and the holiday table is built into the page.

Worked example

Suppose you are in New York and want to call a colleague in Tokyo on 15 March. You pick New York as the first city, Tokyo as the second, and 15 March as the date. The planner shows that 15 March in New York is 16 March in Tokyo, because Tokyo is fourteen hours ahead. The date line is crossed.

Now suppose the call is between London and Tokyo on the same date. London is on UTC in March and Tokyo is nine hours ahead, so midday on 15 March in London is 9 in the evening on 15 March in Tokyo. The date is the same on both sides, and the planner says so.

Finally, suppose the date is 25 December and the cities are London and Sydney. The planner shows the date difference and warns that 25 December is Christmas Day in both places, and that Sydney also has Boxing Day on 26 December. A meeting on either day would be a poor choice.

Planning across the date line

The date line causes more confusion than any other part of time zones, because it changes the calendar, not just the clock. A flight from Los Angeles to Tokyo leaves on one day and arrives two days later on the calendar, even though the flight itself takes about eleven hours. The missing day is not lost — it is the date line doing its job.

The same effect hits meetings and deadlines. A deadline of Friday in Sydney is Thursday in Los Angeles, and a Monday meeting in New York is a Tuesday meeting in Tokyo. If you only check the time difference and not the date, you can be a whole day early or a whole day late. The planner shows both dates side by side so the mistake cannot hide.

When you plan across the date line, write the date down for both sides. A message that says the meeting is on 15 March in New York and 16 March in Tokyo leaves no room for doubt. A message that says only 15 March invites the other person to read their own date into it — and half the time, that date is wrong.

Mistakes to avoid

The most common mistake is forgetting the date line. A meeting set for Monday in New York can be Tuesday in Tokyo, and a deadline of Friday in Sydney can be Thursday in Los Angeles. Always check the date on both sides, not just the time difference.

Another mistake is assuming the offset never changes. Daylight saving shifts the difference by one hour for part of the year, and the changeover dates differ by country. In the weeks when one city has changed its clocks and the other has not, the difference is one hour different from usual. The planner warns you, but it cannot know the exact changeover dates — check an official clock.

A third mistake is trusting the holiday table as complete. It holds only widely-known fixed-date holidays for a small set of countries. It does not include movable holidays such as Easter or Eid, whose dates change every year, and it does not include every holiday in every country. Always verify with an official calendar before booking travel or a meeting.

Finally, do not use the planner for flight times. A flight's arrival date depends on the flight duration and the local times at both ends, which the planner does not know. Use the airline's own times for flights, and use this planner for the date arithmetic around them.

Questions people ask

What is the international date line?

It is the line in the Pacific Ocean where the calendar date changes by one day. Crossing it westward moves the date forward a day; crossing it eastward moves it back. That is why the date can differ between two cities even when the time difference is less than a day.

Why does the planner use standard time?

Because daylight saving rules change by country and by year, and a fixed table cannot track them all. The planner uses standard-time offsets and warns you when a city observes daylight saving, so you know the difference can shift by one hour.

Which holidays does the planner check?

Only widely-known fixed-date holidays, such as New Year's Day on 1 January and Christmas Day on 25 December. Movable holidays such as Easter and Eid are not included, because their dates change every year.

Does the planner show the current time anywhere?

No. It works out dates and time differences from fixed offsets. For the exact local time right now, use a world clock. For booking travel or meetings, verify with an official calendar.

Why is Mumbai five and a half hours ahead of UTC?

Because India chose a half-hour offset when its time zone was set. A few places use half-hour or 45-minute offsets. The planner handles them exactly, so the date it shows for Mumbai is correct.

Can I use the planner for flight arrival dates?

Not directly. A flight's arrival date depends on the flight duration as well as the time difference. Use the airline's own times for flights, and use this planner to check the date on both sides around the trip.
