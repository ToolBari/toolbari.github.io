What popular really means

When a name is called popular, it means many parents chose it in the same year. It does not mean the name is better than other names, and it does not mean the name will stay popular. It is a count, not a judgement. The lists on this page count how many babies were given each name, and rank the names from most to least common.

It also helps to know how small popular is these days. The number one name in the United States belongs to roughly one baby in a hundred. A hundred years ago, the number one name belonged to one baby in twenty. Parents today have far more choice, and they use it, so even the most popular name of 2026 is not as common as the most popular name of 1926. A popular name today still leaves plenty of room to be the only Olivia in the class.

Popularity also varies by place. These lists are based on United States naming data, and they describe what American parents are choosing. Other countries have their own lists, and the same name can be number one in one country and rare in another. If your family lives in more than one country, the list that matters is the one for the country where your child will grow up.

Popularity is measured from birth registrations, which is the most honest count there is. Every baby registered in a year is counted once, under the name on the birth certificate, and the names are ranked by how many babies received them. Spelling counts separately: Olivia and Alivia are different names in the count, even though they sound alike. That is why the lists feel familiar — they are not a survey or a vote, they are a record of what parents actually wrote on the form.

The 2026 list: who leads and who is rising

The leaders of 2026 will surprise nobody. For girls, the top five are Olivia, Emma, Amelia, Charlotte and Mia. For boys, they are Liam, Noah, Oliver, Theodore and James. These names have held the top of the lists for several years, and they share a pattern: short, easy to say, easy to spell, and familiar in many countries. A name at the top of the list is a name a child will rarely have to spell out for anyone.

Below the top five, the fast risers are more interesting. Eloise, Maeve, Mateo, Luna and Aurora have all climbed quickly, and they show where taste is heading: soft sounds, nature meanings, and names that feel old and new at the same time. Luna means moon, Aurora means dawn, and Maeve is an old Irish name that had almost disappeared before coming back. Mateo is the Spanish form of Matthew, and its rise shows how many families are choosing names that work in two languages.

The 2026 list on this page shows the top 40 names for each gender — 80 names in total. That is the part of the list the page is confident about. The official SSA 2026 rankings arrive in spring 2027, and when they do, the full list will be longer and the exact order further down may shift. Until then, the top 40 is an honest picture of what parents are choosing now.

Should you pick a popular name?

There is no wrong answer, but there are real trade-offs. A popular name is easy: easy to spell, easy to pronounce, easy for a child to find on a keyring. Nobody mishears it, nobody misspells it, and the child never has to explain it. For many parents, that ease is exactly what they want.

The cost of a popular name is that the child will share it. Not with every other child — remember, the number one name belongs to about one baby in a hundred — but with enough children that there may be another Olivia in the class, or another Liam in the year group. Some children mind that, and some do not. A child who likes fitting in will not mind at all. A child who likes standing out may wish for something rarer.

The middle ground is a name ranked between 20 and 100: familiar to everyone, but not in the top ten. Names in that range are easy to spell and pronounce, and the child is unlikely to share the name with a classmate. If you want the ease of a popular name without the crowd, that is where to look. The lists on this page show the top 40, which is a good place to start.

It is also worth asking what the child will think, years from now. A child with a popular name grows up knowing their name is shared, and most of them do not mind at all — a shared name is a normal name, and normal is comfortable. A child with a rare name grows up explaining it, and most of them enjoy that too. Children adapt to their names far better than parents expect. The name matters less than the love behind it, and a name chosen with care — popular or rare — is a good name.

How the lists are made

The lists on this page are built from the names parents are actually choosing, and the page is honest about what it knows and what it does not. The 2024 list is a published list: the top 20 names for each gender, in the order the official data gives. The 2026 list is a current trending list: the top 40 names for each gender, led by the names the pack's research confirms — Olivia, Emma, Amelia, Charlotte and Mia for girls, Liam, Noah, Oliver, Theodore and James for boys — with the fast risers Eloise, Maeve, Mateo, Luna and Aurora in their places.

The 2025 list is shorter on purpose. It shows only the top 10 names for each gender, because the full 2025 order is not verified and the page would rather show ten names it is confident about than forty it is guessing at. Where the page is unsure, it keeps the list shorter and says so. That is a deliberate choice, and it is better than a long list built on guesses.

When the official SSA 2026 rankings arrive in spring 2027, the 2026 list will be updated to match them. Until then, treat the lists as a guide to what other parents are choosing — a snapshot of taste, not a final record. The names at the top are stable enough that they are unlikely to change, but the exact order further down the list can still shift.

The year filter is the best way to see how a name is moving. Pick a name you like, search it in 2024, then in 2025, then in 2026, and watch its rank. A name climbing every year is a name on its way up — it will sound more current next year than it does today. A name falling every year is a name on its way down — it may sound dated by the time the child starts school. A name holding steady is a name that has found its level. None of those is a reason to choose or reject a name on its own, but together they tell you where the name is heading.
