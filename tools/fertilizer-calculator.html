How to use this calculator

Enter the area you want to feed in square metres, the application rate in grams per square metre from your product label, and the NPK percentages printed on the bag — the three numbers such as 10-10-10 that say how much nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium the fertilizer contains. Enter the bag weight too, if you buy in bags. The calculator shows the total fertilizer to apply in grams and kilograms, the number of bags, and how much of each nutrient that delivers, with the working shown.

The rate is the number that matters most, and it comes from the label, not from this page. Different products have different strengths, different crops want different amounts, and the label is written for the product in your hand. If the label gives a range — say 20 to 40 grams per square metre — start at the lower end: you can always feed again, but you cannot take fertilizer back once it is spread.

The NPK percentages are printed on every bag as three numbers separated by dashes. A bag marked 10-10-10 contains 10 percent nitrogen, 10 percent phosphorus (as phosphate) and 10 percent potassium (as potash) by weight. A bag marked 20-5-10 contains 20 percent nitrogen, 5 percent phosphorus and 10 percent potassium. Enter the three numbers from your bag, and the calculator works out what each one delivers.

What NPK means

NPK stands for the three nutrients plants need in the largest amounts: N for nitrogen, P for phosphorus and K for potassium (K is the chemical symbol for potassium, from the Latin kalium). Nitrogen grows leaves and stems — it is the nutrient for green, leafy growth, and a plant short of nitrogen turns yellow and grows slowly. Phosphorus grows roots and flowers — it is the nutrient for strong roots, fruit and seed, and a plant short of phosphorus flowers poorly. Potassium grows strength — it helps the plant resist disease and drought and ripen its fruit, and a plant short of potassium has weak stems and brown leaf edges.

The three numbers on the bag are percentages by weight, and they always add up to less than 100 — the rest is filler, coating and other ingredients that carry the nutrients or make the granules spread evenly. A 10-10-10 fertilizer is a balanced feed, giving equal shares of the three nutrients. A 20-5-10 is a leafy-growth feed, heavy on nitrogen. A 5-10-10 is a flowering and fruiting feed, light on nitrogen and heavier on the other two. The right balance depends on what you grow and what your soil already has.

Plants need other nutrients too — calcium, magnesium, sulphur and trace elements such as iron and zinc — but in smaller amounts, and most soils and most complete fertilizers supply them. If a plant shows a strange symptom that NPK does not explain — yellow leaves with green veins, for example — the cause is more likely a trace nutrient or the soil's acidity than a lack of NPK, and a soil test is the way to find out.

How much to apply

The rate on the label is the starting point, and it is given in grams per square metre for granular fertilizer or millilitres per litre of water for liquid feed. This calculator works with the granular form: area times rate gives the total. A 100 square metre lawn at 30 grams per square metre needs 3000 grams — 3 kilograms. A 10 square metre vegetable bed at the same rate needs 300 grams. The arithmetic is simple; the judgement is in the rate.

More is not better. Too much fertilizer burns roots and leaves — the salts in it draw water out of the plant — and the excess washes into drains and waterways, where it feeds algae instead of your plants. A plant given twice the label rate does not grow twice as fast; it grows worse, or it dies. If you are unsure, use the lower end of the label range and watch the plants for a few weeks before feeding again.

Less is safer than more, and nothing is safer than guessing. If you have no label — a bag with the label torn off, or a handful of granules from a neighbour — do not guess the rate. A soil test, a new bag with a label, or a smaller test area are all better than spreading an unknown product at an unknown rate over a whole garden.

Worked example

Take a 100 square metre lawn, fed with a 10-10-10 fertilizer at 30 grams per square metre, sold in 25 kilogram bags. The total fertilizer is the area times the rate: 100 times 30, which is 3000 grams, or 3 kilograms. The bags needed are 3 divided by 25, which is 0.12 — less than one bag, so one bag is more than enough, with plenty left for the next feed.

The nutrients delivered are the total times each percentage. Nitrogen is 10 percent of 3000 grams, which is 300 grams. Phosphorus is 10 percent, which is 300 grams. Potassium is 10 percent, which is 300 grams. So one feed puts 300 grams of each nutrient onto 100 square metres — 3 grams of each per square metre.

Now change the product: the same lawn fed with a 20-5-10 fertilizer at the same 30 grams per square metre. The total is still 3 kilograms, but the nutrients change: nitrogen is 20 percent of 3000, which is 600 grams; phosphorus is 5 percent, which is 150 grams; potassium is 10 percent, which is 300 grams. The same weight of fertilizer, a different feed — which is why the percentages matter as much as the total.

When and how to apply

Feed when the plants are growing and the soil is moist — spring and early summer for most gardens, when growth is fastest and the plants can use the nutrients. Feeding a dormant plant wastes fertilizer, because the plant is not taking anything up, and feeding in high summer heat can scorch leaves. Water the area before and after feeding, or feed before rain, so the granules dissolve and wash into the soil rather than sitting on the leaves.

Spread evenly. A handful here and a handful there leaves stripes — dark green where the granules landed thickly, pale where they missed. A spreader, a shaker or a careful hand all work, but the aim is the same: the same amount on every square metre. For a lawn, walk in straight lines and overlap slightly; for a bed, scatter and rake lightly, keeping the granules off the plant leaves.

Keep fertilizer off paths and patios, and sweep up any spills: granules on hard surfaces wash into drains with the next rain, and they can stain. Wear gloves, wash your hands afterwards, and keep the bag sealed and dry between feeds — damp fertilizer clumps and becomes hard to spread evenly.

Safety first

Fertilizer is a useful product and a harmful one in the wrong place. Follow the product label — it gives the rate, the timing and the warnings for the product in your hand, and it is the authority. Never exceed label rates: more fertilizer does not mean more growth, and it can burn plants, poison soil life and pollute water.

Keep fertilizer away from children and pets. Store it sealed, dry and out of reach, and never leave an open bag where a child or a dog can reach it. If a child or pet eats fertilizer, contact a doctor or vet at once and take the bag or the label with you. Wash your hands after handling it, and keep it out of eyes and mouth.

Keep it out of water. Never spread fertilizer near a pond, stream or drain, and never wash spills into a drain. The nutrients that feed your plants feed algae in water, and the result is green, oxygen-starved water that kills fish. A buffer of unfertilized ground beside any water is the simplest protection.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is using too much. The label rate is a maximum for the conditions it was written for, not a target to exceed, and doubling it does not double growth — it burns roots and pollutes water. If in doubt, use less and watch the plants.

The second mistake is feeding at the wrong time. Fertilizer spread on dry soil in hot sun sits on the surface and scorches leaves; fertilizer spread on dormant plants in winter washes away unused. Feed when the soil is moist and the plants are growing, and water it in.

The third mistake is feeding the same way every year without checking the soil. A garden fed with the same balanced fertilizer for years can build up phosphorus while running short of something else, and the plants will tell you — poor flowering, yellow leaves, weak growth — if you watch them. A soil test every few years is cheaper than a season of the wrong feed.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you what rate to use or what fertilizer to buy. It does the arithmetic on the numbers you enter — the area, the rate and the percentages — and it does not know your soil, your plants or your climate. The rate comes from the label, the product choice comes from what you grow, and a soil test is the only way to know what your soil actually needs.

It also cannot account for what is already in your soil. A bed rich in compost may need little or no added fertilizer, and a bed that has been fed for years may already hold more phosphorus than the plants can use. The calculator tells you what a feed delivers; whether the soil needs it is a question for a soil test and your own observation.

Questions people ask

What does NPK mean?

NPK stands for nitrogen (N), phosphorus (P) and potassium (K) — the three nutrients plants need in the largest amounts. The three numbers on a bag, such as 10-10-10, are the percentages of each by weight. Nitrogen grows leaves, phosphorus grows roots and flowers, and potassium builds strength and disease resistance.

How much fertilizer do I need?

Multiply the area in square metres by the application rate in grams per square metre from your product label. A 100 square metre lawn at 30 grams per square metre needs 3000 grams, or 3 kilograms. Enter your numbers in the calculator to get the total, the bags and the nutrients delivered.

Can I use too much fertilizer?

Yes, and it is the most common mistake. Too much fertilizer burns roots and leaves, because the salts in it draw water out of the plant, and the excess washes into drains and waterways. Never exceed the label rate — if you are unsure, use the lower end of the range and watch the plants.

Is fertilizer safe for children and pets?

Not if eaten. Keep fertilizer sealed, dry and out of reach of children and pets, wear gloves when you handle it, and wash your hands afterwards. If a child or pet eats fertilizer, contact a doctor or vet at once and take the bag or label with you.

When is the best time to fertilize?

When the plants are growing and the soil is moist — spring and early summer for most gardens. Water the area before and after feeding, or feed before rain, so the granules dissolve into the soil. Do not feed dormant plants in winter, and do not feed in hot sun on dry soil.

What is the difference between granular and liquid fertilizer?

Granular fertilizer is spread dry and dissolves slowly with water; it feeds over weeks. Liquid fertilizer is mixed with water and applied to the soil or leaves; it acts fast and washes through quickly. This calculator works with granular rates in grams per square metre — liquid feeds use millilitres per litre of water instead.
