How to use this calculator

Enter the number of visitors your page had in a period, and the number of conversions — sales, signups, or whatever action counts as a conversion for you — in the same period. The calculator divides conversions by visitors and multiplies by 100 to give your conversion rate as a percent. Add your average order value and it also shows what a small lift in the rate would earn, which is usually the most useful number on the page.

The target rate box answers a different question: how many conversions would you need to reach the rate you want? If your page had 10000 visitors and you want a 3 percent rate, you need 300 conversions — the calculator shows the number and how many more that is than you have now. That turns a vague goal like do better into a concrete one like find 100 more sales from the same traffic.

The lift figures show the value of small improvements. A 0.5 percent lift on 10000 visitors is 50 extra conversions, and at an average order value of 1200 that is 60000 of extra revenue from the same visitors, with no extra ad spend. That is why improving a page often pays better than buying more traffic: the traffic you already have is the cheapest traffic you will ever get.

What a conversion rate really tells you

A conversion rate is a ratio, not a score. It tells you what share of your visitors did the thing you wanted them to do, and nothing else. A rate of 2 percent means 2 out of every 100 visitors converted and 98 did not — which sounds low until you remember that most visitors to most pages never buy anything at all. The rate is useful because it lets you compare pages and periods fairly, whatever the size of the traffic.

The rate hides as much as it reveals. It does not tell you whether the visitors were the right people, whether the product was right, or whether the price was right. A page with a low rate and high-value sales can earn more than a page with a high rate and tiny sales, and a page with a high rate from a small, warm audience can look better than a page doing the harder job of selling to strangers. Always read the rate next to the number of visitors, the average order value, and the total revenue.

The most useful comparison is with your own past numbers. If your page converted at 1.5 percent last month and 2 percent this month, something you changed helped — and if it fell, something hurt. Comparing your rate with another business is much less useful, because their product, price, audience and traffic source are all different. Use the calculator to track your own trend, and treat other people's rates as background, not as targets.

Worked examples: a shop, a signup page, a landing page

A small online shop had 10000 visitors and 200 sales in a month. The conversion rate is 200 divided by 10000, times 100, which is 2 percent. The average order value is 1200, so the month's revenue from those visitors is 240000. A 0.5 percent lift would add 50 sales and 60000 of revenue; a 1 percent lift would add 100 sales and 120000. To reach a target rate of 3 percent, the shop needs 300 sales — 100 more than it has now.

A signup page for a free course had 5000 visitors and 150 signups, which is a rate of 3 percent. Signups are not sales, so the average order value box stays blank and the calculator shows the rate and the target only. To reach 4 percent the page needs 200 signups, which is 50 more. Because signing up costs nothing, a higher rate is normal here — the barrier is lower, so more visitors say yes. Comparing this 3 percent with a shop's 2 percent would be meaningless: the actions are different.

A landing page for a paid workshop had 2000 visitors and 80 bookings, a rate of 4 percent. The ticket price is 5000, so the revenue is 400000. A 1 percent lift would add 20 bookings and 100000 of revenue — a big prize from a small page. Landing pages often convert higher than whole shops because they have one offer and one button, and every visitor arrived for that offer. If your landing page converts below your shop, the offer or the page is probably the problem, not the traffic.

What a good rate looks like by industry

Rates differ so much by industry that a single good number does not exist, and anyone who quotes one without context is guessing. As a rough guide, online shops typically convert between 1 and 3 percent of their visitors into buyers, and many sit near 2 percent. Pages that ask for something free — a signup, a download, a newsletter — typically convert higher, often 3 to 10 percent, because the visitor risks nothing. Pages that ask for something expensive or long-term, like a car or a mortgage enquiry, typically convert lower, often below 1 percent, because the decision is bigger.

Traffic source matters as much as industry. Visitors who arrive from a search for your exact product convert far better than visitors who arrived from a general social post, because the searchers already decided they want it. Visitors who arrive from an email to existing customers convert better than both, because they already trust you. So a page with a low overall rate may simply have a lot of cold traffic, and a page with a high rate may simply have a warm audience — the rate alone does not tell you which.

Use these ranges as a rough sense-check, never as a promise. If your shop converts at 0.5 percent, something is probably wrong — the page may be slow, confusing, or showing the wrong product to the wrong people — and fixing it is worth more than buying more traffic. If your shop converts at 4 percent, you are doing well, and the next gains will come from bigger orders or more visitors rather than from squeezing the rate further. Either way, the trend over time is the number that matters most.

How to raise your rate

Start with speed and clarity, because they cost nothing and help everyone. A page that loads slowly loses visitors before they ever see the offer, and a page that hides its price or its buy button loses the ones who stay. Make the main action obvious, put the price where people can see it, and remove anything that distracts from the one thing you want the visitor to do. Then test one change at a time and measure it with this calculator, so you know what actually helped.

Next, remove the reasons people leave. Visitors abandon a page when they do not trust it, do not understand it, or cannot find what they need. Clear delivery times, a simple returns promise, honest reviews and a working contact route all raise trust. Short, plain descriptions raise understanding. A search box and clear categories help visitors find things. None of these is glamorous, and all of them move the rate more than a new colour scheme.

Finally, match the page to the visitor. Someone who clicked an ad for running shoes should land on running shoes, not on your homepage. Someone who clicked a discount offer should see the discount, not a full-price list. Every extra step between the click and the buy button costs conversions, so count the steps on your own page and remove the ones that add nothing. A shorter path with fewer surprises converts better than a clever path with more of them.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is counting the wrong visitors. If you count every page view, including the same person coming back five times, your rate looks lower than it really is — and if you count only buyers, it looks higher. Use unique visitors for the period, and use the same definition every time you measure, so the trend means something. Changing the definition halfway through makes the numbers useless.

The second mistake is measuring too short a period. A hundred visitors is not enough to judge a rate: one extra sale swings it by a whole percent. Wait until you have at least a few hundred visitors — a thousand is better — before you draw conclusions, and remember that weekends, holidays and promotions all move the numbers. A rate measured over a sale weekend is not comparable with a rate measured over a quiet week.

The third mistake is chasing the rate instead of the revenue. A page can raise its rate by turning away cheap traffic and keeping only the keenest buyers — and earn less overall, because it has fewer sales. Or it can lower its price to lift the rate and lose money on every order. Always check the revenue alongside the rate, and judge a change by whether it earned more money, not by whether the percent went up.

What this calculator cannot do

This calculator cannot tell you why your visitors do or do not convert — that needs watching real people use the page, reading their messages, and testing changes one at a time. It cannot separate new visitors from returning ones, or buyers from browsers, unless you feed it numbers that are already split that way. It cannot read your website statistics for you, and it does not connect to any shop or tracking tool: you type the numbers in, and it does the arithmetic.

It also cannot set your target for you. A target rate is a business decision, not a maths problem — it depends on your margins, your traffic and what you are willing to spend to improve the page. Use the calculator to see what a target would require in real conversions, and to see what a small lift would earn, then decide whether the work is worth it. The arithmetic is honest; the decision is yours.

Questions people ask

What is a conversion rate?

A conversion rate is the share of your visitors who did the thing you wanted — bought, signed up, or booked. It is conversions divided by visitors, times 100. If 200 out of 10000 visitors bought, the rate is 2 percent.

What is a good conversion rate?

It depends on what you ask for and who visits. Online shops typically convert 1 to 3 percent of visitors into buyers, while free signup pages often convert higher. Compare your page with its own past numbers first — that trend matters more than anyone else's rate.

How do I raise my conversion rate?

Start with speed and clarity: a fast page, a clear offer, a visible price and one obvious next step. Then remove the reasons people leave — unclear delivery, no trust signals, a long path to the buy button. Change one thing at a time and measure it, so you know what helped.

Do I count every visitor or only unique visitors?

Count unique visitors for the period, so one person visiting five times counts once. If you count every page view, the rate looks lower than it really is. Whatever you choose, use the same definition every time so the trend is honest.

Why is my rate low but sales fine?

Because the rate is only one half of the story. A page with many visitors and a low rate can still sell a lot, and a page with high-value sales can earn well at a low rate. Check the revenue and the average order value alongside the rate before you worry.

How often should I check it?

Monthly is enough for most small pages, and weekly is plenty during a campaign or after a change. Check it over a long enough period to have at least a few hundred visitors, or the number will jump around too much to mean anything.
