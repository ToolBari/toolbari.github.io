How to use this planner

Start by entering your room's length and width in centimetres. The plan redraws itself to scale as soon as you change a number. Then enter the door: where it sits along the bottom wall, measured from the left corner, and how wide it is. The door is drawn as a gap in the bottom wall, with a dashed area in front of it — that dashed area is the space the door needs to open, and nothing should stand in it.

Next, add your furniture. Each button adds one piece at its usual size — a bed is 200 by 150 centimetres, a sofa 200 by 90, a table 150 by 90, a desk 120 by 60, a wardrobe 180 by 60 and a TV stand 150 by 45. The piece appears on the plan, and you drag it where you want it: press and hold with your finger on a phone, or click and hold with a mouse, then move it. Tap a piece to select it — the selected piece gets a thick green border — and then you can change its width and depth in the two boxes below the plan, or remove it.

When the room looks right, tap "Check my layout". The planner checks four things: whether any piece sticks out of the room, whether two pieces overlap, whether anything blocks the door, and whether any two pieces are closer than 60 centimetres apart, which makes a walking path too narrow. Problems are listed one by one so you can fix them. "Save plan" stores the whole plan in this browser on this device, so it is still here when you come back. "Copy plan" copies a text summary — room size, every piece with its size and position, and the check result — so you can paste it into a message or a note.

Measuring a room properly

A plan is only as good as its measurements, and most measuring mistakes come from rushing. Measure wall to wall, not from one piece of furniture to another. Use a tape measure, not your stride, and measure each wall in two places — near the floor and near the ceiling — because walls are rarely perfectly straight. If the two readings differ, use the smaller one: furniture must fit the tightest point, not the widest.

Write down the doors and windows too. For each door, note where it is along the wall and which way it opens — a door that swings into the room needs clear floor space in front of it, and that space is easy to forget when you are looking at an empty room. For each window, note the height of the sill from the floor: a low sill means a tall wardrobe cannot stand under the window, but a desk can. Note the radiators, the power sockets and the light switches as well, because a wardrobe in front of a socket is a wardrobe you will move again within a week.

Measure in centimetres if you can, and write the numbers down as you go — never trust your memory for six measurements. Measure twice: once when you first plan, and once more before you order anything. The second measure takes five minutes and catches the one number you wrote down wrong. This planner works in centimetres throughout, so if your tape is in feet and inches, convert first: one foot is about 30 centimetres, and one inch is about 2.5 centimetres.

The walking-space rule

A room can fit all its furniture and still be miserable to live in, if there is no room to walk. The rule is simple: a walking path needs at least 60 centimetres, and 90 centimetres is comfortable. Sixty centimetres is enough to walk through sideways with a cup of tea. Ninety centimetres lets two people pass, lets you carry a laundry basket, and lets a door open without hitting the furniture. The planner flags any gap under 60 centimetres as tight — not an error, but a warning worth listening to.

Think about the paths, not just the pieces. The path from the door to the bed, the path from the bed to the wardrobe, and the path to the window are the ones you will walk every day. A room with a generous gap in one corner and a squeeze everywhere else is worse than a room with even, modest gaps. When you drag pieces around, look at the spaces between them, not just at the pieces themselves. The empty floor is part of the plan.

Some pieces need extra space of their own. A wardrobe with doors needs about 60 centimetres in front of it so the doors can open and you can stand there. A desk chair needs about 70 centimetres behind the desk so you can push the chair back and stand up. A bed needs at least 60 centimetres on the side you get out of — more if two people share it. These clearances are part of the piece's real footprint, even though they are not part of its size. The planner's 60-centimetre warning is a good start, but add more where a piece needs room to be used, not just room to stand.

Planning around doors and windows

Doors and windows decide a room's layout more than the furniture does, because they cannot be moved. Start every plan by marking them. A door needs two things: the space to swing open, and a clear path through it. The planner draws the door gap and the clear area in front of it, and flags anything that blocks it. But remember that the planner draws the door on the bottom wall of the plan — if your real door is on a different wall, turn the plan in your head, or rotate your measurements so the door wall becomes the bottom one.

Windows decide where the light falls. A desk near a window gets daylight, which is good for working and reading. A bed under a window can be cold in winter and bright at dawn, which some people love and others hate. A television facing a window catches glare. None of these are rules — they are things to notice while you plan. Put the pieces that need light near the window, and the pieces that suffer from glare away from it.

Also think about what the room is for at different times of day. A bedroom is a sleeping room at night and a dressing room in the morning. A living room is a sitting room in the evening and sometimes a dining room at lunch. A layout that works for one use and blocks another is only half a plan. Walk through the day in your head — waking up, getting dressed, sitting down, opening the wardrobe, leaving the room — and check that each move has a clear path.

Worked example: a small bedroom

Take a small bedroom 360 centimetres long and 300 centimetres wide, with the door on the bottom wall near the left corner. The furniture is a bed 200 by 150, a desk 120 by 60 and a wardrobe 180 by 60. That is a lot of furniture for a small room, so the layout matters. Put the bed in the top-left corner, along the left wall. Put the wardrobe along the right wall, with its long side against the wall, so it takes 60 centimetres of depth and leaves the middle of the room open. Put the desk in the bottom-left corner, under the window if there is one there.

Now check the gaps. Between the foot of the bed and the desk there are 60 centimetres — the minimum walking path, and the planner will not flag it, because 60 is the limit, not under it. Between the bed and the wardrobe there are about 100 centimetres, which is a comfortable path. The door area is clear: the desk sits to the left of the door and the wardrobe to the right, and nothing stands in front of the opening. Everything fits, and every path works — but only just. If the bed were 10 centimetres longer, the path to the desk would drop below 60 and the planner would flag it.

Try this example in the planner: set the room to 360 by 300, set the door at 200 centimetres from the left corner and 90 wide, then add the three pieces and drag them into place. Run the check and see the clean result. Then drag the desk 20 centimetres closer to the bed and run the check again — the tight-path warning appears. That is how the planner earns its keep: it catches the squeeze before you buy the furniture, not after.

Common mistakes

The most common mistake is forgetting the door swing. People plan the room with the door closed in their mind, then the real door opens and hits the wardrobe. Always leave the space in front of the door clear, and remember which way the door opens — into the room or out of it. If the door opens into the room, the clear area is inside the room. The planner flags anything blocking the door zone, but only if you entered the door position correctly, so measure it.

The second mistake is furniture that fits the room but not the doorway. A wardrobe 180 centimetres wide fits a 360-centimetre wall beautifully — and may not fit through a 75-centimetre door at all. Measure the doorway, the hallway and the stairs before you buy, not after the delivery van arrives. The Furniture Size Planner on ToolBari checks exactly this: whether a piece fits the room, the door and the stairs. The third mistake is planning for the room as it is, not as it will be used. A desk facing a blank wall is fine for storage and poor for work. A sofa with its back to the door feels wrong every time someone walks in.

A fourth mistake is trusting one measurement. Walls lean, floors slope, and tape measures slip. Measure twice, and use the smaller reading where two readings differ. And a fifth mistake is filling every centimetre. A room with no empty floor feels smaller than it is, and there is nowhere to put the next thing you buy. Leave one stretch of wall and one patch of floor empty on purpose — the room will feel bigger, and you will have room to change your mind.

What this planner cannot do

This planner cannot see your real room. It does not know about sloping ceilings, radiators, pipes, power sockets, uneven floors or windows that open inward. It draws a rectangle with a door — if your room is L-shaped, has an alcove, or has a bay window, the plan is an approximation, and you must allow for the parts it cannot draw. It cannot check whether your floor can take the weight of a heavy wardrobe, or whether a wall is strong enough to hold a shelf.

It also cannot replace measuring. The plan is only as accurate as the numbers you type in, and a 10-centimetre error in a room measurement can turn a comfortable layout into a squeeze. Measure twice, check the doorway and the stairs, and treat the plan as a sketch that helps you think — not as a promise that the furniture will fit. For a full renovation, a moved wall, or anything structural, ask a qualified professional. This planner helps you arrange furniture; it does not design buildings.

Questions people ask

How do I measure my room?

Use a tape measure and measure wall to wall, in centimetres, along the length and the width. Measure each wall in two places and use the smaller reading, because walls are rarely perfectly straight. Note the doors and windows too — where they are and which way the doors open — then measure everything a second time before you buy.

How much walking space do I need?

A walking path needs at least 60 centimetres, and 90 centimetres is comfortable. Sixty is enough to walk through; ninety lets two people pass and lets you carry things. This planner flags any gap under 60 centimetres as tight. Remember that wardrobes need about 60 centimetres in front for the doors, and a desk chair needs about 70 centimetres behind the desk.

Can I save my plan?

Yes. Tap Save plan and the whole plan — room size, door and every piece with its position — is stored in this browser on this device, so it is still here when you come back. Copy plan gives you a text summary you can paste into a message or a note. The plan is only saved on this device, so it will not appear on another phone or computer.

Does this work on my phone?

Yes. The planner is made to work by touch: press and hold a piece with your finger and drag it where you want it. The plan fits the phone screen, the buttons are large, and nothing needs a mouse or a keyboard. It works the same way on a desktop with a mouse.

What if my room is not a rectangle?

Measure the biggest rectangle that fits inside the room and plan with that, or split an L-shaped room into two rectangles and plan each part. Note the odd corner, alcove or bay window separately, and leave extra space around it. The planner draws a rectangle, so for an unusual room it is a starting sketch — check the real corners with a tape before you buy.

How do I know if a sofa will fit through the door?

Measure the sofa's depth and height and compare them with the door's width and height — the depth is usually the problem, not the length. A sofa can often be turned on its side to pass, and taking the legs off can save 10 to 15 centimetres.
