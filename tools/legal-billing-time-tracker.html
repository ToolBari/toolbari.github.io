What this tracker does and who it is for

Time is the raw material of legal work, and it is the easiest thing to lose track of. A call here, a draft there, a quick piece of research that takes an hour — by the end of the week, the hours are scattered across memory, notebooks, and good intentions. When the fee note is due, the choice is between guessing and reconstructing the week from fragments. Guessing undercharges the work; reconstructing takes an evening. Neither is good practice.

This tracker keeps the record as the work happens. Start the timer when a task begins and stop it when the task ends, or add entries by hand for work already done: the client or matter, the date, the hours, the rate, and a short note about what was done. The tracker adds up the hours and the fees per client, shows the totals, and copies the whole billing summary out as plain text.

It is built for lawyers and small firms who bill by the hour and want a simple, honest record without signing up for a system. It is not an invoicing tool and it does not send anything to anyone: no invoices are issued, no payments are taken, and no client data leaves this browser. It is the notebook, kept properly, with the arithmetic done for you.

How to use the tracker, step by step

There are two ways to record time. The first is the timer: tap Start timer when you begin a task, and Stop timer when you finish. The timer shows the time passing, and stopping it puts the hours — to two decimal places — into the hours field of the form. Add the client or matter, the date, and the hourly rate, write a short note about what was done, and save the entry. The timer runs while this page is open, so keep the page open while you work.

The second way is a manual entry, for work already done. Fill in the same fields — client, date, hours, rate, description — and save. Hours can be entered as decimals: one and a half hours is 1.5. The rate is the hourly rate for that client or matter, entered in your own currency, as a plain number.

Every entry appears in the time entries list, with its hours and its fee worked out. The totals by client section adds up each client's entries, and the summary line shows the grand total. Delete an entry if it was entered by mistake. Copy the billing summary regularly — the entries live in this browser only, and the copy is your backup.

What the output means

Each entry shows the date, the client or matter, the hours in hours and minutes, the rate, and the fee for that entry — hours multiplied by rate. A ninety-minute call at a rate of 120 is 1.5 hours times 120, which is 180. The arithmetic is shown in plain numbers so it can be checked at a glance, and the summary can be copied into a fee note or a record without retyping.

The totals by client section groups the entries: for each client, the number of entries, the total hours, and the total fee. That is the figure the fee note is built from. Clients are matched by the exact name you type, so keep names consistent — Smith matter and Smith Matter count as two different clients, and the totals will split. Pick one spelling and use it every time.

The summary line gives the grand total across all clients: entries, hours, and fees. It is the state of the whole book at a glance. The tracker does not add tax, disbursements, or discounts — those belong on the fee note itself, under the rules where you practise. The tracker gives you the clean hours and fees; the fee note is where the rest is added.

Hours are shown in hours and minutes as well as decimals, because a client reads 1h 30m more easily than 1.5. The fee is always worked out from the decimal hours, so the two views agree: ninety minutes is 1.5 hours, and the fee is the same either way. If a figure ever looks wrong, check the hours first — a decimal point in the wrong place is the most common mistake.

A worked example

Imagine a sole practitioner with two clients this week. On Monday she spends 2 hours drafting a contract for ABC Ltd at a rate of 150, and 1.5 hours on a client call for the Patel file at a rate of 120. On Wednesday she spends 3 hours on court preparation for ABC Ltd, and on Friday 0.5 hours reviewing a document for the Patel file.

She records each piece of work as an entry. The tracker works out the fees: Monday's drafting is 2 times 150, which is 300; the call is 1.5 times 120, which is 180; Wednesday's preparation is 3 times 150, which is 450; Friday's review is 0.5 times 120, which is 60. The totals by client show ABC Ltd at 5 hours and 750, and the Patel file at 2 hours and 240.

The grand total is 7 hours and 990. When the fee notes are due, she copies the billing summary, which lists every entry with its date, hours, rate and fee, and the totals per client. The fee notes take minutes, every figure is traceable to a dated entry, and nothing was reconstructed from memory at the end of the month.

Tips for a good result

Record time the day the work is done. Time recorded the same day is accurate; time recorded at the end of the week is a reconstruction, and reconstructions always lose the small pieces — the ten-minute call, the quick email, the short review. The timer helps here: start it when the task starts, and the hours are captured whether you remember them or not.

Write a short description on every entry. A fee note that says what was done is easier for a client to accept than one that lists hours alone, and a description written today is evidence of the work done. Keep descriptions short and factual — what was done, not a commentary — and never put anything in a description that you would not want the client to read.

Keep client names consistent, set the rate when you save the entry, and review the totals weekly. A weekly review catches mistakes while they are fresh — a wrong rate, a duplicated entry, a missing day — and keeps the billing summary ready to use. Copy the summary after each review and keep it with the firm's records.

One more habit that pays off: keep the rate with the entry, not in your head. Rates change — a new rate for a new year, a different rate for a different client — and an entry saved without its rate is an entry that has to be priced later, when the rate is harder to remember. Enter the rate when you save the entry, and the fee is worked out at once, while the work is fresh.

Limits of the tracker — what it cannot do

This tracker records time and works out fees; it does not issue invoices, take payments, or send anything to a client. The billing summary is a record for your own use, and turning it into a fee note — with the right wording, tax treatment, and disbursements — is your job, under the rules where you practise.

It does not know the fee rules, billing duties, or record-keeping requirements where you work. Those differ by place and by professional body, and they change. It does not check that a rate is reasonable, that the time was properly spent, or that a fee is recoverable. It does arithmetic; the professional judgement is yours.

The timer runs only while this page is open, and the entries live in this browser only. If the browser data is cleared, the entries are gone — copy the summary regularly and keep the copy with the firm's records. Used within these limits, the tracker does one job well: it keeps an honest, dated record of the hours, so the fee note is built on facts instead of memory.

Questions people ask

How do I track billable time accurately?

Record time the day the work is done, using the timer or a manual entry. Same-day records are accurate; end-of-week reconstructions always lose the small pieces.

What should a time entry record?

The client or matter, the date, the hours, the hourly rate, and a short note about what was done. A description makes the fee note easier for a client to accept.

How is a legal fee worked out from hours?

Each entry's fee is its hours multiplied by its hourly rate. The tracker adds the entries per client, and the grand total adds every client together.

Can I track time for several clients?

Yes. Entries are grouped by the client or matter name, and the totals section shows each client's hours and fees separately. Keep names spelled the same way every time.

Can I print a billing summary?

Yes. Tap the copy button to copy the whole summary as plain text, paste it into any document or note, and print it from there.

What should a fee note include?

The work done, the dates, the hours and the rate, the fee, and any tax or disbursements under local rules. This tracker gives you the hours and fees; the fee note itself is your document.
