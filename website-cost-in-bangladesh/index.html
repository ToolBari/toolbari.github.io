What makes up the cost of a website

A website price is not one single charge — it is made of a few parts that fit together. The first part is the domain name, the address people type to reach the site. A domain is normally bought for a year at a time and renewed each year, and it belongs to whoever it is registered to — that is why it matters that it is bought in your own name. The second part is hosting, the space on a server where the site's files live so visitors can open it at any time. The third part is the build itself: the design, the pages, and the way everything is put together. The last part is upkeep — the small jobs that keep a site working after it goes live, like updating text or fixing a broken link. When you compare prices, ask which parts are included and which are charged separately.

What changes the price

Two websites can both be called a "business website" and still cost very different amounts, because the price follows the work involved. The number of pages is the most obvious factor: a single landing page is a smaller job than a site with five or more pages of content. A shop costs more than a simple information site because it needs product pages, a way for customers to order, and a way to handle payments. Custom design also adds to the price — a design made from scratch takes longer than one built from a standard layout. The content matters too: if your text and photos are already ready, the build moves faster. None of these is a hidden charge; they are simply more work, and it is fair to ask how each one affects the quote you are given.

Building it yourself vs hiring someone

There are two broad ways to get a website: build it yourself with a website builder, or hire someone to build it for you. Doing it yourself saves the build fee, but it costs your time instead — learning the tool, designing the pages, writing the content, and fixing the things that go wrong. For a simple personal page, that can be a fair trade. For a business site, the time adds up quickly, and a half-finished site that never goes live helps nobody. Hiring someone means paying for the build, but the work is done by a person who does it every day. A good middle path is to prepare as much as you can yourself — your business name, your text and photos — and let the builder handle the design and the technical work. Questions to ask before you pay

Before you agree to any price, it is worth asking a few plain questions. The answers tell you what you are actually buying, and they make it much easier to compare one offer with another.

  • What exactly is included in the price — design, pages, hosting, and the domain?
  • Who owns the domain name, and is it registered in my name?
  • What happens if I want to change the text or add a page later?
  • How do I pay, and when is the balance due?
  • What do you need from me before work can start?

Common questions, answered in plain words

Do I need a website if I already have a Facebook page?

A Facebook page is a good place to reach people, but it is not a website. A page on a social platform belongs to the platform: its rules can change and its reach can drop. A website of your own is an address you control, where customers can find your prices, your contact details, and your work in one place. Many small businesses use both — social media to be found, and a website to be trusted.

What should I prepare before ordering a website?

The build goes much faster when a few things are ready before work starts. Write down what the site should do — show products, take orders, or simply explain the business — and gather your text, photos, logo, and contact details. If you sell things, make a list of your products with prices. If you are a freelancer building a portfolio, the guide to starting freelancing explains how a simple site fits into finding your first clients. The more you prepare, the fewer rounds of changes the build needs.
