How to Check If a Link Is Safe

Before you click any link, you can see where it really goes — hover over it on a computer, or press and hold it on a phone, and read the address that appears. This guide shows you how to preview a link, spot shortened and lookalike addresses, check the sender, and what to do if you already clicked.

A link is only as honest as the address behind it. The words on a link can say anything — a familiar company name, a friendly invitation, an urgent warning — while the real destination points somewhere else entirely. Most harmful links rely on one thing: that you click first and look later. The checks in this guide take less than a minute, and they work for links in email, text messages, chat apps and social media alike.

This guide gives general advice about checking links. It is not incident-specific security advice. If you think your accounts or money are at risk, contact your bank or the service involved directly through their official channels.

Preview the link before you click

The single most useful habit is also the simplest: look at where a link goes before you tap it. On a computer, move your mouse over the link without clicking and read the address that appears at the bottom of the browser window. On a phone, press and hold the link until a small preview of the address appears above your finger. Neither action opens the link — you are only peeking at it.

Once you can see the address, read it carefully, and read it from right to left. The part just before the first single slash is the real domain — the website the link actually opens. Everything after that slash is just a page on that site, and everything before the domain is only part of the address. A link that claims to be from your bank should show your bank's own domain in that spot. If it shows a name you do not recognise, do not click.

If no preview appears at all, or the address looks nothing like the sender who shared it, treat that as a warning in itself. When in doubt, skip the link entirely: open your browser, type the address you already know, or open the official app, and check there instead. If the message is genuine, the same information will be waiting for you in your account.

Shortened links: what hides behind them

A shortened link is a short address that redirects to a longer one. The problem is that the short form hides the destination completely — you cannot tell where it leads before you click, and the preview trick above only shows you the short address, not the place it points to. That is exactly why scammers like them.

Short links are not always bad. Many legitimate senders use them to keep long addresses tidy, especially in text messages and social posts where space is short. The risk depends on the context: a short link from a sender you trust, about something you were expecting, is usually fine. A short link in an unexpected message — a parcel you did not order, a prize you did not enter, a warning about an account you do not have — should be treated with suspicion.

Never click a short link just to find out where it goes. If the message claims to be important, find the same information another way: open the official app, or type the company's address into your browser yourself. If the message was genuine, nothing is lost; if it was fake, you have avoided the trap.

Lookalike domains and how to spot them

A lookalike domain is a web address designed to look like a real company's address at a glance. Scammers register these addresses and build pages that copy the real site, hoping you will not notice the difference. The tricks are small, and they all rely on fast reading.

Look for these patterns when you preview an address:

  • A misspelled brand name, such as exampel.com instead of example.com — one letter swapped or missing.
  • Extra words added to a real name, such as secure-example.com or example-support.net, where the brand is only part of a longer name.
  • A real brand name buried in the middle, such as example.com.login-page.net — the real domain here is login-page.net, not example.com.
  • A different ending on the same name, such as example.org when the real company uses example.com.

The defence is to slow down. Read the address letter by letter, from right to left, and ask yourself whether the domain is exactly the one you expect. If you are unsure, do not use the link: type the address you know into your browser and go there directly.

Check the sender, not just the link

A link does not arrive on its own — it arrives in a message from someone. Checking that someone is often faster than checking the link, and just as revealing. Start with who the message claims to be from, then check whether the details match.

In an email, look at the sender name and the full sender address shown by your mail app. The name on display can say anything, so the address behind it is what matters. A message claiming to be from your bank but sent from a free mailbox or a misspelled domain is a strong red flag. In a text message, look at the sender number or name shown at the top. Delivery companies and banks usually send from a short code or a named sender, not from a random mobile number. The same applies to chat apps: check the profile, the number, and whether you have spoken to this contact before. A new account with no photo and no history that suddenly sends you a link is a classic sign of a fake.

Also ask yourself whether the message fits. Did you order a parcel? Do you have an account with that company? Did you enter a competition? If the answer is no, the message is almost certainly not for you. Scammers send the same message to thousands of people and rely on a few of them having a parcel on the way or an account with that company.

What to do if you already clicked

First, do not panic — and do not enter anything else on the page. Close the tab or the app, and do not download any file the page offers. If you only clicked and entered nothing, the risk is low, but keep an eye on your accounts for anything unusual over the next few days.

If you entered a password, change it right away — on the real site, reached by typing the address yourself — and change it anywhere else you used the same password. If you entered card details, contact your bank straight away and ask them to watch the card or block it. If you shared a one-time code, sign in to the account, change the password, and check the recent activity and the recovery settings. Tell the real company what happened through their official contact channels, so they can flag the fake message.

Reporting the message helps protect the next person who receives it. Most email apps have a report option in the message menu — use it, and the message goes to your provider's abuse team. On a phone, forward a suspicious text to your network operator's spam reporting number if your country has one, then delete the message. Many chat apps let you report and block a contact from the chat menu. When you report, do not forward the message to friends or post screenshots of it in public groups — the link inside it is still live, and someone else might click it.

Questions people ask

How can I see where a link goes without clicking?

On a computer, hover your mouse over the link and read the address that appears at the bottom of the browser window. On a phone, press and hold the link until a preview of the address appears. Read the address carefully, from right to left, because the part just before the first single slash is the real domain.

Are shortened links dangerous?

Not always, but they hide the destination, so you cannot tell where they lead before you click. A short link from a sender you trust is usually fine, but one in an unexpected message should be treated with suspicion. If the message matters, find the same information through the official app or website instead of using the link.

What is a lookalike domain?

A web address designed to look like a real company's address at a glance. Common tricks include a misspelled brand name, extra words added to the name, or the real brand name buried in the middle of a longer address. Reading the address slowly, from right to left, usually exposes the trick.

What should I do if I clicked a suspicious link?

Close the tab or app and do not enter anything else on the page. If you entered a password, change it right away on the real site, and change it anywhere else you used the same one. If you entered card details, contact your bank immediately, and report the message through your email app or chat app.
