The formula

Future value = P × (1 + r ÷ n)n × t — P is the principal, r the annual rate (as a decimal), n the number of compounding periods per year, and t the term in years. Total interest is simply the future value minus the principal.

Worked examples

  1. 10,000 at 10% for 2 years, yearly: 10,000 × 1.10² = 12,100 — interest earned 2,100.
  2. The same money, compounded monthly: 10,000 × (1 + 0.10 ÷ 12)24 = 12,203.91 — about 103.91 more than yearly compounding.
  3. 50,000 at 8% for 5 years, quarterly: 50,000 × (1 + 0.08 ÷ 4)20 = 74,297.37 — interest earned 24,297.37.

Questions people ask

How is compound interest calculated?

Future value = P × (1 + r ÷ n) raised to the power of n × t, where P is the principal, r is the annual rate as a decimal, n is how many times interest compounds per year, and t is the term in years. For example, 10,000 at 10% for 2 years compounded yearly becomes 10,000 × 1.10² = 12,100.

What is the difference between simple and compound interest?

Simple interest is paid only on the original principal. Compound interest is paid on the principal plus the interest already earned, so the balance grows faster the longer it runs — 10,000 at 10% for 2 years earns 2,000 as simple interest but 2,100 compounded yearly.

Does compounding more often make a big difference?

A small one at the same rate: 10,000 at 10% for 2 years grows to 12,100 compounded yearly and 12,203.91 compounded monthly — about 103.91 more. The rate and the number of years matter far more than the frequency.
