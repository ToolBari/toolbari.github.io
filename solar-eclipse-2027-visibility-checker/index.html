Total, partial, or nothing

A solar eclipse looks different depending on where you stand. If you are inside the path of totality, the Moon covers the Sun completely for a few minutes, the sky goes dark, and you can see the Sun's corona glowing around the dark disc. That is a total eclipse, and it is one of the most striking things in nature.

If you are outside the path but near it, you see a partial eclipse. The Moon covers part of the Sun, the light dims a little, and the Sun keeps the shape of a crescent. The sky does not go dark, and the Sun is never fully hidden. A deep partial eclipse, where most of the Sun is covered, is still a partial eclipse — impressive, but not totality.

Far from the path, there is nothing to see at all. This checker covers three outcomes for the cities in its table: total, partial, and not in our list. For a city we have no data for, it says so honestly instead of guessing, because a wrong answer about an eclipse would be worse than no answer.

Cities inside the path

Seven cities in our table are inside the path of totality on 2 August 2027. From west to east, they are Cadiz in southern Spain, Tangier in Morocco, Tunis in Tunisia, Luxor and Sohag in Egypt, Jeddah in Saudi Arabia, and Sana'a in Yemen. The path itself is about 258 kilometres wide, and it also crosses Gibraltar, Algeria, Libya and north-eastern Somalia.

From any of these cities, the Sun is completely covered for several minutes. Luxor has the longest totality of the cities in the table, about 6 minutes 20 seconds, and the longest totality on land anywhere is about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor. That is the longest total solar eclipse visible from land until 2114.

If you are in one of these cities on the day, you do not need to travel anywhere to see totality. You need a clear view of the sky, a safe way to look at the Sun, and a plan for the heat in the middle of the day, especially in Egypt.

Just outside the path

Two cities in our table sit just outside the path, and they are worth knowing about because they are easy to get wrong. Aswan, in southern Egypt, is not in the path: it sees a deep partial eclipse, where a large part of the Sun is covered but the Sun is never fully hidden. Cairo, in northern Egypt, sees a partial eclipse only.

The difference between a deep partial eclipse and totality is bigger than it sounds. Even a small uncovered sliver of Sun is bright enough to keep the day looking like day. Only inside the path does the Sun disappear completely and the sky go dark. Someone in Aswan will see a dramatic crescent Sun; someone in Luxor, not far to the north, will see the sky go dark.

If you are in Aswan or Cairo and want to see totality, the honest answer is that you need to travel into the path. Luxor and Sohag are the nearest cities in our table that see totality, and the trip is worth planning well ahead of the day.

What totality looks like

Totality begins when the Moon covers the last sliver of the Sun. The light drops suddenly, the temperature falls a little, and the sky goes dark enough for the brightest stars to appear. Around the black disc of the Moon, the Sun's corona glows — a pale, wispy halo that is normally hidden by the Sun's brightness.

During totality only, it is safe to look directly at the eclipse without glasses, because the Sun's bright disc is fully covered. The moment the Sun starts to reappear, the glasses must go straight back on. That moment comes without warning, so it is worth having your glasses in your hand, not in your pocket.

A partial eclipse never looks like this. Through eclipse glasses, the Sun is a crescent that slowly shrinks and grows again, and the light dims but the day stays day. It is still worth watching, but it is a different experience from the sudden darkness of totality.

Planning around what you will see

Your plans depend on which side of the path's edge you are on. If your city sees totality, plan to be outside with a clear view of the sky in the early afternoon, with shade and water nearby. In Egypt, the midday heat can reach about 43 degrees, so the hours around totality need as much planning as totality itself.

If your city sees only a partial eclipse, you have a choice. You can watch the partial eclipse from where you are, which is still a fine sight through proper glasses, or you can travel into the path for totality. The partial eclipse is visible from a much wider area than totality, so even a short trip can make the difference.

Whatever you decide, check the timing for your exact spot with a local guide. Our table gives the published window for Luxor only, and every other place needs its own local timing. The checker tells you what kind of eclipse to expect; the local guide tells you when.

Safety first, always

The safety rules for an eclipse are simple, and they do not change. Look at the Sun only through eclipse glasses that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard. Ordinary sunglasses are never safe, however dark they seem. Smoked glass, camera film and homemade filters are never safe either.

A pinhole viewer is a safe indirect way to watch, and it is easy to make. A small hole in a piece of card projects an image of the Sun onto a surface, so you watch the projection instead of the Sun itself. It is a good option for children, for groups, and for anyone who does not have certified glasses.

Everything on this page about safety is general information only, not medical advice. If you are unsure about your glasses, your eyes or your plans, ask a local expert before the day. The eclipse is a rare and beautiful event, and it is worth watching safely.

Questions people ask

What is the difference between a total and a partial eclipse?

In a total eclipse the Moon covers the Sun completely and the sky goes dark for a few minutes. In a partial eclipse the Moon covers only part of the Sun, the sky stays light, and the Sun is never fully hidden.

Use the checker above. It covers nine cities: seven inside the path, Aswan and Cairo outside it. For any other city it will say it is not in our list, and a local eclipse guide will have the answer.

Is Aswan in the path of totality?

No. Aswan sees a deep partial eclipse only, so the Sun is never fully covered there.

What will Cairo see?

Cairo sees a partial eclipse only. The Sun is never fully covered there, so the sky does not go dark.

Is it safe to watch with sunglasses?
