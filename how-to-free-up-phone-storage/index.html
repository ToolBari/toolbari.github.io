How to Free Up Phone Storage

A full phone usually does not need new storage — it needs a tidy-up. Start by seeing what is using the space, then compress your photos and videos, clear out caches and downloads, and move the files you want to keep somewhere safe.

Most phones fill up gradually, a few photos and downloads at a time, until one day there is no room left for a new photo or an update. The fix is rarely to buy more storage or to delete your memories. It is a short tidy-up, done in the right order: find out what is taking the space, shrink what you can shrink, remove what you no longer need, and move the rest somewhere safe. Set aside twenty minutes and work through the steps one by one.

First, see what is eating the space

Before you delete anything, look at the storage breakdown on your phone. In your phone's settings there is a storage screen that shows how the space is divided: photos and videos, apps, system files, and everything else.

The breakdown tells you where to start. If photos and videos take the largest share, that is where the biggest wins are. If apps dominate, look at the apps you no longer open. Checking the breakdown first stops you from deleting things at random and hoping for the best. It is worth glancing at this screen once a month, so a small tidy-up each month replaces one big clear-out when the phone stops working.

Photos and videos: compress before you delete

Photos and videos are usually the biggest thing on a phone, and videos take far more space than photos, which is why the camera roll is almost always the first place to look. The good news is that you rarely need to delete anything to make room.

Compress first, and delete last. Compression shrinks a photo or a video file so it takes far less space, and the result still looks fine on a phone screen. Our How to Compress an Image guide shows how it works. Compressing the photos you keep is almost always better than deleting photos you might want later.

Then delete only what you truly do not want. Every camera roll has them: burst shots where ten photos were taken and one is good, blurry photos, accidental screenshots, and photos of things you needed once and never again. Delete the extras and keep the best shot. The same goes for videos: a clip you filmed to send to someone once does not need to live on your phone forever. One rule before you delete anything: back up the photos that matter first, check that the copies open, and only then remove them from the phone.

Clear app caches the safe way

Apps store temporary files, called a cache, so they can load faster next time. A browser keeps copies of pages you visited, and a video app keeps thumbnails. On their own these files are small, but over months they pile up, and a handful of well-used apps can quietly hold a large share of your storage.

Clearing a cache is safe. It removes only temporary files that the app can rebuild, and it does not touch your account, your messages, or your saved work. The app may take a little longer to open the first time after a cache clear, and then it behaves exactly as before. To clear one, open your phone's settings, find the list of apps, pick an app, and look for its storage options. Start with the apps you use most, since those are the ones with the largest caches.

Watch out for one lookalike button: "clear data". Clearing data is very different from clearing a cache. It signs you out, wipes the app's settings, and returns the app to a fresh state. Only use it when that is what you mean to do.

Tidy the downloads folder

The downloads folder is where forgotten files go to live. Every file you have ever downloaded and opened once is probably still there: documents, PDFs, installers, and images sent to you in messages. Most of them served their purpose on the day they arrived.

Open your file manager and look at the downloads folder, sorted by size so the largest files show first. Delete what you no longer need: old documents, files you already have elsewhere, and anything you cannot remember downloading. If you are unsure about a file, open it before deciding — a quick look is faster than wondering later where it went.

Chat apps deserve a look too. Many of them save every photo and video you receive straight to the phone, and a busy group chat can fill a phone in weeks. Look for the media settings in your chat apps and turn off automatic saving, then delete the received media you do not need.

Move files off the phone

Some files are worth keeping but do not need to live on your phone. Old videos, whole photo albums from past years, and documents you rarely open are all good candidates for moving elsewhere.

The simplest way is a cable to your computer. Connect the phone, copy the photos, videos and documents across, and check that the copies open properly before you delete anything from the phone. Cloud storage works the same way: upload the files, wait for the upload to finish, open a few of them to confirm they arrived, and then remove them from the phone.

Keep one rule in mind while you move things: never let a file exist in only one place during the move. Copy it first, check the copy, and only then delete the original. Once the copy is safe, the phone copy is just taking up room.

What you should never delete

Not everything on a phone is safe to remove, and a careless clear-out can cause more trouble than a full phone. The first rule is never to delete system files or pre-installed system apps. They belong to the phone itself, and removing them can stop updates from working or break features you rely on.

The second rule is never to delete the only copy of something important. Photos, documents, chat histories and voice notes that exist only on your phone should be backed up before anything is removed. A file with no copy anywhere else is one accident away from being lost forever.

Third, leave alone any file inside an app's own folder that you do not recognise. Apps store their working files in folders with names that mean nothing to a person, and deleting one can corrupt the app's data. If you do not know what a file is, do not delete it. And keep the files you still need, such as tickets and booking confirmations for an upcoming trip. When in doubt, move the file somewhere safe instead of deleting it.

A quick monthly tidy routine

Once the phone has room again, keep it that way with a short routine once a month. Five quick steps are enough.

  1. Check the storage breakdown and see what has grown since last month.
  2. Delete screenshots, duplicates and blurry photos from the camera roll.
  3. Clear the caches of the apps you use most.
  4. Empty the downloads folder of files you no longer need.
  5. Move the month's photos and videos to your backup, then remove them from the phone.

Done regularly, this routine keeps the phone comfortable and means the "storage full" warning never comes back. The best time to do it is a fixed day each month, so it becomes a habit rather than a chore you keep putting off.

Questions people ask

What usually takes the most phone storage?

Photos and videos are usually the largest share of a phone's storage, and videos take far more space than photos. After that come apps and the data they store, and files in the downloads folder. The storage screen in your phone's settings shows the exact breakdown for your own phone.

Is it safe to clear an app's cache?

Yes. Clearing a cache only removes temporary files that the app can rebuild, and it does not delete your account, your messages or your saved work. Do not confuse it with clearing data, which signs you out and resets the app to a fresh state.

Should I delete photos or compress them?

Compress first. Compression shrinks photos and videos so they take far less space while still looking fine on a phone screen. Delete only the shots you do not want, such as duplicates, blurry photos and old screenshots, and back up the photos that matter before deleting anything.

What should I never delete from my phone?

Never delete system files, and never delete the only copy of something important. Back up photos, documents and chats before removing them from the phone, and leave alone any file inside an app's folder that you do not recognise. When in doubt, move the file to a safe place instead of deleting it.
