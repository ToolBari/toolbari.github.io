How the conversion works

This converter uses the standard Kuwaiti algorithm, the best-known arithmetic method for moving between the Hijri and Gregorian calendars. It works in two steps: first it turns the date you enter into a Julian day number, which is a simple count of days, and then it turns that count back into a date on the other calendar. The same method runs in both directions.

The algorithm treats each Hijri month as lasting 29 or 30 days in a fixed pattern, and each Hijri year as 354 or 355 days, with extra days added in a thirty-year cycle. That pattern matches the real moon closely over long periods, but it is still a pattern, not a sighting, which is why every result on this page is labelled approximate.

The thirty-year cycle

The Kuwaiti algorithm keeps the calendar in step with the moon by using a thirty-year cycle. In each cycle, eleven years are leap years of 355 days and the other nineteen are ordinary years of 354 days. The leap years fall in a fixed pattern: the 2nd, 5th, 7th, 10th, 13th, 16th, 18th, 21st, 24th, 26th, and 29th years of each cycle.

That pattern is what the formula in this converter encodes. The extra day in a leap year is added to Dhul-Hijjah, the last month, which then has 30 days instead of 29. Over a full thirty-year cycle, the calendar gains 10,631 days, which matches 360 lunar months closely enough that the algorithm stays accurate to about a day for centuries in either direction.

Why every result is approximate

The months of the Islamic calendar begin with the sighting of the new crescent moon. Clouds, geography, and local practice all affect when the moon is seen, so the same month can begin on different days in different places. No formula can predict a sighting exactly, and any converter that claimed to would be misleading you.

The Kuwaiti algorithm is accurate to about a day for most dates, which makes it very useful for planning, for working out roughly when a Hijri date falls, and for converting birthdays and anniversaries. It is not a substitute for the local moon-sighting announcement when the exact date matters, such as the start of Ramadan or the day of Eid.

The Hijri months

The Islamic year has twelve lunar months: Muharram, Safar, Rabi al-Awwal, Rabi al-Thani, Jumada al-Ula, Jumada al-Akhirah, Rajab, Shaban, Ramadan, Shawwal, Dhul-Qadah, and Dhul-Hijjah. Each month lasts 29 or 30 days, depending on when the new moon is sighted, so the year has 354 days in an ordinary year and 355 in a leap year.

Ramadan, the ninth month, is the month of fasting, and Shawwal, the tenth, begins with Eid al-Fitr. Dhul-Hijjah, the twelfth month, is the month of the Hajj pilgrimage, and Eid al-Adha falls on the tenth day of it. The other months are quieter in the calendar, but each has its own place in the rhythm of the year.

When the exact date matters

For most purposes, being within a day is more than good enough. But for the start of Ramadan, the day of Eid, and the days of Hajj, the exact date matters, and those dates are set by moon sighting, not by calculation. Mosques and Islamic authorities announce them once the moon has been seen, and their word is the one to follow.

Use this converter to plan ahead and to know roughly when to expect an announcement, then wait for the confirmed date. Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin on 8 February 2027 and Eid al-Fitr on 9 March 2027, but both depend on the sighting, and the confirmed dates may move by a day.

How long is a Hijri year?

A Hijri year is about 354 or 355 days long, which is about eleven days shorter than a solar year. That is why Ramadan moves earlier through the seasons year by year: each Ramadan begins about eleven days earlier than the one before, and over about thirty-three years it travels all the way around the seasons.

The short year is also why the Hijri year number grows faster than the Gregorian one. The calendar began in 622 of the Gregorian era, and by 2027 the Hijri year has reached 1448, because 1,405 solar years hold about 1,448 lunar years. The converter handles all of that arithmetic for you.
