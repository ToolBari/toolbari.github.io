What SEO is, in plain words

SEO stands for search engine optimisation, which is a long name for a simple idea: making your pages easy for a search engine to find, understand and show to the right people. A search engine keeps a giant index of pages from across the web, and when someone types a question or a few words into the search box, it picks the pages it believes answer that search best. SEO is the work you do on your own pages so that the search engine can see clearly what each page is about, trust that the page is real and useful, and show it to the people who are looking for exactly that.

That is all it is. There is no secret trick, and there is no single setting that puts a page at the top. Good SEO is a handful of ordinary jobs done well: a clear title that says what the page is about, a short description that tells a reader why they should open it, content that actually answers the question, pages that load quickly and work on a phone, and links that go where they promise to go. The tools on this page help you with each of those jobs, one at a time, and this hub puts them in the order that makes sense for a new or small site.

Where to start: the order that works

If your site is new, or if you have never checked it before, work through the tools in this order. Each step builds on the one before it, and the order saves you from fixing small details while a bigger problem is still hiding underneath.

  1. Meta tags first. Every page needs a title and a short description, because those are the words a search engine shows when your page appears in the results. Use the meta tag generator to build a clean set of tags for each page. A page without a clear title is a page the search engine has to guess about, and guessing never goes well.
  2. Sitemap next. A sitemap is a simple list of every page on your site, written in a format search engines can read. It helps the search engine find pages it might otherwise miss, especially new pages and pages that are not linked from your homepage. Build one with the sitemap generator and keep it up to date as you add pages.
  3. Speed after that. A slow page loses readers before they ever see your words, because most people will not wait for a page that takes too long to load. Use the speed checker to test your connection and work through the 20-point checklist. Images are usually the reason a page is slow, so large photos are the first place to look.
  4. Links next. A link that leads nowhere is a dead end for a reader and a broken signal for a search engine. Use the broken link checker to find the links that fail, then fix them, point them at the right page, or remove them. Do this for every page you care about, not just the homepage.
  5. Audit last. The website audit tool asks twenty plain questions about your site and scores the answers, area by area. It gives you a list of fixes in order of impact, so you know what to do next instead of guessing. Run it after the first four steps, and it will show you what is left.

Work through the five steps once, then come back once a month for a short check. Check a page again whenever you publish it or change it, and keep a short note of what you fixed, so you do not check the same problem twice. SEO is maintenance, not a one-time job, and a small regular habit beats one big burst of effort that never gets repeated.

The tools on this page

There are eight tools here, and each one does a single job. They fall into three groups: planning what to write, building pages that search engines can read, and checking that everything works.

  • Keyword Research Tool. Planning starts here. Type one seed word and the tool turns it into grouped keyword ideas: questions people ask, comparisons people make, how-to phrases, nearby searches and long specific phrases. The ideas help you plan pages that match what people are actually looking for.
  • Meta Tag Generator. Every page needs a title, a description and a set of sharing tags. This tool builds the full set from five short boxes: title, description, page address, image address and site name. It also warns you when a title or description is too long to show in full.
  • Sitemap XML Generator. A sitemap is the map of your site. This tool builds one for you, so search engines can find every page, including new pages and pages that sit deep inside your site.
  • Schema Markup Generator. Schema markup is structured data that tells a search engine what a page is: an article, a product, a recipe, an event. It does not change what visitors see, but it helps a search engine understand the page and show it properly.
  • Website Speed Checker. This tool tests your own connection and this page's load time, and it scores a 20-point speed checklist that you fill in honestly. It does not pretend to measure another website's speed, because a page like this cannot do that, and it says so plainly.
  • Broken Link Checker. This tool lists the links on the page you are viewing and tests the ones it can reach, and it also checks same-site links that you paste in. It reports honestly which links fail, so you can fix them before your readers find them.
  • SERP Preview Tool. Before you publish a page, type its title, address and description into this tool and see how it will look in the search results, on desktop and on a phone. A title that gets cut off is easy to fix before publishing and embarrassing after.
  • Website Audit Tool. This is the full check-up. Twenty plain questions about your site, a scored report by area, and the fixes ordered by impact. Use it after the other tools, when you want to see the whole picture in one place.

You do not need to use all eight tools in one sitting. Pick the job in front of you, use the tool for that job, fix what it finds, and move on. The hub will still be here next month, and the tools will still be free.

How to use this hub

Every tool works in your browser, with no signup and no download. Tap a card to open a tool, type or paste a few words, and read the result. The tools work the same on a phone and on a computer, and the buttons are large enough to tap comfortably.

Work on one page at a time. Open a tool, run it on the page you are working on, and fix what it finds before you move to the next page. If you try to fix a whole site in one afternoon, you will end up with a long list and nothing finished. One page, fully fixed, is worth more than ten pages half checked.

If a tool shows a warning, read it carefully. Warnings on these pages are written in plain words, and they tell you what is wrong and what to do about it. A warning is not an error to fear; it is the tool doing its job. Fix the warning, run the tool again, and check that it is gone.

What tools cannot do for you

A tool can check a title, count characters, test a link and score a checklist. It cannot decide what your site is about, write a helpful page, or choose the readers you want to serve. Those are your jobs, and they are the jobs that matter most. The best-checked page in the world still fails if it does not answer a real question in clear words.

A tool also cannot rank a site by itself. Ranking is the search engine's decision, and it is based on whether your pages answer real searches well, whether people stay and read them, and whether the site works properly. Tools help you check and fix, and fixing problems gives your pages a fair chance, but there is no button that puts a page at the top, and anyone who sells you one is not being honest.

Finally, tools cannot replace time. A new page may take weeks to be found and shown, even when everything is done right, because search engines take time to discover new pages and decide where they belong. Keep publishing helpful pages, keep fixing what the tools find, and check again next month. That steady habit is what SEO actually is: small, honest work, repeated.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Chasing one big keyword. A new site rarely wins the short, popular words, because big sites have held them for years. Specific phrases work better, and the keyword tool will give you plenty of them.
  • Using the same title and description on every page. Each page deserves its own title and its own description, written for that page. Repeating the same words everywhere tells search engines that your pages are all the same.
  • Ignoring speed on a phone. Most readers arrive on a phone, often on a slow connection. A page that feels fast on a computer may feel very slow on a phone, so test where your readers actually are.
  • Never checking links. Pages move, files get deleted, and addresses change. A link that worked last year may be broken today, and the only way to know is to check.
  • Fixing only the easy items. A missing comma is easy to fix and barely matters. A broken page, a page with no title, or a page that takes forever to load matters far more. Fix the big problems first, and let the audit tool order them for you.
  • Expecting results overnight. SEO is slow, honest work. If a page does not appear in the results after a week, that does not mean the work failed. Give it time, keep the pages helpful, and check again.
