How to Write Meeting Notes

Meeting notes are useful only when people can find the decisions and the next steps in them. The best notes are short, clear, and shared quickly, so everyone leaves the meeting knowing who is doing what and by when. This guide shows you how to prepare before the meeting, what to capture during it, and how to clean up and share your notes afterwards.

Before the meeting: set up your notes

Good notes start before anyone sits down. The note-taker should know they have the job before the meeting begins, not halfway through it. If the role rotates, agree on the rotation in advance so nobody is caught by surprise. It also helps to know the agenda ahead of time, because the agenda tells you what decisions the meeting is supposed to reach and which topics will need the most careful notes.

Prepare a template before the meeting starts. A template is simply a blank set of headings you fill in as you go: the date, the people present, the decisions, the action items, and any open questions. Starting from a template saves you from deciding how to organise your notes while people are talking, and it keeps every set of notes from your team looking the same, which makes them easier to find and read later.

Fill in what you already know before the meeting begins. Add the date, the meeting title, the list of people invited, and the agenda items. If the meeting follows up on an earlier one, pull out the action items from last time so you can check them off or carry them forward. A few minutes of preparation like this makes the note-taking itself much calmer, because you are filling in blanks instead of starting from an empty page.

During the meeting: capture decisions, not words

The biggest mistake note-takers make is trying to write down everything that was said. A transcript is not a set of notes. Nobody reads a transcript afterwards, and trying to keep up with every word means you miss the moments that matter, such as the second when a decision is actually made. Your job is to listen for outcomes and write those down.

There are four things worth capturing every time. First, the decisions: what was agreed, and briefly why. Second, the action items: what needs to be done next. Third, the owner of each action item: the one person responsible for it. Fourth, the date it is due. If any of these four is missing, the note is not finished. An action item without an owner is a wish, not a plan, and an action item without a date will drift until someone asks about it again.

Write in short lines, not full sentences. Short lines are faster to write and easier to scan later. Use the speakers own words for decisions when you can, because exact wording avoids arguments later about what was agreed. When a discussion goes around in circles, wait for the conclusion before writing anything down; a single line that says what was decided is worth more than a page of back-and-forth.

Do not be afraid to ask for clarity in the room. If a decision is unclear, say so: a quick question such as "Just to confirm, are we agreeing to this?" helps everyone, not just the note-taker. If an action item is assigned to a whole group, ask who in the group will own it. If no date is mentioned, ask for one. These small interruptions save far more time than they cost, because they prevent the confusion that shows up days later when nobody remembers what was meant.

Mark open questions as you go. Not every topic ends in a decision, and that is fine, but it should be visible in the notes. A line that says a question was raised and left open, with a note of who will look into it, stops the same question from being debated again from scratch at the next meeting.

A simple reusable structure

You do not need a complicated format. The same five headings work for almost any meeting, from a quick team check-in to a long planning session. Use them every time and your notes will write themselves.

  • Meeting details: the date, the meeting name, who attended, and who was invited but could not come.
  • Decisions: a short list of what was agreed, with one line of context for each decision so the reasoning is not lost.
  • Action items: each action on its own line, with the owner and the due date written next to it.
  • Open questions: topics raised but not settled, with a note of who will follow up.
  • Next meeting: the date and the main topic, if one was agreed.

Keep the action items together in one list, even if they were discussed at different points in the meeting. When someone opens the notes a week later, they will look for the actions first, and a single list is far easier to scan than actions scattered through pages of discussion. Some teams put the action list at the very top of the notes, above the decisions, for exactly this reason.

Keep the whole document short. For most meetings, one page is enough. If your notes run longer than that, ask yourself what can be cut: background discussion, repeated points, and topics that were mentioned but went nowhere can usually be reduced to a single line or left out entirely. Short notes get read. Long notes get filed and forgotten.

Example notes

The short example below shows what finished notes look like for a small team meeting. It is an example only, and the names in it are made up.

Example: Weekly planning meeting, Monday 9 November. Present: Ayesha, Rahim, and Sara. Decisions: the team will launch the new sign-up form on 20 November, and the launch announcement will go out the same day. Action items: Ayesha will finish the form text by 13 November; Rahim will test the form on a phone by 16 November; Sara will draft the announcement by 17 November. Open questions: the team still needs to agree on the wording for the welcome message, and Ayesha will bring two options to the next meeting. Next meeting: Monday 16 November.

Notice how short that example is. Every sentence earns its place: the decisions say what was agreed, every action has an owner and a date, and the open question has a named person following it up. Nobody needs to read a transcript to know what happened or what to do next.

After the meeting: clean up and share

Do not send your raw notes. The notes you took during the meeting are a draft, written in a hurry, and they almost always need a short tidy-up before they go to anyone else. The best time to do this is right after the meeting, while the discussion is still fresh in your mind. Ten minutes spent now saves confusion later.

Start by reading through the notes once and fixing anything unclear. Turn half-finished lines into complete thoughts. Check every action item: does it have an owner, and does it have a date? If you wrote down a name you are unsure of, or a date you half-heard, check it now rather than guessing. Remove anything that turned out to be wrong or that the meeting moved past, and make sure the decisions are stated plainly, in the order they were made.

Then check the wording of the decisions. A decision should be readable by someone who was not in the room. If a line only makes sense to people who heard the discussion, add a short phrase of context. For example, instead of writing "Agreed: option two," write "Agreed: option two, the shorter form, because the team felt the longer form asked for too much." That one extra clause is the difference between notes that are useful and notes that raise more questions than they answer.

Share the notes the same day if you can, and within one working day at the latest. Send them to everyone who attended, and to anyone who was invited but could not come, so nobody is left out of the loop. If your team keeps notes in one shared place, file them there too, with a clear title that includes the date, so they can be found months later without a search through old messages.

Finally, follow up on the actions. Notes are only the first half of the job; the second half is making sure the actions actually happen. A short message to the owners a day or two before a deadline is often all it takes. When the next meeting comes around, open with the action list from the previous notes: which actions are done, which are still open, and which need a new date. That habit turns meeting notes from a record of the past into a tool that keeps work moving.

Questions people ask

What should meeting notes include?

Good meeting notes include the date, the people who attended, the decisions made, the action items with an owner and a date for each one, and any open questions left for later. They do not need to record every word that was said. A short summary of the discussion behind each decision is useful, so someone who was not there can understand why the decision was made.

Should notes record everything that was said?

No. Notes that try to record everything end up long, hard to read, and rarely opened again. Your job is to filter: write down decisions, actions, owners, dates, and the key reasons behind a decision. If a point was discussed but nothing was decided, one line saying it was discussed and left open is enough.

Who should write the notes?

One person should be named as the note-taker before the meeting starts, and it should not usually be the person running the meeting. Rotating the role between meetings keeps the work fair and gives everyone practice. If the group is large, the note-taker can ask someone to confirm the action items at the end of the meeting.

How soon should notes be shared after a meeting?

Share notes on the same day if you can, and within one working day at the latest. The longer they wait, the more the details fade and the more likely action items are forgotten. A quick tidy-up right after the meeting is usually enough: fix the wording, check the owners and dates, then send them to everyone who attended and anyone who was invited but could not come.
