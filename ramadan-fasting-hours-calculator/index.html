How the fast length is worked out

The length of a fast is a simple subtraction. The fast begins at dawn, when suhoor ends, and finishes at sunset, when iftar begins, so the length is the gap between those two times on the same day. This calculator takes the two times you enter and subtracts the earlier one from the later one, giving the answer in hours and minutes.

Nothing else goes into the sum. There is no allowance, no adjustment, and no hidden data: the two times you type in are the whole calculation. That is why the answer is exact for your city and your day, as long as the two times came from a timetable you trust.

Why fasts are different lengths in different places

Fasting hours depend on where you live and what time of year Ramadan falls in. Near the equator, the day is about twelve to thirteen hours long all year round, so the fast is much the same length every Ramadan. Farther from the equator, the seasons stretch and shrink the day, and the fast follows.

Ramadan moves through the seasons over the years, because the Islamic calendar is lunar and about eleven days shorter than the solar year. When Ramadan falls in summer in the northern hemisphere, the fasts are long; when it falls in winter, they are short. Ramadan 2027 falls in February and March, so northern fasts are moderate and growing a little longer each day.

Fasts get longer through Ramadan 2027

Ramadan 2027 is expected to run from Monday 8 February to Monday 8 March, with Eid al-Fitr expected on Tuesday 9 March. In the northern hemisphere those weeks sit in late winter turning into early spring, when the days are lengthening. Dawn comes a little earlier and sunset a little later as the month goes on, so each fast is a minute or two longer than the one before.

The change is gentle, but it adds up. Over thirty days, the fast can grow by an hour or more in some places, which is why it is worth checking your timetable every few days rather than relying on the times from the first week. In the southern hemisphere the pattern is reversed, and the fasts shorten slightly as the month passes.

Making a long fast easier

Long fasts are easier with a little planning. Drink plenty of water between iftar and suhoor, because the body can only store so much, and eat a suhoor that will last: something with slow-release energy rather than a sugary snack that fades by mid-morning. A short rest in the afternoon helps too, if your day allows it.

Keep the hardest work for the morning, when your energy is highest, and leave lighter tasks for the last hours before iftar. This is general information only, not medical advice: if fasting affects your health, speak to a doctor, and remember that the rules of fasting make allowances for illness, travel, and other genuine difficulties.

Short fasts and long fasts around the world

The range of fasting hours around the world is wide. In cities near the equator, such as Singapore or Nairobi, the fast is close to thirteen hours whatever the season. In London or New York in a summer Ramadan, it can pass nineteen hours, while in a winter Ramadan it can be under ten. In far northern towns in summer, the sun barely sets at all, and local scholars give special guidance.

That variety is one reason this calculator asks for your own times instead of offering a single answer. A page that said the fast lasts a certain number of hours would be wrong for most of its readers. Your timetable knows your latitude and your season, and the two numbers you copy from it carry all of that information with them.

Checking your times each day

Because dawn and sunset shift a little every day, the fast length shifts with them. A timetable printed for the whole month already has the change built in, so the simplest habit is to look at the line for today, not the line for the first day. If you use a prayer app, it will update the times for you automatically.

It is worth re-running this calculator once a week through the month. Watching the fast grow by a few minutes each time is a small reminder of how the month is passing, and it keeps your planning honest: the suhoor alarm that was right on day one may need to move by the third week.

One more habit worth keeping: write the two times down somewhere visible, such as a note on the fridge, so the whole household can see them. Children in particular like knowing how long is left, and a note on the wall answers the question without anyone having to ask.

Questions people ask

How long is the fast in Ramadan 2027?

It depends on your city and the date, because the fast runs from dawn to sunset and both move through the month. Enter your suhoor end and iftar times from your local timetable and the calculator will show the length for your day.

What counts as the start of the fast?

The fast starts at dawn, the time of the Fajr prayer, which is when suhoor ends. The fast finishes at sunset, the time of the Maghrib prayer, which is when iftar begins.

What if my iftar time is earlier than my suhoor time?

Then one of the two times is wrong, because iftar always comes later in the same day than the end of suhoor. The calculator will ask you to check the times. A common mistake is entering a morning time where an evening time belongs, or the other way round.

Do fasting hours change during Ramadan?

Yes, a little each day. In the northern hemisphere during Ramadan 2027, which falls in February and March, the days are lengthening, so the fast grows by a minute or two a day. In the southern hemisphere it shortens slightly instead.

Why is the fast longer in some countries than others?

It comes down to latitude and season. Near the equator the day is about the same length all year, while farther north or south the seasons stretch summer days and shrink winter ones. Ramadan moves through the seasons over the years, so the same city sees long fasts in some years and short ones in others.

Is this medical advice?

No. Everything on this page is general information about the length of the fast. If you have a health condition, are pregnant, or are unsure whether fasting is safe for you, speak to a doctor and to a scholar you trust.
