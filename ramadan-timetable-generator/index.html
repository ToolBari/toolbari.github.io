How the timetable is built

The generator works from four numbers: your suhoor end and iftar times on day 1, and the number of minutes each one shifts per day. Day 2 is day 1 plus one shift, day 3 is day 1 plus two shifts, and so on through day 30. The fast length for each day is the gap between that day's suhoor end and iftar.

Day 1 of the table is 8 February 2027, the expected start of Ramadan 2027, and day 30 is 9 March 2027. The dates come from the calendar, and everything else comes from your numbers. Change the day-1 times or the shifts and the whole table changes with them, because the table is nothing more than your numbers carried forward.

Why it is an estimate, not real prayer times

Real prayer times do not move in a perfectly straight line. Dawn and sunset shift by slightly different amounts from day to day, and the pattern depends on your latitude and the time of year. A timetable built by adding the same shift every day is a straight-line approximation of a gently curving reality, and it drifts a little further from the truth with each passing day.

That drift is why this page labels the table an estimate in plain language, and why the warn box asks you to check your local prayer timetable every day. Use this table for planning the month, for printing and pinning up, and for seeing how the fast length changes. Use your local timetable for the actual times you fast by.

Choosing your daily shift

The best way to pick a shift is to look at your local timetable for the first few days of Ramadan and see how much the times move. In many places in February and March, suhoor gets earlier by about a minute a day and iftar gets later by about a minute a day, but that is only a rough pattern, not a rule.

If you are unsure, enter 0 for both shifts. A timetable with no shift is still useful: it shows the same times every day, which is a fair picture of the first week, and it is honest about what it does not know. You can always come back and rebuild the table with a shift once you have seen a few real days from your timetable.

Ramadan 2027 at a glance

Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin on Monday 8 February 2027 and to last 29 or 30 days, with Eid al-Fitr expected on Tuesday 9 March 2027. The Islamic calendar is lunar, and each month begins with the sighting of the new moon, so the confirmed dates may move by a day. This table covers thirty days, from 8 February to 9 March.

If Ramadan turns out to be 29 days, then day 30 of this table is Eid al-Fitr, the day of celebration, and there is no fast on it. That is normal: a 30-day table simply covers both possibilities, and the moon decides which one the year brings.

A worked example

Suppose your day-1 times are suhoor ending at 5:00 in the morning and iftar at 6:30 in the evening, with suhoor shifting one minute earlier each day and iftar one minute later. Day 2 of the table shows suhoor at 4:59 AM and iftar at 6:31 PM, and day 30 shows suhoor at 4:31 AM and iftar at 6:59 PM.

The arithmetic is easy to check. Day 30 is 29 shifts after day 1, so suhoor moves 29 minutes earlier, from 5:00 to 4:31, and iftar moves 29 minutes later, from 6:30 PM to 6:59 PM. The fast on day 1 is 13 hours 30 minutes, and by day 30 it has grown to 14 hours 28 minutes, which is the table's way of showing the lengthening days.

Using the timetable through the month

Print the table and keep it somewhere you will see it each morning, such as the kitchen wall or the fridge door. Each day, read the line for that day, then check it against your local timetable before you set your suhoor alarm or plan the evening. If the two disagree by a minute or two, trust the local timetable.

The table is also useful for planning ahead. If you are hosting an iftar in the third week, the table tells you roughly when sunset will fall, so you can invite people for the right time. And if you are travelling during the month, remember that the table belongs to one city: a new city needs new day-1 times.

Families often find the timetable useful for children too. Older children who are trying their first fasts can follow the table day by day, and seeing the finish line move a little later each evening helps them understand why the month feels different in its last week than in its first.

When the month is 29 days

Ramadan lasts 29 days in some years and 30 in others, and nobody knows which until the moon is sighted near the end of the month. If the new moon is seen on the evening of the 29th fast, the next day is Eid al-Fitr. If it is not seen, there is a 30th fast, and Eid follows the day after.

This generator always builds thirty days, because that covers both cases. If the month ends after 29 days, simply ignore the last row. The important dates stay the same either way: Ramadan is expected to begin on 8 February 2027, and Eid al-Fitr is expected on 9 March 2027, both depending on the sighting of the moon.

Questions people ask

Is this timetable real prayer times?

No. It is an estimate built from the day-1 times you enter, carried forward by the daily shift you choose. Real prayer times move by slightly different amounts each day, so always check your local prayer timetable for the times you actually fast by.

Why does the table have 30 days?

Ramadan lasts 29 or 30 days, and the table covers the longer case. If the month turns out to be 29 days, day 30 of the table is Eid al-Fitr and there is no fast on it. The moon decides which it is.

What daily shift should I use?

Look at your local timetable for the first few days of Ramadan and see how much the times move. A shift of one minute a day is common in many places, but it varies. If you are unsure, use 0, which keeps the same times every day.

What if my day-1 times are wrong?

The whole table shifts with them, because every row is built from day 1. Re-check your day-1 suhoor end and iftar times against your local timetable, enter the corrected times, and build the table again.

When does Ramadan 2027 start and end?

Ramadan 2027 is expected to begin on Monday 8 February 2027, and Eid al-Fitr is expected on Tuesday 9 March 2027. Both dates depend on the sighting of the new moon, so they may move by a day.

Can I print the timetable?

Yes. Build the table, then use your browser's print function. The table is plain and compact, so it fits on a page, and you can pin it up for the month.
