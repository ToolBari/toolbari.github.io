Quality vs size: the trade-off

Every compressed image is a trade. Lower quality means a smaller file; higher quality means more detail survives. The trick is that the trade is not even: the first bit of compression buys you the biggest saving. Dropping a phone photo from its original quality to around 80% often cuts the file to a fraction of its size, while the picture on screen looks almost exactly the same. Push it much lower, though, and the damage shows — soft edges and blotchy patches where smooth colour used to be.

That is why a moderate setting beats both extremes. Maximum quality keeps every pixel but leaves you with a file too big to upload; minimum quality fits anywhere but looks rough. Somewhere in the middle, the file is small enough for its job and the photo still looks like itself. A picture destined for a form can be far smaller than one you plan to print, so start moderate and adjust from there.

The 80% setting: a good starting point

If you only remember one number, make it 80%. At around 80% quality, a JPG keeps the detail your eye actually notices — faces stay sharp, text stays readable, colours stay true — while the file shrinks to a size that uploads quickly and fits most forms. It is the setting to reach for first when you are not sure what a form wants.

Move away from 80% only when you have a reason. If the file still will not fit, step down gradually — 70%, then 60% — checking the preview each time, rather than jumping straight to the lowest setting. If the photo is one you will print, step up instead — or keep the original untouched and compress a copy.

JPG, PNG, and WebP: which format?

JPG is the workhorse for photos. It compresses well, opens everywhere, and is what phone cameras produce, which is why it is the usual choice for uploads and forms. PNG suits a different job: screenshots, logos, and images with sharp lines or text, where JPG's smoothing would blur the edges. For a camera picture, though, JPG is normally the better pick.

WebP is a newer format that can beat JPG on size for the same quality, and most modern browsers open it. But not every form accepts it, so for official uploads, JPG remains the safer choice. If your image is in a format a form will not take, convert it first — the Image Converter turns images between formats — then compress the result.

Where file size actually matters

The most common reason to compress is a form. Job applications, exam registrations, and government portals often cap uploads at a size that a fresh phone photo easily exceeds, and a file over the limit is rejected outright — sometimes without a clear error message. Compressing first turns a failed upload into a successful one — and if the form also asks for specific dimensions, our guide to resizing a photo for a passport or online form explains how to get both right.

Size also matters in email and on web pages. A message with several full-size photos can bounce against an attachment limit, and a page heavy with uncompressed images loads slowly on a slow connection. Compressing before you send or publish costs a minute and saves everyone else's time. The same applies to documents: photos placed into a PDF keep their full size, so compressing first is the easiest way to keep the finished file light — see our guide to converting an image to PDF.

More questions, answered

Can I compress the same photo twice?

You can, but each round throws away a little more detail, so compressing an already-compressed photo gives a worse result than compressing the original once. Always start from the best copy you have.

Does compressing change the photo's dimensions?

No. Compression changes the file size, not the width and height. If you also need the photo smaller in dimensions, resize it as a separate step, then compress.
