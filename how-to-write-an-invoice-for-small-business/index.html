How to Write an Invoice for a Small Business

An invoice is a written request for payment that lists what you sold, how much it costs, and when the money is due. This guide shows what every invoice must include, how to number invoices, how to write payment terms in plain words, and the mistakes that delay payment.

A good invoice does two jobs at once. It tells your customer exactly what they owe and why, and it gives you a clear record you can follow up on if payment is late. Invoices do not need fancy wording or complicated layouts. What they need is completeness: every detail your customer needs to pay you without having to ask a question first. This guide walks through each part of an invoice in order, then shows a worked example you can check by hand, and finishes with the mistakes that most often slow payment down.

This guide is general information only — it is not legal or tax advice. Rules about invoices, tax and record-keeping differ from place to place, so check the rules that apply where you do business.

What every invoice must show

Every invoice needs the same basic details, whether you sell a service or a product. Start with your own details: your business name and how to reach you. Then add your customer's name and address, so there is no doubt who the invoice is for. Next comes the date you issued the invoice and the invoice number, which you will read about below. The heart of the invoice is the list of items: each thing you sold or each block of time you worked, with a quantity, a unit amount, and the line total. Below the items, show the subtotal, any discount, and the total amount due. Finally, state the payment terms: when payment is due and how the customer should pay. A small note at the bottom is also useful — a thank-you line, or a reminder of the due date — but keep it short. The customer should be able to read the invoice once and know exactly what they owe, what it is for, and when to pay it.

  • Your business name and contact details
  • Your customer's name and address
  • The invoice date and invoice number
  • Each item or service, with quantity, unit amount and line total
  • Subtotal, any discount, and the total amount due
  • Payment terms: the due date and how to pay

How invoice numbering works

Give every invoice a number, and never reuse one. Numbers let you and your customer refer to a specific invoice without confusion, and they make your records easy to check. The simplest system is to start at 1 and count up: 1, 2, 3, and so on. Many small businesses add a prefix so the numbers are easier to read at a glance, such as INV-001, INV-002, INV-003. If you want the year in the number, a format like 2026-001 works well, and you can restart the count each year if you like. Whatever system you choose, keep it in order and keep it unbroken: a missing number looks like a missing invoice, and a repeated number causes confusion. If you make a mistake on an invoice, do not delete it and reuse its number. Issue a corrected invoice with a new number, and keep the old one marked as cancelled. That way your records always tell a true story, from the first invoice to the latest.

Payment terms in plain words

Payment terms are simply the rules you set for when and how you get paid. The most common term is a number of days after the invoice date: due in 14 days or due in 30 days are both normal. Shorter terms mean you get paid sooner, but they can feel strict to a new customer; longer terms are friendlier but leave you waiting. Pick one term and use it on every invoice so customers learn what to expect. State the due date as a calendar date as well as a number of days, so nobody has to count. Write it plainly: Payment due by 15 December 2026 leaves no room for doubt. Also say how the customer should pay — bank transfer, cash, card, or whatever you accept — and include the details they need to pay you. If you charge a late fee, say so on the invoice before the work starts, not after payment is already late. Clear terms agreed in advance prevent most payment arguments.

A worked example

Here is a small invoice worked out step by step. The amounts are in your own currency, and the arithmetic is the same whatever currency you use. A designer bills a customer for two things. First, design work: 4 hours at 25 per hour, which comes to 100. Second, printing: 50 flyers at 0.50 each, which comes to 25. Add the two lines together for the subtotal: 100 plus 25 equals 125. The designer gives a discount of 5, so the total is 125 minus 5, which equals 120. That is the amount the customer owes. On the invoice itself, each line would appear as its own row, with the quantity, the unit amount and the line total, followed by the subtotal, the discount and the total at the bottom. Check your own invoices the same way: multiply each line, add the lines, subtract any discount, and read the total twice before you send it. A small arithmetic error is one of the easiest mistakes to make and one of the easiest to avoid.

Mistakes that delay payment

The most common reason an invoice is paid late is that the customer cannot tell what to pay or how. A missing total, a due date that says only due soon, or payment details left off the invoice all cause the same problem: the customer has to ask you a question before they can pay, and the payment waits while the question goes back and forth. Sending the invoice late is another frequent mistake. Send it as soon as the work is done, while the job is still fresh in the customer's mind — an invoice sent a month later is far more likely to be questioned or forgotten. Vague item descriptions cause delays too: services rendered tells the customer nothing, while 4 hours of design work is clear and easy to approve. Finally, keep a copy of every invoice you send, and note the date you sent it. If a payment is late, a polite reminder that names the invoice number, the date and the amount is much more effective than a vague message. Follow up once, a few days after the due date, and keep a record of every reminder you send.

Questions people ask

What must an invoice include?

Your business name and contact details, your customer's name and address, the invoice date and number, a list of items or services with quantities and amounts, the subtotal, any discount, the total due, and the payment terms. With those details on the page, your customer can pay without having to ask you anything first.

How should I number invoices?

Start at 1 and count up, in order, without skipping or repeating a number. A prefix such as INV-001 or a year such as 2026-001 is fine if it helps you read the numbers at a glance. If you make a mistake, issue a corrected invoice with a new number and mark the old one as cancelled.

What payment terms should I write?

State a due date as a calendar date, such as 'Payment due by 15 December 2026', and say how the customer should pay. Terms of 14 or 30 days after the invoice date are both common — pick one and use it on every invoice so customers know what to expect.

What mistakes delay payment?

The most common ones are sending the invoice late, leaving off the total or the payment details, writing a vague due date, and describing the work so briefly that the customer cannot tell what they are paying for. Send the invoice promptly, describe each item clearly, and state the amount, the due date and how to pay.
