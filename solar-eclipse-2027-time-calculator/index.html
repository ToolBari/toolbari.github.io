What happens on 2 August 2027

On Monday 2 August 2027, the Moon passes directly between the Earth and the Sun, and for a few minutes the daytime sky goes dark along a narrow band of the Earth's surface. That band, the path of totality, is about 258 kilometres wide, and it runs from southern Spain across the Strait of Gibraltar, through Morocco, Algeria, Tunisia and Libya, across Egypt, and on through Saudi Arabia and Yemen to north-eastern Somalia.

Inside the path, the Sun is completely covered by the Moon, and for the minutes of totality you can see the Sun's outer atmosphere, the corona, glowing around the dark disc. Outside the path, the eclipse is only partial: the Moon covers part of the Sun, the sky stays light, and the Sun is never fully hidden. That is why the same eclipse is a completely different event depending on where you stand.

This eclipse is a long one. The longest totality on land lasts about 6 minutes 23 seconds, in the Egyptian desert near Luxor, and it is the longest total solar eclipse visible from land until 2114. Eclipses this long are rare, which is why so many people are planning to be inside the path on the day.

The Luxor window

Luxor, in southern Egypt, is the one city in our table with a published minute-by-minute window. Totality there lasts about 6 minutes 20 seconds, from about 1:02 to about 1:08 in the afternoon, local time. The Sun is about 82 degrees high in the sky at that moment, almost directly overhead, which makes for a very different eclipse from one seen low over the horizon.

Those figures are the published ones for Luxor, and they are the only specific timings on this page. The calculator shows them in full when you pick Luxor, and it shows them nowhere else, because nowhere else in our table has a published window to show. That is a deliberate choice: a timing that looks exact but was made up would be worse than no timing at all.

Luxor in August is also extremely hot. The midday heat can reach about 43 degrees, so anyone planning to watch there needs shade, water and a plan for the hours before and after totality, not just for the six minutes of darkness. Egypt has the clearest skies in August along the path, which is part of why Luxor is the best-known viewing spot for this eclipse.

Why most cities have no exact time here

You may notice that the calculator gives a precise window for Luxor and only a general answer for everywhere else. That is not an accident. Our table holds published timings for Luxor only, and for the other cities in the path it holds the honest fact that they are inside the path and that totality lasts several minutes there.

Exact eclipse timings depend on your precise position — not just your city, but your street and your distance from the centre of the path. A timing worked out for one spot in a city can be wrong by a minute or more a few kilometres away. A local eclipse guide, or an eclipse app that uses your exact location, will give you the right window for where you actually stand.

So use this calculator for what it is good at: finding out whether a city is inside the path, whether it sees totality or only a partial eclipse, and how long is left until the day. Then, for the exact minutes, check a local guide for the place you will actually be.

Inside the path, or only partial?

Seven cities in our table are inside the path of totality: Cadiz in Spain, Tangier in Morocco, Tunis in Tunisia, Luxor and Sohag in Egypt, Jeddah in Saudi Arabia, and Sana'a in Yemen. From any of them, the Sun is completely covered for several minutes, and the sky goes dark in the middle of the day.

Two cities in our table are not in the path, and the calculator says so plainly. Aswan, in southern Egypt, sees a deep partial eclipse — a large part of the Sun is covered, but the Sun is never fully hidden, and the sky does not go dark. Cairo sees a partial eclipse only, for the same reason. Both are worth watching, but neither is totality.

The difference between a deep partial eclipse and totality is bigger than it sounds. Only inside the path does the Sun disappear completely and the sky go dark. Even a small uncovered sliver of Sun is bright enough to keep the day looking like day, which is why being inside the path matters so much for this eclipse.

Planning a trip to the path

If you are travelling to see this eclipse, the first decision is where inside the path to stand. Egypt has the clearest skies in August, which is why Luxor and Sohag are popular choices, but the heat is serious: Luxor at midday in August can reach about 43 degrees. Spain and Morocco are cooler but cloudier, and every place along the path has its own trade-off.

The second decision is when to arrive. The eclipse happens in the early afternoon in Egypt, so you will want to be in place well before midday, with shade, water and your viewing glasses ready. Arriving the day before is the safe choice, because roads near popular viewing spots will be busy on the morning of the eclipse.

The third decision is what to do after totality. The partial eclipse continues for a long while after the Sun reappears, and the light changes slowly back to normal. Many people stay to watch the whole thing, and the hours after totality are a good time to rest in the shade before travelling on.

Staying safe

Looking at the Sun is dangerous, and an eclipse does not change that. The only safe way to look directly at the Sun is through eclipse glasses that meet the ISO 12312-2 standard. Ordinary sunglasses are never safe, however dark they seem, and neither are smoked glass, camera film or homemade filters.

A pinhole viewer is a safe indirect way to watch. It projects an image of the Sun onto a surface, so you watch the projection instead of the Sun itself, and it is easy to make from a piece of card with a small hole in it. It is a good option for children and for groups.

Everything on this page about safety is general information only, not medical advice. If you are unsure about your glasses or your eyes, ask a local expert before the day. The eclipse will be spectacular, and it is worth watching safely.
